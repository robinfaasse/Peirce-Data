It cut the real helix at the same points as the other helix and at no others.

There are thus two different helices whose intersections with the real helix represent all the fractions of any given denominator. Of these two we shall always employ the one with the loosest curb, that is, the helix whose equation is
$$l = \frac{1}{I-N} (\theta + B)$$
where $N$ is the denominator of the fractions represented by its intersections with the real helix. [ins]The equation of the other helix will be $l = \frac{1}{I+N} (\theta + C)$.[/ins] (Since we are now considering only fractional values, we may assume $N > \frac{1}{2} I$; for [del]all[/del] fractions of [del]less[/del] [ins]lower[/ins] denominators have no values which do not belong to fractions of [del]greater[/del] [ins]higher[/ins] denominators which still do not surpass $I$. Hence, $I - N < \frac{1}{2} I$; so that there will never be two revolutions of the real helix between successive intersections.
