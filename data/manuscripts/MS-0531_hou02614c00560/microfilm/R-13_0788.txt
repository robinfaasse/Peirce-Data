Comprehensive combination is defined by three similar formulae, to
wit,
([u]Principle 6[/u]) Whatever is both A and B [u]is[/u] A.
([u]Principle 7[/u]) Whatever is both A and B [u]is[/u] B.
([u]Principle 8[/u]) If A [u]is[/u] B, and A [u]is[/u] C, then A [u]is[/u] both B and C.
In other words either of the constituents of a comprehensive combination
may be predicated of it, and it is itself predicable of whatever both
its constituents are predicable of. From Principles 1 and 8 we have
([u]Theorem 2[/u]) A [u]is[/u] both A and A.

Principles 1, 5, and 8, being in the form of hypothetical propositions
with two independent antecedents, may each be made the principle
of a form of syllogism. Thus,

M [u]is[/u] P
S [u]is[/u] M
$\therefore$ S [u]is[/u] P

A [u]is[/u] C
B [u]is[/u] C
$\therefore$ Whatever is either A or B [u]is[/u] C.

A [u]is[/u] B
A [u]is[/u] C
$\therefore$ A [u]is[/u] both B and C.

Of these the first is the only one usually recognized or perhaps
worth much consideration[ins] because the middle term does not disappear from the others[/ins]. Yet it is curious to notice this new
form of trichotomy in syllogism.
