[pn]23[/pn] / §2.4.2. [u]Terminology[/u]. [u]Multitude[/u] is the obvious word for what Cantor calls [u]Mächtigkeit[/u] (puissance, potenza). What the devil is the sense of taking such inexpressive terms when there is so familiar a one? As for "cardinal number," I cannot assent [del]shall[/del] to his use of that term. The cardinal numbers are vocables used in the performance of that experiment called counting. It is quite a secondary use of them as adjectives of multitude. We have occasion in this study to speak of cardinal numbers proper & the word is needed in its proper and familiar sense, while it is a pure superfluity in the sense of multitude. As for his balance between [u]ordinal number[/u] (to his use of which I fully assent, although I here for the sake of brevity call the [del]g[/del] integer numbers ordinals, in which perhaps I do wrong) — but as to that balance, I doubt its justice. Multitude appears to me to be a much more special conception than an ordinal. I propose to call his [u]wohlgeordnet[/u] series a [u]Cantorian series[/u], and perhaps I should prefer to call his ordinal numbers [u]Cantorials[/u]. His [u]Menge[/u] (ensemble, insieme) is just what the common word [u]collection[/u] means, this word not at all implying that the objects are brought together otherwise than in thought. The whole doctrine needs a name. Names of sciences are usually formed from the Greek; but that language is peculiar in not having any word for [del]many[/del] [ins]how[/ins] many or how many, etc in contradistinction to [del]French[/del] [ins]how much[/ins]. The plural [u]πολλά[/u] is used, and the ordinal word [u]πόστος[/u].

[u]Πλῆθος[/u] means a throng, but also magnitude, and more commonly this in mathematics. Not caring for the quality of our Greek, one might call the doctrine [del]and[/del] [u]pollotetics[/u], but I doubt its being understood at sight. Since Latin has [u]quot[/u], I am tempted to imitate sutrina and call the doctrine [u]quotitrine[/u] or [u]quotitrina[/u].
