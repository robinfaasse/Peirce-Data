But the whole subject is in an unsatisfactory, if not insecure, state until the mathematical definition has been set forth. I will begin then, by endeavoring to make a definition which shall accurately answer to the confused idea we all have of a collection, Cantor and all of us. We may at once set down as item No 1,

<u>Item No 1</u>. A collection is an individual object, whose existence consists in the [del]independent[/del] [ins]existence[/ins] of [del]certain[/del] other individual objects called its members.

But in order to state what these individuals are (and it must be supposed that any object we deal with in mathematics is capable of definite statement, whether we are capable of making such statement or not) we have to describe them, this description being applicable to each of them and to nothing else. Therefore [del]all[/del] the members of every collection have some describable character common to them [pn]page 2[/pn] and peculiar to them. Suppose then we reform Item No 1 as follows:

<u>Item No 1'</u>. A collection is an object having individual identity. For [del]any[/del] [ins]each[/ins] collection there is, at least, one character such that the collection would not exist if any individual that actually has that character had not that character; but the collection always [del]would exist[/del] [ins]in the present state of things[/ins] whenever every object actually having that character should have that character. [del]These[/del] [ins]Each of these[/ins] individuals is a member of the collection and nothing else is so.

Supposing two descriptions, 'being m' and 'being n', there is a collection of whatever is m and a collection of whatever is n. Now, if [del]every[/del] [ins]whatever is[/ins] m is [del]an[/del] n, and [del]every[/del] [ins]whatever is[/ins] n is [del]an[/del] m, then those two collections are [ins]one[/ins] identical collection. If, however, whatever is m is n but some n is not m then the collection of m's is said to be a part of the collection of n's.
