My dear Professor Moore:

I wish you the most successful new year you have had so far.

I dare say you may have written a second time about my argument. I am curious to see how it struck you after my second statement. But so far I have heard nothing. I write now to communicate another proof. But first I will explain my translations of Cantor's terms.

A Menge, I call a collection. For greater precision, where necessary,

[pn]page 4[/pn]
such a collection, which Cantor calls abzählbar, I call denumeral, and these adjectives enumerable etc are equally applicable to collections & multitudes.

Collections greater than denumeral collections, I would call ultranumeral.

In particular I call the multitude of all possible sums of [del]each[/del] [ins]whose mem-[/ins]bers are members of a denumeral collection, the first abnumeral multitude. The multitude of all possible collections of members of a collection of the first abnumeral multitude, I call the second abnumeral multitude, and so on. When it is proved (if it ever is) that there are no other ultranumerable [del]collections[/del] [ins]multitudes[/ins] than the denumeral series of abnumeral multitudes, the word "abnumeral" will lose its usefulness.
