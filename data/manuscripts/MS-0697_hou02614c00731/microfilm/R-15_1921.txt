[pn]Ms 697[/pn] [u]Lessons in Practical Logic[/u]

[u]Lesson 1.[/u]

The object of this course is to teach something of the art of investigating the truth.

It is really a question of little consequence whether this is a proper definition of Logic or not. That is a mere question of words; but men who have not thoroughly studied Logic are so apt to confound questions of words and questions of fact, [del]calling[/del] both considering verbal discussions as real discussions [del]and still more real discussions as merely verbal,[/del] — that I shall do well to [del]say[/del] [ins]say[/ins] a few words in defence of the name that I have given to this course of lessons. And besides, I am perhaps bound to show that the subject, which the instruction is really to relate to, is the same as [del]that[/del] advertised.

Now if you examine Hamilton's Logic or any of these Logics which are the immediate product of [del]pure[/del] Kantianism as his was ([del]— not his[/del] [ins]peculiar[/ins] [del]system[/del] but his lectures in which his system does not appear as it was worked up later) you will find Logic defined as the Science of [del]Pure[/del] Thought as Thought — or something of that sort. This is [ins]an[/ins] extremely different conception of the subject from that with which I set out. Take for example Mr. Mansell's admirable Prolegomena Logica where the Kantian conception of Logic is developed in the most consistent and beautiful manner.
