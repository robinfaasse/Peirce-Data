POLE-STAR. The second magnitude star near the north pole of the heavens.

POLARIS. The pole-star, which see.

POLLUX. An orange star of magnitude 1.2 in the head of the following twin, beta Geminorum.

PONIATOWSKI'S Bull. The [ins]obsolete[/ins] constellation Taurus Ponitovii, which see.

PRAESEPE [L. Tr. of Gr. phatne, manger, crib] A loose cluster of stars, appearing as a nebula to the naked eye, in the breast of the crab, epsilon Cancri.

PROCYON [Gr. prokyon, for pro tou kynos, before the dog, because it made its first appearance in the morning twilight each year a little earlier than the dog.] An ancient southern constellation re-presenting a little dog. Though south of the zodiac, it lies a little north of the equinoctial, 20 degrees north and a little east of Sirius. It contains the very white 0.6 magnitude star of the same name, the eighth brightest in the heavens, which with Sirius and Betelgeuze forms a magnificent triangle.

PYXIS NAUTICA. A southern constellation introduced by Lacaille, representing a ship's compass or binnacle. It seems to be no longer in use.

PSALTERIUM. Another name for the obsolete constellation Harpa Georgii, which see.

PUPPIS. A subdivision of the constellation Argo, introduced by Baily, in the British Association Catalogue.

PHECDA. A very white star of magnitude 2.6 on the left thigh of the great bear, the southernmost star of the quadrilateral, at the bottom of the pan of the dipper near the handle, gamma Ursae majoris. [Ar. [del]Pekha[/del] [ins]faedh[/ins]-al-dubb-al-akbar, the thigh of the great bear.]

PHERKAD [Ar. El-ferkad, the calf] A name applied to two stars, viz., Pherkad major, a very yellow star of magnitude 2.1, and pherkad minor, a very white star of magnitude 3.2, both in the side of the little bear. They are the two stars of the little dipper [ins]of quadrilateral[/ins] the furthest from the pole; the brighter is called beta the fainter gamma Ursae minoris.

PHURUD. [Ar. El-furûd, the single ones, applied to a group of stars in Canis major and Columbus, probably a corruption of el-kurûd, the monkeys] a yellowish star of magnitude 3.0 on the end of the right foot of the dog, zeta Canis majoris.

PARTHENOPE The 11th planetoid, discovered by De Gasparis at Naples in 1850.

PSYCHE. The 15th planetoid, discovered by De Gasparis at Naples in 1852.

PHOCEA The 25th planetoid discovered by Chacornac at Marseilles in 1853.

PROSERPINA. The 26th planetoid discovered by Luther at Bilk in 1853.

POMONA. The 32nd planetoid, discovered by Goldschmidt in Paris in 1854.
