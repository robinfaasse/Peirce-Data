[ins][Gr. [u]parabola[/u], superposition, a name bestowed upon the curve by Apollonius of Perga in the last quarter of the third century before Christ. See the explanation below.][/ins]
1. A curve of which the historical definition is that it is the intersection of a cone with a plane parallel with its side. [del]The name is derived from the following property. Let the figure represent the cone. Let ABG be the triangle through the axis of the cone; let DE be a line perpendicular to this triangle, cutting BG in H. Let the cone be cut by a plane through DE parallel to AB, so that the intersection with the cone will be the curve called the parabola. Let Z be the point where this curve cuts AB. Then the line ZH is called the diameter of the parabola, or the principal diameter, or the diameter from generation; it is now called the axis. From Z draw ZO at right angles to ZH and of such a length as to make ZO:ZA::BG[sup]2[/sup]:AB·AG. This line ZO is called the latus rectum [ins]by Apollonius[/ins] [del]or thial[/del]; it is now also called the parameter. Now take any point whatever, as K, on the curve. From it draw KL parallel to DE meeting the diameter in L. [ins]If now on ZL as a base[/ins] we erect a rectangle equal in area to the square on KL the other side of this rectangle may be precisely superposed upon the latus rectum ZO. If a similar construction were made in the case of the ellipse, the side of the rectangle would fall short [elleipein] of the latus rectum, in the case of the hyperbola would surpass [hyperballein] it.[/del] The modern scientific definition of the parabola is that it is that plane curve of the second order which is tangent to the line at infinity. The parabola is often also frequently defined as the curve which is everywhere equally distant from a fixed point, called its focus, and from a fixed line called its directrix. The normal to a parabola [del]everywhere[/del] [ins]at every point on the curve[/ins] bisects the angle between the ordinate and the line to the focus.
