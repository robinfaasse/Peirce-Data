[u]Theory of parallels[/u], the geometrical discussion of the number of lines which can be drawn [del]through a given point parallel to a given line[/del] [ins]in a plane through a given point so as not to intersect a given line[/ins], with other kindred matters. The fifth postulate of Euclid reads, "And if a right line incident upon two right lines make the two interior angles on the same side [del]less[/del] less in sum than two right angles, then those two right lines will meet on the side on which the angles are less than two right angles if produced to infinity." This proposition being much more complicated than any other assumed by Euclid without proof, a great number of attempts were made by mathematicians to [del]prove[/del] [ins]demonstrate[/ins] it. Finally, it was conclusively shown, as Gauss expressed it, that we have no reason to believe that the celebrated postulate is more than approximately true. We are thus thrown into a state of doubt between three possible systems of geometry, the Euclidean and two non-Euclidean systems, according as we assume that there can be drawn through any given point parallel to any given line two real lines, only one line, or two imaginary lines. But while there is no reason to think that the Euclidean geometry is exactly true, it has been shown to be so precise that we can hardly hope to ascertain by direct measurements made on this earth on which side it errs before the human race becomes extinct; although indirect evidence connected with some astronomical hypothesis may turn up any day.
