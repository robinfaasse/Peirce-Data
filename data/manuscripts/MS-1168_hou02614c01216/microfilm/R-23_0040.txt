PARABOLOID, n. A surface of the second order having some of its sections parabolas. Paraboloid of
revolution, a surface of solid generated by the revolution of a parabola about its axis.

PARALLEL, a. For the definition put:
1. In geom., intersecting at infinity. Euclid defines parallel straight lines as lines which
lie in the same plane and yet will [del]not[/del] [ins]never[/ins] meet however far they may be produced; and parallel planes
as planes which will never meet, however far they may be produced. The modern definition has the
disadvantage of requiring further explanation, indeed almost an education, to make it intelligi-
ble; but it has three logical advantages, two trifling, the third of immense importance. These
are, 1st, that it is not like the Euclidean definition a negative one; 2nd, that it makes one
conception applicable equally to parallel lines and parallel planes; 3rd, that it is a statement
which, whether literally true or not, must be admitted in form for the sake of the important gene-
ralizations which [del]1886899689[/del] result from it. If an observer could stand upon an infinitely extend-
ed plane upon which numerous straight lines had been drawn, he would see every pair of lines as
meeting, the non-parallel ones as crossing, and the parallels as meeting on that line which would
naturally be called the horizon, although it would really consist of all the parts of the plane at
an infinite distance from him, the geometer's "line at infinity." The observer could not embrace
the whole plane at one view, he could not see out of the back of his head, and therefore he could
not see what happened to two parallels after they had met at the line at infinity. But, except for
what his person hides, every point of the infinite plane on which he is standing appears just as
it would if the light from each point shone only in the direction of his eye. Let the observer
stand aside, then, and imagine that where his eye was there is placed a point which excercises
such an attraction for light that the rays from every point of the plane shine in no direction ex-
cept directly towards that point, and having reached it pass through it in the same directions un-
disturbed. Now imagine a pane of ground glass held up in the neighborhood of that point, and a
picture of the plane would be projected upon [ins]it[/ins] such as a man would see if he could see out of the
back of his head. On such a picture, there is no doubt that all lines meeting on the line at in-
finity would be seen to cross it. In fact there would cease to be anything [del]t888888888888[/del] [ins]to[/ins]
mark the line at infinity, or distinguish it from any other line. Again, there might be lines
which would appear as parallel on the projection; and [del]those lines would undoubtedly cross[/del] [ins]the lines represented by them would undoubtedly cross[/ins]. Thus
it is seen that if we only admit that parallel lines cross at infinity, everything is reconciled
and we can say that the projection represents every intersection [ins]and only an intersection[/ins] by [del]a[/del] [ins]an[/ins] intersection. It might be
objected that parallels are everywhere equidistant, and therefore have a finite distance from one
another at infinity. This is true, but it only shows, what is otherwise known, that the distance
between two coincident points at infinity is indeterminate. Similar considerations might be inde-
finitely multiplied, and go to show the First, that if there be any error in saying that parallel
lines and planes intersect at infinity, it is an error only with regard to what can never become
an object of experience, and second, that the assumption of the formula enables us to generalize
and comprehend the truths of geometry in a remarkable degree. But the full force of such reason-
ing can only be appreciated by a mind trained in modern mathematics.
