The fifth[ins]<sup>*</sup>[/ins] and last definition that need be mentioned was proposed by Benjamin Peirce (a son of whom the present writer had the good fortune of being.) It is, "Mathematics is the Science which draws necessary conclusions." Besides those who have echoed this statement, others (as George Chrystal in the ninth edition of the Encyclopaedia Britannica) have supported it by substantial reproductions of it with such divergencies in accidental forms of conception as manifest the work of independent logical analysis. Since it is impossible to draw necessary conclusions except from perfect knowledge, and [del]any[/del] perfect knowledge of a reality is out of the question, it follows that such conclusions can be drawn from hypotheses or mental assumptions alone; and thus the only substantial difference between Benjamin Peirce's definition and that adopted [del]in[/del] [ins]in[/ins] the present memoir is that the former [del]does[/del] excludes from the work of mathematics the framing of mathematical hypotheses.

The reason the definition is here thus modified is as follows. A scientific definition of a name already in use should be so framed that, while including in the main under the breadth of the name all that which is usually included and excluding from it that which is usually excluded, the its precise delimitation shall make [del]the word as useful in science as possible.[/del] [ins]to those men who know most about the objects it denotes.[/ins] For example, a science ought not to be defined, as it often is, as a systematized collection of ascertained truths; because it is more useful to those who know it best that it should refer to the scientific activities of its promoters. Its essence ought therefore to be made to lie in the investigations and their enlightened methods [del]and not in the results and their truth. Moreover,[/ins] every inquiry which can be properly performed only by those who are engaged upon the main branch of inquiry should be included under the science.
