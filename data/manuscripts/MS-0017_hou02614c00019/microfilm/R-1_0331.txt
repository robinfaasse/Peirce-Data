[u]3[sup]rd[/sup][/u], the main characteristic of time is that the two directions in it,
forward and backward, are unlike. Yet algebra takes no account of this;
and it may be doubted whether it could do so. So causality is much
more dependent upon time than is number; and yet there is no mathe-
matical theory of causality.

For these and other reasons, the definition is untenable and errs
very widely. Nevertheless, it shows some remarkable approaches
to the truth. It is true, for [del]example[/del] [ins]instance[/ins], that, using the word "dia-
gram" in a wide sense, so as to include algebraical formulae [del]as[/del] [ins]among[/ins]
diagrams, [del]even[/del] all mathematical reasoning refers to diagrams; and
these diagrams are spatially extended images in which distinctions
of color, illumination, etc. are immaterial, so that only their pure
spatial relations are attended to. At the same time they are often
made to undergo gradual deformations in time. It is further true
that mathematical reasoning requires objects to be distinguished
without being unlike; and such distinctions can only be effected
by means of the intuitions of time and space. It is therefore
a fact that mathematics does make peculiar use of time and
space and introduces into its images little but the "pure" images
of time and space.
