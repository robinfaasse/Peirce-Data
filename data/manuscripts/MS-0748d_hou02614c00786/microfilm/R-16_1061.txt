We may refer to the modus ponens all forms of inference
like the following:

Whenever A is, B is.
Now A is,
Hence, now B is.

Wherever A is B is.
Here is A,
Hence, here is B.

Whatever A is B is.
This is A,
Hence, this is B.

The second premises of these inferences are of an
entirely different nature from ordinary categorical proposi-
tions. The this, here and now, may easily be supposed to
be the same or nearly the same as class-names, so that the
propositions would be like "S is P." But in truth every
proposition whatever has some sign corresponding to the
now, here, and this of these propositions. Only this sign
is not usually a word. Suppose I say "Hamlet was mad."
What Hamlet do I mean? Shakespeare's creation or the his-
torical man? Suppose I say, "Any pair of conics in a plane
have four points in common." Am I speaking of real space,
or the imaginary space of the modern geometers? Suppose
I say "Every man is born of a woman." Do I mean only such
men as live now, or do I mean to include even primeval men?
Suppose I say, "No possible force can produce perpetual
motion." Do I mean to include under the possible all that
is supposable without contradiction or what is in accordance
with the laws of abstract mechanics or what is in accordance
with experience. To make the purport of these questions
more clear, consider that the subject of a categorical pro-
