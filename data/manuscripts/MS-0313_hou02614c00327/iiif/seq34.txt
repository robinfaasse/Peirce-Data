It is no great matter if the reader can see what
it is that I should say, or what it is that I am to
leave unsaid. It is sufficient if I have shown
what it is that I mean to say.

I have said that the primary object of the
Science of Ethics is to determine what is good
and what is bad. And I have said that the
fundamental question of ethics is to determine
what good means. I have said that this
question is the most fundamental question
of ethics, because upon the answer to it
depends the answer to every other question
of ethics. And I have said that the answer
to this question is that good is indefinable.

It is now time to consider what are the
consequences of this answer. And first I
must say something more as to what I mean
by saying that good is indefinable. I have
already said that I do not mean that it is
indefinable in the sense in which the word
"good" is indefinable. I mean that the
thing which the word "good" stands for is
indefinable. And I have said that I mean
by this that the thing which the word
"good" stands for is a simple thing,
and not a complex thing. I mean that
it is a thing which cannot be analysed
into parts. I mean that it is a thing
which is not composed of other things.

But what do I mean by saying that a thing
is simple? I mean that it is a thing which
cannot be defined. And what do I mean
by saying that a thing can be defined?
I mean that it can be analysed into parts.
I mean that it is a thing which is composed
of other things. And what do I mean by
saying that a thing can be analysed into
parts? I mean that it can be shown to
be composed of other things. And what
do I mean by saying that a thing is
composed of other things? I mean that
it is a thing which can be defined.
