The actual fact is that there is a deep
cleavage in the Republican Party which is
perfectly well known to the entire country, and
has been since the Taft-Roosevelt struggle
of 1912. That cleavage exists today in
Washington between the two groups of
Republican Senators, and in the country
between those voters who would like to
see Mr. Hoover reelected and those who
would prefer Senator Norris or Senator
Borah or Governor La Follette. No one
is going to be deceived by any talk
about party unity. There is no unity in
policy or in principle, and even the
machinery is divided, for in several
states the official Republican organization
is openly opposed to the national
administration.

The same thing is true of the Democrats.
There is a profound difference of opinion
within that party on almost every
major question of the day.

This means that the voters cannot
vote for a coherent and unified party.
Whether they choose the Republicans or the
Democrats, they are choosing a coalition
of factions which will disagree among
themselves as soon as they are elected.

Under our system this is inevitable.
A party must include almost every
shade of opinion if it is to win a
majority in a nation so large and
with such varied interests as our own.
Each party is really a kind of
league of nations, and the real
political struggle takes place, not
so much between the two parties, as
within each of them for control of
the organization.
