II.
We have now reached the point at which one
may profitably ask ourselves what logical goodness
is. We noticed that any kind of goodness is the
adaptation of its subject to [del]an[/del] its end. This is a
remark so obvious that you may very likely
dismiss it as nothing more than a verbal
definition. Yet I do not know that we shall find
any more succinct statement of the principle
of pragmatism than this. The fault of the vulgar
utilitarian is not that he asks [ins]would be[/ins] what [del]is[/del] the
use of this or that, but that he never does ask.
He simply rests in his present desires and never
submits them to any criticism at all. He wants,
let us suppose, to go to heaven. But what would be
the [ins]good of[/ins] [del]use of[/del] going to heaven? He may answer that
there he would be happy. But that is a mere
word. It is as much as to say that he wants what
