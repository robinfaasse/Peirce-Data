(1b) ($1'\alpha$). $X$ and $Z$ both superior to $A_{0}$. $X$ superior to $Y$, $Z$ inferior to $Y$; so that $X$ superior to $Z$.
$X$ is second-superior to $Z$ by the second rule.

(1b) ($1'\beta$). $X$ inferior to $A_{0}$; $Z$ superior to $A_{0}$
$X$ is second-superior to $Z$ by the fourth rule.

(2a) ($2'$). $X$ inferior to $A_{0}$; $Z$ identical with $A_{0}$.
$X$ is second-superior to $Z$ by the first rule.

(2b) ($2'$). $X$ inferior to $A_{0}$; $Z$ superior to $A_{0}$.
$X$ is second-superior to $Z$ by the fourth rule.

(2c) ($2'$) $X$ and $Y$ inferior to $A_{0}$. $X$ inferior to $Y$, and $Y$ to $Z$, so that $X$ inferior to $Z$.
$X$ is second-superior to $Z$ by the third rule.

It will be seen that in all cases $X$ is second-superior to $Z$, when it is so to $Y$ and $Y$ is so to $Z$.

That two things cannot be second-superior to each other is evident; for the rules show that if one thing is second-superior to another it is either superior or inferior to that other, and therefore not identical with it. But from the property just proved (putting $X$ in place of $Z$,) if $X$ were second-superior to $Y$ and $Y$ second superior to $X$, then $X$ would be second superior to $X$.
