[del]So[/del] In intercommunication, too, likenesses are quite
indispensible. Imagine two men who know no common
[del]tongue[/del] speech, thrown together remote from the rest of
the race. They must communicate; but how are
they to do so? By imitative sounds [del](which are a kind
of likeness),[/del] by [del]so[/del] imitative gestures, and by pictures.
These are three kinds of likenesses. It is true that
they will also use other signs, finger-pointings, and
the like. But after all the [del]pictures or[/del] likenesses
will be the only means of describing [del]their feelings
or[/del] the qualities of the things and actions which they
have in mind. Rudimentary language, when men
first began to talk together, must have largely consisted
either in [ins]directly[/ins] imitative words, or in conventional names
which they attached to pictures. The Egyptian language
is an excessively rude one. It was, perhaps, as far as we
know, the earliest to be written; and the writing is all
in pictures. Some of these pictures came to stand for
sounds, - letters [del]and[/del] syllables. But others stand directly
for ideas. They are not nouns; they are not verbs;
they are just pictorial ideas.
