§39. In all reasoning, we have to use a mixture of
likenesses, indices, and symbols. We cannot dispense
with any of them. The complex whole may be called a
symbol; for its symbolic, living character is the prevailing
one. A metaphor is not always to be despised: though a man
may be said to be composed of living tissues, yet portions of
his nails, teeth, hair, [ins]and bones[/ins] [del]which are most necessary to him[/del] have ceased to undergo the
metabolic processes which constitute life, and these are
liquids in his body which are not alive. Now, we may [ins]liken[/ins] [del]call[/del]
the indices we use in reasoning to the hard parts of the
body, and the likenesses we use to the blood: the one [ins]one[/ins]
holds us stiffly up to the realities, the other with its swift
changes supplies the nutriment for the main body of
thought.

Suppose a man to reason [del]as follows[/del]. The Bible says
that Enoch and Elijah were caught up into heaven; then,
either the Bible errs, or else it is not strictly true that
all men are mortal. What the Bible is, and what the [del]world[/del]
historic world of men is, [ins]to[/ins] which this reasoning relates, must
be shown by indices. The reasoner makes some sort of
mental diagram by which he sees that his alterna-
tive conclusion must be true, if the premise is [ins]so[/ins]; and this
diagram is an icon or likeness. The rest is symbols;
and the whole may be considered as a modified symbol.
[del]This is not a dead thing,[/del] but carries the mind from one point to
another. The art of reasoning is the art of marshalling such signs, and of
[del]finding out the truth.[/del]
