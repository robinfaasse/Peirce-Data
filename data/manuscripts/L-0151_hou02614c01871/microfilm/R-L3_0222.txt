When a man recounts to me how many systems of philosophy he has read
a feeling of weariness invades me. In cases of individuals unlike your-
self this feeling is tempered by some amusement, but I feel sure you know
that I know, however many systems a man may have read the number would
bear the proportion to all the philosophies of 1 : [del]7[/del] [ins]?[/ins] (1 + m)[sup]n[/sup], m being a
positive quantity. Yet all these systems of philosophy are reducible to
a few fundamental forms; the variables, due to the race or temperament of
the devotees, constituting the anecdotal part of the creed without alter-
ing its skeleton. All that the study of philosophy or any other study can
do for man is to teach him to think for himself.

If he be honest as well as calm and sane, a moderate acquaintance with
a few profound thinkers will teach him how to use his faculties, and he
will then be as fit to consider these stupendous problems as if he had
read - and remembered - every speculation which had ever been made.
After the [del]/[/del] opening sentence of your letter. It is not very flattering to
read further on that "Haeckel's particular form" (of Monism) (whatever you
may mean by that) "seems to have least in its favor". However, as I am
not a subscriber to Haeckel's or any other form of Monism or any other -
ism, I am implicated only through your very undistributed middle.

I agree with you that A. Dallas Bache, exercised the strongest and
most beneficent influence on all who had the privilege of knowing him
intimately, as I did, boy though I was. I had always the most unbounded
admiration for your Father who was one of the intellectual giants of our
country in that day. No men could have attained their eminence without
that scientific calm and fairness which wins all the progress and saves
from all the pitfalls. I have been led to believe that you inherited from
your Father this trait, it is, therefore, difficult for me to explain
several predicates of your letter besides the first which I mentioned.
What are your grounds for calling Haeckel a "one sided" man (unless a pun
on Monism) ? What facts have you to prove that "it is but to make [del]in him[/del]
instinct our main staff" ? And what is instinct anyhow ? What do you
advise in the usual case of the instincts of two persons leading in dif-
ferent directions ? As to the identity of the conception of God by St.
Augustine and Haeckel I have already said I give the identical words of
each (the former in the accepted, [del]/[/del] Church translation). Of course these
separate propositions can be tinkered by the theological tack hammer to a
point of dissimilarity, but if they mean what they say the conceptions
are identical to the extent stated in my "Creedless Union" article. Your
penultimate sentence is this: "But I think there is vast vital good in
religious Faith and Fervor, even if the object of one's cult be only a
Haeckel".

If in the latter part of this sentence the allusion is to me its
utter inapplicability destroys its sting. The depreciation of Haeckel
arises from the fact that you do not know him and, as you say, are no
judge of his influence in Biology.

I cannot admit two kinds of guides; one for "everyday" and one for
"a number of centuries"; or one for week days and one for Sundays.

I believe firmly the same intelligence employed in the same way is
our only guide in buying shoes or criticising Kant; nor can I see any
logical ground for your conclusion that because of a difference in the
period which must elapse before the conclusion is reached, the process of
reaching it is necessarily different.
