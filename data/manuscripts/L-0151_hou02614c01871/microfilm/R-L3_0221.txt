Room 1042 Drexel Building Philadelphia

[date]July 2. 1902.[/date]

My dear Professor Peirce,

The first sentence of your valued letter of the 27th
ult. fairly amazes me. It is this: "Haeckel seems to satisfy your whole
soul". What remotest vestige of basis have you for such an assertion?
In the Epitome of the Welträthsel I have carefully [del]avoided[/del] [ins]evaded[/ins] introducing a
word as to my own mental attitude on the cosmogony. I have simply permit-
ted myself the reflection embodied in the last quatrain; and I was justified
in this by the language employed by St. Augustine in his confessions and by
Haeckel in his great resume of his life's work. I have said in the modest
article in "Mind" that few would challenge the right of Haeckel to speak
with authority as one of the greatest of Naturalists, and in spite of the
detraction of his eminent opponents in Germany, Virchow and Dubois-Raymond,
and the echo of abuse from certain lesser personages in the United States
and England, no sane man with the information necessary to form an opinion
will contradict this statement; especially after a personal acquaintance
with the man. I repeat, therefore, I am astonished that one, the premisses
of whose "everyday reasonings" must be more than unfortunate guesses,
should have been able to write the opening sentence of your note. Quite on
the contrary, as I have hinted in the lines to my class, I cannot accept
Haeckel's negations of the paraphernalia of creeds, not because I believe
any of them but because I am unable to disprove them or any other gossamer
spun by the brain. Repulsive, and even ridiculous as the anthropomorphic
idea of God and his government is, one may accept the whole scheme of the
Welträthsel without excluding it. There is time and place before, during,
or after Haeckel's cycle, from protyle to the dissolution of the Primate
man, for the appearance of a personal Deity, but so far as my intelligence
permits me to go there is not a shadow of proof of such a Deity.

What Haeckel has done is to unite all the small and large islands of
human knowledge into one archipelago with intelligible mutual relationships:
but where this is done it is only an archipelago, after all, lying in an
immense and, so far as we can see, a measureless Ocean of the unknown.

The cosmogonies of the Buddhist, the Brahmans, the Fire worshippers,
the Jews, the Christians, the Monists, or any other of the thousands of
more or less imaginative attempts to formulate what is unknowable are
equally unintelligible to me. I respect them all in proportion to the sin-
cerity of those who profess them, but am forced to reject them all.
Huxley's seems to me the only rational attitude (Letter to Canon Kingsley
May 22. 1863). He says x x "there are only four possible ontological
hypotheses now that Polytheism is dead
I. There is no x (Atheism on Berkeleyan principles)
II. There is but one x (Materialism or Pantheism according as you
turn it heads or tails)
III. There are two x[ins]s[/ins] Spirit and Matter = Speculatores incertae sedis
IV. There are three x God, Souls, and Matter = Orthodox Theologians
To say that I adopt any one of these hypotheses as a representa-
tion of fact would be absurd; but No 2 is the one I can work
with best". x x x "It may turn out that I am quite wrong and
there are no x, nor 20 x's".

This statement formulates much more cleverly than mine my own posi-
tion, as does the following sentence in a previous part of the same letter.
"Materialism and Spiritualism are opposite poles of the same absurdity".
