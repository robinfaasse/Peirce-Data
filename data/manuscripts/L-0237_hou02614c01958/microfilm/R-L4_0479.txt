Another question is, supposing Aristotle's definition be adopted, that a syllogism is a necessary form of inference from two or more premisses, whether there is any one mode of decomposition of all syllogisms? The answer would seem [del]to be[/del] [ins]to be[/ins] that there is one form of syllogism which seems simple. It is

A is true
B is true
$\therefore$ A and B are true.

This simple copulation forms a step in every syllogism, and generally the only difficult step. In most syllogisms, there is besides a loss of information and an elimination. These two elements are quite independent of one another. Either may take place without the other; although it is only in special cases that elimination can take place without loss of information. In all cases, the purpose of the elimination is that of any other act of abstraction; and the loss of information [del]is[/del] commonly its inevitable concomitant.

Elimination of a term of first intention is always produced by the substitution for some term of first intention of a term of second intention, according to some logical formula, after which the term of second intention can be removed according to another logical formula. These two processes may, of course, be regarded as one. Elimination may either take place independently of the form of the eliminated term or in consequence of that form. The latter is the more important case. It usually follows from the principle of contradiction, that x that is not-x is nothing. Indeed, all cases [ins]of elimination by form[/ins] may [del]be[/del] regarded as substantially of that nature.
