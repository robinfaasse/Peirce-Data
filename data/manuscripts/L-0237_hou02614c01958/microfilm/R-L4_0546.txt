Since it is the universal practice, not only of mathemati-
cians but of everybody who ever made [ins]complex[/ins] signs of any kind,
to write signs together with nothing intervening to signify
relative multiplication, Schröder ought logically to write
instead of $x \dagger y$ $x ; \dagger ; y$ or rather $x ; ; ; \dagger ; ; ; y$ and so
on ad infinitum. If he [del]fails[/del] [ins]fails[/ins] to have an everywhere "condensed"
multitude of semicolons, he writes characters together to
signify relative multiplication. We are thus forced by the
nature of things to that usage [ins]this[/ins] which leaves the dot as the simplest
possible way of signifying the first relation of logic.

My other remark about particular copulas is that we have
all been wrong in [del]also[/del] putting the obelus over the uni-
versal copulas. For as

$x \cdot y = \overline{\bar{x} \curlywedge \bar{y}}$ we ought to consider $\overline{x \cdot y} = \bar{x} \curlywedge \bar{y}$

That is, [del]a[/del] composition is not the negative of $\curlywedge$ but of
aggregation. So we ought to write

$\overline{x \curlywedge y} = \bar{x} \curlywedge \bar{y}$ not $x \bar{\curlywedge} y$. In short, DeMorgan's dots
are logically placed.
