I will now pass to the matters of [u]Logical Analysis[/u] which,
in my opinion, are wanted in the article.

One question, very urgent, as it is seems to me, is, what does the
logical decomposition of a reasoning consist in? Shall we say
that if two processes start from the same premisses and reach
the same [ins]necessary[/ins] conclusion, and that in a purely formal way, so
that, if other terms were substituted, the conclusion would be
equally necessary, then those two processes of reasoning are
logically identical? That is to say, if two leading principles
of reasoning [del]met[/del] have precisely the same effect, they are,
so far identical; if they differ, they must have different effects
in some cases. This would be the pragmatical method, based
on my maxim, published in the [u]Pop. Sci. Monthly[/u] for [date]Jan 1878[/date] and
in the [u]Revue Philosophique[/u], and since generally conceded to be
original with me: "Consider what effects that might conceivably
have practical bearings we conceive the object of any concep-
tion to have. Then, our conception of those effects is the whole
of our conception of the object." Like some other ideas of mine,
this is rated by some others [ins]to be[/ins] much more satisfactory than by
myself; but that is of no importance. It would follow that
the analysis of an argument is something like the resolution
of a force, in that it can be performed in various ways, some
of which are extravagantly aside from any rational purpose.
Although this analogy is not apparently a deep one, yet it
immediately prompts us to remark that the resolution of a
force into [del]many[/del] [ins]orthogonal[/ins] [del]constituents[/del] [ins]components[/ins] has a special value, although
this also may be performed in various ways; and further that the
resolution of a force into components which obey natural laws
can only be performed in one way, the components being in
any multitude and not necessarily orthogonal.
