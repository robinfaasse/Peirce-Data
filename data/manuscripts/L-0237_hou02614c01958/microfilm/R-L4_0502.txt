p. 9 I do not think you make it clear that there is any
difference between "the a's which are b" and "the b's which
are a" except a difference in words. But anyway,
even if there be such a difference (which I doubt) you
seem to admit that for logical purposes they are identical.

In my view forms may have precisely the same meaning
and yet it may be most important to distinguish between them
in logic. Thus, if a line means nothing but the place where
a [del]point[/del] [ins]particle[/ins] may be at [del]some[/del] [ins]possible[/ins] part of a time, then to talk of lines
and to talk of the motions of particles means the same thing.
Yet the one leads to the notion of a surface while the other does
not. Again, Camestres and Cesare are forms of precisely the
same argument. Yet logically they are perfectly distinct,
one being of the general class
P loves whatever M loves
whatever P loves benefits whatever S loves
[u]$\therefore$ whatever M loves benefits whatever S loves[/u]
and the other of the general class
whatever P loves benefits whatever M loves
P loves whatever S loves
[u]$\therefore$ whatever M loves is benefitted by whatever S loves[/u]

I observe you call a hypothetical proposition a compound
proposition. It seems to me that that is inconsistent with
regarding the copula as expressive of the relation of
antecedent and consequent. For from that view it follows
[del]that[/del] a categorical, say all men are mortal, is
[ins]If anything, X, is a man then that X is mortal[/ins]
so that the difference between the categorical and condi-
tional (or other hypothetical) is that the relative pronoun X occurs
in the categorical. Thus the categorical is [del]less[/del] [ins]simple than the hypothetical[/ins]
