<u>Signs</u>. Next I note that [del]signs[/del] [ins]a sign[/ins] is something [del]which[/del]
brings an object into relation to another sign of the [del]same[/del]
object, its <u>interpretant</u>. Next, signs are of three kinds:
<u>icons</u>, which represent objects only so far as they are
like them; <u>indices</u>, which represent objects by blindly
forcing a relation between the interpretant and the
object; and <u>symbols</u>, whose characteristic is that they
represent their objects upon some general principle
represented by the interpretant. A perfect symbol is
one which separately indicates what is the interpretant
which it determines. Since it is the nature of a symbol
to determine its interpretant rationally, that is upon a
general principle, in order to be a good symbol, it ought to
show what the determination is to which it leads. This
is the <u>inference</u>. But even if it does not reach that pitch
of perfection, it ought to separately indicate what its
object is. If it does that, it is a <u>proposition</u>. A proposition
has no force as proposition, that is has no truth or falsity,
unless it on the one hand involves a reference to some
<u>index</u> which points out what it is talking about, and, on the
other hand, involves a reference to some <u>icon</u> which images
the [del]sort of[/del] thing which it declares [del]that image of[/del] [ins]the object of[/ins] that index to be.
