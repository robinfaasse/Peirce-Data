Milford Pa [date]1901 Dec 12[/date]

My dear Mrs. Franklin:

I will now answer your letter. But I will take the liberty of retaining Keynes article unless you want it. If you do, you had better just say so, because I have a big pile of unread books.

I have a sort of suspicion I may be wrong about existence; but no such formal arguments as you adduce can shake me. Indeed, I differ so little from what you say that I should hardly be moved to say anything. Only I shouldn't want you to argue [del]that silence[/del] [ins]is unqualified assent,[/ins] as about Mitchell's statement.

The passage of Kant I alluded to was his refutation of the ontological proof, especially p. 599 of 1st Ed. of C. d. r. V. As the thing was often stated in the middle ages, you can't [del]go[/del] dispute the truth of a definition. Now a perfect being is by definition existent. So there you are! What is the answer? That existence is not a predicate. The question is, Is any existent perfect. God is, logically stated, means Something is God. When you have two terms as Some man is good, of course you can convert. For you mean Something is man and good. But when you only have one there is nothing to convert.

A proposition is a sign which separately indicates its subject. The subject may be, strictly speaking, a symbol. But it fulfills the function of [ins]term[/ins] an index. When you say Some man is good, the "man" is not, therefore, really the subject or part of the subject but part of the predicate.
