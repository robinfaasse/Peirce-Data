It seems to me that the worst thing in your article is what appears
to me to be the manifest fallacy of your professed proof that the
syllogism cannot be proved. That you assume as a premiss
what is not universally admitted, and which I, for example, am
prepared to disprove, namely, that "proof consists in referring
a special case to a general rule," is a comparatively small fault. Sup-
pose it were so. Still you are arguing [del]that it is[/del] just as a person
might who were to refuse to employ a watchman because the
watchman might turn thief, or to employ any precaution
because no precaution is infallible. If, therefore, there is no
better reason against the proof of the syllogism than what you
give, it is an objection to all proof of everything, and equally
refutes your proof that the syllogism cannot be proved. I say
that there is no "syllogistic axiom." Logicians have been perfectly
right in holding that the [ins]Aristotelian[/ins] syllogism involves no axiom. The [u]dictum[/u]
[u]de omni[/u] is a definition; and that is the reason why the syllo-
gism produces no addition to knowledge. When I say that all
men [del]is[/del] [ins]are[/ins] mortal, what I mean to say is, that whatever is a man
is mortal. Substitute any other relation for the copula, and it
is all the same.
Whatever loves a man loves a mortal
Socrates loves a man

A proposition must relate to something. What does your supposed axiom
relate to? What I mean by a proof is rendering a truth plain and
undeniable. You do not render your proposition plain and un-
deniable, but its contradictory that the syllogism is proved, that is,
is rendered plain and undeniable by the dictum de omni, remains.
Infallibility is an absurdity, — that is hypothetical infallibility, for prac-
tically one may say the syllogism is infallible. [del]Observe, if you please, that it
is one thing to see the force of a single syllogism, and quite another to admit all mistaken syllogisms.[/del]
