Besides this singular and primary universe of the Truth, there is
one for a non-relative or monadic predicate, two for a dyadic,
and so on, and besides these, there may be universes of space,
time, and possibility. These are general. But the universe of
possibility may become singular. In that case, it is a mere
matter of formal convenience to distinguish it from the Truth.
There may be several universes of possibility, of different kinds.
We may conveniently call those subjects and the universes for
which the predicate or rheme leaves blanks, one when non-
relative, two if dyadic, etc., [u]terminal subjects and universes[/u], and
the others [u]propositional subjects and universes[/u]; although I apprehend
that the distinction is merely artificial.

Ordinary logic, including the logic of relations, takes no
cognizance of any [del]two genuinely relative[/del] general universes,
but considers them all as collections. A collection is a
singular subject whose existence [ins](i.e. presence in the universe)[/ins] consists in the existence
of other subjects, [del]for[/del] [ins]called its members,[/ins] which are both singular and independent
of one another and of the collection; and the truth of any
proposition concerning the collection consists in the truth
of corresponding propositions concerning every one of its
members. Now there is no greatest collection, as I have
very prettily proved. (But Cantor marks a passage in one
of his papers which he sends to me from which it appears
that he had reached the same result by means of his
ordinal numbers.) This completely solves the puzzle

point had occupied me for years. It would be a great
