about questions of physics. A Greek could not believe
that there it was any way to learn to understand a
flower, to begin by picking it to pieces and thus
spoiling the flower. In like manner, he thought that
if you want to know why, if you throw a stone, the
stone keeps on moving for a while after it leaves the
hand, you must not analyze the phenomenon and
experiment upon its [ins]different[/ins] elements separately, which would
be a course of tedious inanity; but you must dismiss
close thought and give such an account of the matter
as the entire phenomenon suggests. The inevitable result
of this opinion was that Greek physics was as futile
as it could be. Of course, I do not mean the physics
of Archimedes and Ptolemy, nor (so far as we know
it) that of Hipparchus, Eratosthenes, and Posidonius,
men who analyzed the facts and studied them minutely,
which we now know is the only way to succeed.
