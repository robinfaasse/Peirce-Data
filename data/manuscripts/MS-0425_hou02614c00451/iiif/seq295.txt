I. Some writers maintain that the goodness and badness
of reasonings is not mere signified by, but is constituted
and composed of the satisfaction or dissatisfaction
of a certain logical feeling, or desire, within us. One of
the most generally esteemed of [ins]the[/ins] logicians of today, Sigwart,
a translation of whose Logic is published in two huge
octavos, is of this number. Schröder, the leader of exact
logic in Germany follows him. Sigwart's argument, and
the fallacy of it, will be explained below. But logic
supposes that there is a difference between good and bad
reasoning dependent upon whether or not reasoning
leads to the [u]truth[/u]; and by the truth is meant something
not dependent upon how we think or feel about it.
Sigwart's position amounts to a denial that there is any
such distinction. It harmonizes well with the prevalent
practice of German reasoners, which follows feeling
