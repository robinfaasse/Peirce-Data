The doctrine of a light of reason is one of the oldest of philosophemes and [ins]our inquiry will demonstrate that it is[/ins] [del]one of the truest, as we shall find.[/del] The phrase may probably be found in every literature. The "old philosopher," Lao-Tze, who lived in the 6th century B.C. says for example, * "Whoso [del]employ[/del] [ins]uses[/ins] reason's light and turns back and goes home to its enlightenment does not surrender his person to perdition. This is called practising the eternal." The [u]idea[/u] is involved in the old Babylonian philosophy of the first chapter of Genesis, where the Godhead says "Let us make man [del]after[/del] in our image, after our likeness." It may, no doubt, justly be said that this is only an explanation to account for the resemblance of the images of gods to men, a difficulty which the second commandement meets in another way.
