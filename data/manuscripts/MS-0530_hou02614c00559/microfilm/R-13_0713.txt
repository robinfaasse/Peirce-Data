A disjunctive proposition is one [del]where[/del] [ins]a hypothetical[/ins] [del]falsity[/del] [ins]whose falsity[/ins] would involve the truth or falsity of one or more of its members.

There is no recognized designation for a hypothetical of which neither the truth nor the falsity would involve the truth or the falsity of any of its members. Such are the propositions,

A and B are either both true or both false;
One and only one of the propositions A and B is true.

Aulus Gellius (about A.D. 150), apparently making notes from [ins]of a[/ins] book of Marcus Varro (who was ten years older than Cicero), mentions that the copulative proposition is called 'conjunction' or 'copulatum'; and Martianus Capella (about A.D. 470) calls it 'copulativa'.
