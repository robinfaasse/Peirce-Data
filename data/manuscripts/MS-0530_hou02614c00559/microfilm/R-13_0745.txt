Accordingly, (lover)[ins]^(benefactor)[/ins] for "lover applying to every one of the benefactors" is quite accurate, if we allow the numerical simile. Moreover, just as $2^3 = 2^1 \cdot 2^1 \cdot 2^1$, so "a lover of [del]a[/del][ins]every[/ins] benefactor" is a lover of the first benefactor, which is at once a lover of the second benefactor, which is a lover of the third benefactor etc. That is, it is a "logical product," i.e. a composite of lover of a benefactor, [del]taken[/del] compounded with itself, [del]every[/del][ins]according to every[/ins] benefactor. The objection made is, that in mathematics, it is usual to write $\underline{F}^2 \underline{x}$ to mean $\underline{F}\underline{F}\underline{x}$; and that there are important advantages in doing this.

Accordingly, (lover)[ins]^(benefactor)[/ins] ought to mean lover of a lover of a lover, etc. as many times repeated as there are benefactors.
