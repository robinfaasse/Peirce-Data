This is called 'Composition' by Clauberg and Le Grand.

The members are called the parts of the hypothetical by almost all logicians. Whately alone seems to take pains to call them [u]members[/u].

The members are said to be [u]conjoined[/u] (or joined together, or, in French, [u]joints[/u]), by Blundevile, the Port Royalists and others; to be 'coupled' by Blundevile, the Conimbricenses, Burgersdicius, etc.; to be 'compounded' by Clauberg; to be 'united' (or 'adunatae' or 'unitae') by Clauberg, Aldrich, Whately, etc.; to be 'connected' by Burgersdicius; to be 'knit together' by Wilson.

Watts distinguishes [ins]between[/ins] the distinction of [u]simple[/u] and [u]complex[/u], and that of [u]single[/u] and [u]compound[/u]; and he does not call [del]the[/del] a hypothetical a complex proposition but only a compound one. That is to say, he does not admit that it can be [del]called[/del] quite properly called one proposition.
