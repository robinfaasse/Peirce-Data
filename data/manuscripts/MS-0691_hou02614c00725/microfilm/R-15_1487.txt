Consequently, the Theaetetus cannot have been
written until long after, for two conclusive reasons.
First, down to [del]near[/del] [ins]about[/ins] 390 B.C. Plato had written only
historically concerning Socrates; and would not have
introduced a palpable fiction. Secondly, although Plato
sparingly introduces anachronisms, they are merely
as passing references. He could not have made an
anachronism so glaring and obtrusive until long
years had elapsed, — say not before 380 B.C. Therefore,
I hold those critics to be wrong who infer that the
Theaetetus was written soon after 394 B.C. Its gene-
ral substance seems to place it not much before the
Parmenides, which, we shall see, was written about 367
B.C. Therefore, other critics suppose that the battle alluded
to was a fight which took place near Corinth in
368 B.C. But it seems to me that that supposes an
almost unsupposable [del]and utterly [unclear]unwarrantable[/unclear][/del] anachronism.
