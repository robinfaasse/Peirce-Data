§1. [u]Of Graphs in general[/u].

A [u]graph[/u], in the most general sense, would be a diagram composed, first, of [u]spots[/u], or parts treated as points having simple visible qualities, or colors, and also different [u]sides[/u]; second, of [u]nexes[/u], or parts treated as lines, though they too may have colors and sides; third, of [u]faces[/u], or parts treated as surfaces; and so on to higher dimensions.

An [u]infimal[/u] graph is one which has no faces, nor elements of higher dimensionality, nor any [ins]distinguishable[/ins] sides to its nexes.

A [u]bond-nexed[/u] graph is an infimal graph in which every nex [ins](called above)[/ins] has [del]two[/del] extremities and no more.

A graph has its Listing numbers. The census-theorem, as applied to infimal graphs, is: The chorisis of the graph less its cyclosis equals the chorisis of the spots [del]less[/del] plus the singularity of the nexes.

A [u]valental[/u] graph is [del]an infimal[/del] [ins]a bond-nexed[/ins] graph in which each [del]color[/del] of a spot determines the number of distinguishable sides, called [u]hands[/u], that spot possesses; and the end of a [del]nex[/del] [ins]bond[/ins] is attached to each hand. The unattached ends of the bonds are called the [del]loose ends of the graph[/del]
