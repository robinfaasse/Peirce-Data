§1. [u]The Law of Inquiry.[/u] Successful inquiry [del]is possible only[/del] [ins]progresses only[/ins] [del]pursues[/del] [ins]as it[/ins] [del]one invariable course; though it may be broken off, – or as we must presume, may make a long halt at any one of the posts on the road. The whole course of a complete inquiry consists of three successive parts, which I shall call its stages, each stage having a type which is peculiar to it.[/del]

[del]The mind is set in motion by some experience, whether in the World of Bodies, or the World of Minds, or in the World of Ideas, that attracts special attention. In order to its doing so, it must be out of the common run, and thus feel more or less like a surprise. This leads the inquirer to the close observation of similar phenomena, and to a deep, deep pondering upon them in search of some governing idea that may connect them; and at length he is thus led to some more or less reasonable conjecture. This is the first great stage of inquiry. I call it retroduction because it starts at consequents and recedes to a conjectural antecedent from which these consequents would or might very likely follow.[/del]
