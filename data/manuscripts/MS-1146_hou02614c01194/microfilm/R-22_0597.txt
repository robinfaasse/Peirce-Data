That virtue could be taught was a patent fact, but what was disputed
was the proposition of Socrates that virtue was [del]merely[/del] [ins]clear[/ins] knowledge.
So in Aristotle's time, it was, probably assumed that the circle could
be squared, since there must be some square neither greater nor smaller;
[ins]indeed whoever approves of the reading of the MSS must admit this, which is, for him, Aristotle's [marginalia]unquestionable major premise.[/marginalia][/ins]
But what was doubted was whether the quadrature of certain
lunes by [del]Hipparchus[/del] Hippocrates of Chios would help one to
square the circle, and that would depend on whether a circle could
be dissected into such lunes. Thus the presumptive conclusions
were historically the minor premises of the Syllogisms.
