I wish now to show that the direct electrolysis of coloring
matter is most essential to highly economical bleaching. This is a
part of a still wider proposition of immense practical importance,
namely, that if any chemical action can be produced by electrolysis,
then that is the most economical way of producing it. This proposi-
tion I believe to be as nearly without exception as it is in the na-
ture of an economical proposition to be. For those actions which
electrolysis brings about are of the kind called "endothermic," that
is to say, they involve the absorption of heat, and to bring them
about is to do work: they require expenditure of power. But chemi-
cal actions which take place of themselves are usually "exothermic,"
that is, they involve the production of heat, and in all cases they
set free energy. Now if an "endothermic" operation be so performed
that a part of the process is "exothermic," more work must have been
expended than was necessary, and it is impossible to fully utilize the
heat evolved in the exothermic part, which in fact will commonly go to
waste.

Let us see how this applies to the operation of bleaching.
This operation must involve a decomposition of the coloring-matter.
