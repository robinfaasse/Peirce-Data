[u]Chapter I. Introduction concerning Multitudes[/u]

[u]Art. 1[/u]. Before taking up Topics, which treats of continuity, it is neces-
sary to call attention to some theorems concerning discrete collections which
have escaped G. Cantor, although I have been brought to a knowledge of
them by the study of the writings of that great mind. In some respects, my
demonstrations of these theorems are unsatisfactory, and therefore I have
not yet quite shown that there is any reason at all for admitting them; for
there is no probability in mathematics. Yet I think that the defectiveness of
certain steps in the proofs are rather of the nature of a want of perspicuity of
thought than of real inconclusiveness.

[u]Art 2[/u]. If we generalize the idea of multiplication as much as possible, I think that
we may say that the product of a horse and a dog is one object, namely, the
pair composed of the horse and the dog, which may, or may not, be regarded as
the same as the pair in which the dog occupies the first place and the horse the
second. There is thus a distinction between ordered and unordered, or commutative,
multiplication. Under this general conception we may bring the ideas of the
multiplication of length and breadth to form an area, the [ins]non-relative[/ins] multiplication
of [del]the[/del] integer numbers, [del]correlative[/del] [ins]functional[/ins] [del]relative[/del] multiplication, and [del]the[/del] logical multiplication.
