REPORT
ON THE EFFECT OF A LIVE LOAD ON [u]Mr Morison's[/u] NORTH RIVER BRIDGE.
By C. S. Peirce,
Member of the National Academy of Sciences.

CHAPTER I. The Catenary.

§1. I am requested to put my solution of the problem in such a form that it can be applied in similar cases. To give it generality, then, as well as to preserve a unity of thought and notation throughout, I begin with elementary problems. The [u]catenary[/u] is the curve assumed by a weighted cord, that is perfectly flexible. The [u]common catenary[/u] is the case where the weight is uniformly distributed along the cord.

[u]Notation[/u]. Let the particles that are weighted be supposed to be numbered from 1 to N. Let the particles 0 and N+1 be fixed to towers.
[fig]Diagram of a cord with numbered particles suspended between towers[/fig] (The figure represents the inclination as everywhere in the same direction, to facilitate control over the + and - signs.) Let the stretches of cord between which the weights are hung be numbered from 0 to N. Thus the [u]n[/u]th stretch is between the [u]n[/u]th and (n+1)th particles, and the [u]n[/u]th particle is between the (n-1)th and [u]n[/u]th stretches.
[fig]Diagram of a continuous curve suspended between towers[/fig] When the distribution of weight is continuous to the end, we shall not usually distinguish between N and N+1.

Let H be the value of [u]n[/u] when the [u]n[/u]th stretch is horizontal. Under a live load H will vary.

Accented letters indicate quantities measured from H as an origin. Unaccented [del]let[/del] letters are measured from 0 as origin.

Subscripts are attached to variables to indicate the number of the particle or stretch referred to; thus [u]x[/u]H is the value of [u]x[/u] for the [u]n[/u]th particle when n=H.
