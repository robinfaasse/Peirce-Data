The idea is this. If, every day, you borrow a dollar for a week, you can pay back each dollar with the dollar you borrow a week later; and if there is no end to the performance, you gain seven dollars. If, however, your [del]total[/del] borrowings [del]are[/del] [ins]come to an end[/ins], you have eventually to pay out those seven dollars; and that is just the difference between a finite collection and an infinite one.

Leave out of account [ins]in the first instance[/ins] all those $v$s that are $\gamma$'d each by more than one $v$, then if of the others the first is $\gamma$ to a second, and the second is $\gamma$ to a third, etc., [del]if[/del] [ins]then if[/ins] the collection [del]is[/del] [ins]being[/ins] finite, you will eventually reach a $v$ which [del]has no[/del] [ins]finds no[/ins] $v$ [ins]that[/ins] can be $\gamma$ to except the first; and as for the $v$s which are $\gamma$'d by several $v$s each, they do not help to avoid the result, as long as each $v$ is $\gamma$ to some $v$ that is un-$\gamma$'d by any other $v$. It is simply the analytic form of saying that the collection of $v$s is exhaustible.
