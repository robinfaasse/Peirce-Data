[del]25[/del] [ins]4[/ins] Among functions, or operations, there is one extensive
class which is of particular importance. I call it the class of
[u]correspondential[/u] functions, or operations. Namely, if all the
variables, [ins]but one[/ins] independent and dependent, [del]all but one[/del] have a set of
values assigned to them, then, if the relation between them is a
[u]Correspondence,[/u] the number of different values which the remain-
ing variable can have, is [u]generally[/u] the same, whatever the partic-
ular set of assigned values may be; although this number is [u]not[/u]
necessarily the same when different quantities are thus left over
to the last. I say [u]generally[/u] the same, because there may be pe-
culiar isolated exceptions, though this limitation can have no
significance in dichotomic mathematics. A function which is in
correspondence with its arguments may be called a correspondential
function. It may be remarked that it is not the habit of math-
ematicians, in general statements, to pay attention to isolated
exceptions; and when a mathematician uses the phrase "in general"
he means to be understood as not considering possible peculiar
cases. Thus, I have known a great mathematician to enunciate a
proposition concerning multiple algebra to be true "in general"
when the state of the case was that there were just two instances
of its being true against an infinity of instances of its being
false.
