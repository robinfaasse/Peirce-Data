The object of taking the last two roles was to see whether one could get any help from a mathematical discussion in resolving a vexed question about the nature of [del]greatness[/del] [ins]human greatness[/ins]. Among the writers who have considered this subject seriously enough to think it worth while to embody their opinions in books devoted to that purpose, we find, though they are not numerous, that extreme differences prevail. Some authors incline more or less to the idea that great men are made of the most ordinary clay and that they differ from those we meet every day, not so much in any extraordinary native gifts, as in the singular circumstances in which they have lived and been reared. On the other hand, the heaviest weight, intellectually, of all this class of writers, maintains that the great man is so far superior to the rest of mankind that unfavorable circumstances are as powerless to suppress him as they would be to subject a human being to a community of dogs, if [del]he were[/del] placed among them.
