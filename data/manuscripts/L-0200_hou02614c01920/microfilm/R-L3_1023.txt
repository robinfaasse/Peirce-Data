An exceedingly useful rule about fractions (and indeed
[del]it almost suffices to deduce every property of fractions[/del]) is that
[u]$\frac{\text{A}+\text{B}}{\text{C}+\text{D}}$ is intermediate in value between $\frac{\text{A}}{\text{C}}$ and $\frac{\text{B}}{\text{D}}$.[/u]

E.g. Which is the greatest $\frac{27}{109}$ or $\frac{28}{113}$? By the rule the sequence
is $\frac{27}{109}$ $\frac{28}{113}$ $\frac{1}{4}$. But by the rule $\frac{1}{4}$ $\frac{2}{8}$ $\frac{1}{4}$ etc, so that $\frac{1}{4} = \frac{28}{112}$ and
we have the sequence $\frac{0}{1}$ $\frac{28}{113}$ $\frac{28}{112}$ Hence $\frac{1}{4} > \frac{28}{113} > \frac{27}{109}$.

In the advanced book I am inclined to favor
having a section in which some simple bits of theory
are introduced; because if that is properly handled
so as to cause the pupil to [del]really[/del] think about the
essential nature of number, there is no doubt I think
— not in my mind, — that he will acquire in that way
a confidence and mastery that he will never get
otherwise.

I would begin by showing how the cardinal numbers
differ from ordinary words in being instruments with
which we perform the experiment called counting.
Counting consists in successively marking each
single object of a collection [ins](which was unmarked before)[/ins] and on marking it
pronouncing (or silently thinking of pronouncing)
the next following vocable of the system of cardinal
numbers. Zero ought to be reckoned a cardinal
number, which is pronounced on seeing that all the
single objects to be counted are as yet unmarked.
