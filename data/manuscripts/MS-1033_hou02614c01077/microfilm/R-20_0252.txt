[pn]Acetylene[/pn] [u]Part II. Properties of Acetylene.[/u]

[u]Composition.[/u] In 1836, the atomic weights of Berzelius, which were generally in agreement with those now in use, had for a time been abandoned in favor of numbers called "equivalents," being electrolytic equivalents as far as possible, and [ins]mostly[/ins] agreeing with the quotient of the atomic weight by what we now call the valency. [del]But[/del] The equivalent of carbon was however, not taken as 3, but as 6. Avogadro's law, that in perfect gases of all kinds there is, at any given temperature, the same number of molecules per unit of volume in any one gas as in any other, was not generally appreciated. Hence, there was no definite criterion by which to determine the relative weights of molecules of different bodies. Prof. Edmund Davy, the discoverer of acetylene, correctly analyzed it, and ascertained that it consisted of 1 part of hydrogen and 12 of carbon. He wrote its formula HC₂, to express that fact, and called it [u]bicarburet of hydrogen[/u]. (See references under Hist[del]ory[/del])

[u]Constitution.[/u] Davy estimated the density of the gas as 0.917, and subsequent determinations have shown that that is substantially correct. Since it is thus 13 times as heavy as hydrogen, whose molecule is H₂, it follows by Avogadro's law that its molecular composition is H₂C₂. It is highly desirable that we should know the exact density [ins]at that proportion[/ins], because that would show how many of the molecules are dissociated or joined together. But no very exact determinations have been made.

Since carbon is quadrivalent, that is, has for each atom four "bonds," or attachments to other atoms, though our ideas of what "bonds" are are very vague, and the whole conception is rather a "vicarious hypothesis," as Kepler would say, than a truth, — that is, it is true in [del]working[/del] [ins]its[/ins] the consequences we are accustomed to deduce from it, but not true enough to be [del]pressed[/del] [ins]used[/ins] in new applications without verification, — but since carbon is quadrivalent, chemists usually represent acetylene by this "graph" [fig]chemical structure H-C≡C-H[/fig].
