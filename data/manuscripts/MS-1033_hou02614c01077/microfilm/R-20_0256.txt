But the immediate result of decomposing acetylene would be to leave the hydrogen in the form of also single atoms, not joined in pairs, as the atoms of hydrogen are as soon as they have had time to arrange themselves, while the carbon would be in the same nascent state or state of dissociation, and what is more in the state of gas. This decomposition does not take place spontaneously, at ordinary temperatures, because this [ins]immed[/ins] first step of the decomposition would involve destruction of heat; although this would be far more than made up again when the hydrogen atoms combined into pairs and especially when the carbon atoms fell [del]together[/del] into the form of soot. But just as an inkstand if once knocked off the table will fall all the way to the floor, so the atoms that compose acetylene if once jostled out of their places by a great shock will not fall back into acetylene again but into the form of hydrogen with a cloud of soot in it.[/del]

The principal workers in the field of thermochemistry, or the science of heat of combination, have been (not to speak of Favre and Silbermann and Andrews, who were its early promoters) Julius Thomsen of Copenhagen and Berthelot. They have pursued different methods of experimentation and of reasoning, and their results do not agree very well; but the results of each are consistent with themselves and tell the same story in the main. Thomsen's numbers are rather more in favor (though they are not implicitly believed) and I am better acquainted with them. Besides, the latest careful review of the philosophy of the subject by William Sutherland of Melbourne (London, Edinburgh, and Dublin Philosophical Magazine and Journal of Science [5] XL. 1) deals with Thomsen's figures. They are expressed in Kilocalories per gramme-atom. A gramme-atom is an amount of a substance weighing as many grammes as there are units in its atomic weight, when H=1. A Kilocalory is the amount of heat required to raise a Kilogramme of water 1°C. The definition usually given is from 0°C. to 1°C.; but the degree of precision of the determinations is hardly such as to warrant discriminating between the specific heat of water at ordinary temperatures and at the freezing-point.
