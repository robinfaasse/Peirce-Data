Digest of the Chemistry of Acetylene.

[u]Part I. Outline History, noticing the most important works only.[/u]

[date]1836[/date], Prof. Edmund Davy (a relative and former pupil of Sir Humphry) publicly made acetylene before the Royal Dublin Society. [del]See[/del] [ins]See his paper[/ins] Report of Bristol meeting of the British Association for the Advancement of Science (6th Meeting) Part ii. p. 62. Also, a brief paper in the Proceedings of Royal Irish Academy I. 56. Also, a memoir of his in Transactions Royal Irish Acad. XVIII. 80. There is also an account of the discovery in Annalen der Pharmacie. XXIII. 144.

He states that it burns brighter than olefiant gas, and also [del]contrary[/del] [ins]is contrary[/ins] to Henry's theory of the luminosity of flames. The violence of explosion of its mixture with oxygen is noted. Its composition, density, solubility, etc. were correctly determined by him. But its peculiar smell was not noticed.

He did discover acetylene in trying to make potassium by heating a mixture of [ins]previously ignited cream of tartar and charcoal powder.[/ins] [del]carbonate of potash and coal.[/del] What he got was the carbide of potassium, KC₂, which he described in a special paper (British Association Report 6th Meeting p. 53.) This carbide treated with water at ordinary temperatures gives off acetylene. This method of preparation [del]is better than from carbonate of calcium; but the high cost of potassium and its high atomic weight would prevent its competing with the method now in use.[/del]

This discovery attracted very little attention and was soon forgotten. His excellent method of making the carbide, far superior to any other used before [date]1890[/date]; and had proper attention been paid to it, it would have influenced the development of chemistry in general a good deal.
