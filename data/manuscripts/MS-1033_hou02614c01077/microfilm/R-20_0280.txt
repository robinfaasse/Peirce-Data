[u]2. Methods of preparing acetylene.[/u]

1. Davy prepared acetylene by decomposing the carbide of potassium with cold water. He made the $\text{K}_{2}\text{C}_{2}$ by igniting at a high temperature, in an iron bottle previously ignited, bitartrate of potassa, — which thus became a carbonate, — and charcoal powder. (Transactions Royal Academy XVIII. 80.)

2. Berthelot in 1860 made acetylene by passing olefiant gas mixed with the vapor either of alcohol, ether, aldehyde, or wood-spirit, through red-hot tubes. (Comptes rendus L. 805.) [ins]The proportion of acetylene was small.[/ins] [del]Annales de chimie et de physique [3] X. 365.[/del]

3. De Wilde in 1864 recommended making acetylene by passing ethylene vapor through a red-hot tube. (Bulletin de la Société chimique [2] I. 3.) In 1874 he proposed to improve upon this by acting upon the chloride of ethylene by quicklime or caustic soda (Berichte der deutschen chemischen Gesellschaft p. 352) He is decidedly of opinion that this is the easiest method, although it involves making beforehand and keeping both olefiant gas and chlorine.

4. H. Vohl in April 1865 recommended as an easy way of preparing acetylene the decomposition of American petroleum by passing the vapors through an iron tube partially filled with iron turnings, in order to give a greater surface, and heated to redness. (Dingler's polytechnisches Journal CLXXVI. Berthelot in 1866 declared that iron decomposes acetylene catalytically, and says that at the softening point of glass it determines the complete destruction of the acetylene.

5. Rieth first proposed in 1867 to prepare acetylene from a Bunsen burner arranged to make incomplete combustion. Berthelot took up the idea; and 1872 Feb 16 Jungfleisch described an apparatus for the imperfect combustion of lighting-gas. The acetylene was afterwards absorbed by [ins]an ammoniacal solution of cuprous chloride; and the acetylene was[/ins] [del]which had been washed free from ammonia[/del] [ins]afterwards[/ins] kept and decomposed by boiling hydrochloric acid when acetylene was needed (Bulletin de la Société chimique [2] XVII. 145.)

E. von Meyer in 1874 found that in the imperfect combustion of olefiant gas all the oxygen goes to making carbonic oxide without forming any water.

In 1875 Berthelot recommends this as the best way. He describes the operation in detail, and how to make the ammoniacal cuprous chloride. (Annales de chimie et de physique [5] V. 365.)
