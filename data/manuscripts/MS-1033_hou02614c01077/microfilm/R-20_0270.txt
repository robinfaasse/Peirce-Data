Acetylene

3. The electric spark passed through the vapor of almost any organic [del]substance[/del] produces acetylene. It was, really, so formed by Adolphe Perrot in [del]1857[/del] from the vapor of alcohol and that of ether (Comptes rendus XLVII. [del]350[/del]) although he did not recognize its nature. In 1866, P. de Wilde (Bull. de la Société chimique [2] VI. 267) showed that acetylene was so formed, as well as from the vapors of ethylene, acetone, amylic alcohol, oil of turpentine, benzene, and naphthalen. But de Wilde said this is not the case with the vapors of formic acid, acetic acid, or chloroform. But it was afterward shown that there are ways of producing it from chloroform and [del]from[/del] acetates, as will be shown below. Berthelot had announced in [del]1860[/del] March 3, that it is so produced from marsh gas (Comptes rendus [del]L. 505[/del]) and he says in 1866 that he had stated in 1862 (though the assertion escaped my attention) that all the hydrocarbons give acetylene by the spark. In 1884 Destrem (Comptes rendus XCIX. 138) says, that the electric spark passing through the vapor of benzene gives 42% acetylene and 58% hydrogen, and passing through the vapor of aniline gives 21% acetylene, 65% hydrogen, 9% prussic acid, and 5% nitrogen. But he says that in passing through the vapor of toluidene it [del]gives[/del] [ins]gives[/ins] 23% of ethylene and 77% of [del]acetylene[/del] [ins]hydrogen[/ins]. But then the [del]toluidene[/del] will, according to de Wilde's statement above, which was confirmed by Berthelot apparently, be decomposed into acetylene and hydrogen.

[date]1877 April 9[/date], P. Truchot (Comptes rendus LXXXIV. 714) described the effects of passing electric sparks through liquids heated [del]nearly[/del] to their boiling-points. Some very volatile liquids were experimented upon, such as "hydride of amylene," by which he probably means pentane, since that was called "amylic hydride" by its discoverer, Laurent (it boils at 30°, pentane at 37° to 39°), "amylene" [by which he may mean what now passes under the name of "commercial amylene"], and ether. These liquids would give say a litre per hour of gas; less volatile liquids give less. In all cases he obtained acetylene, ethylene, methane, and hydrogen, with no hydrocarbons containing more complex molecules. There was probably some ethane; but of that he could not make sure.
