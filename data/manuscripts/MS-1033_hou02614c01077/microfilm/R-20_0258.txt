[u]Acetylene[/u]

[u]Solid state[/u]. Berthelot, 1880 May 31 (Comptes rendus XC. 1240) gave the heat
of combustion as +318.1 (naturally greater than Thomsen's value since
his water is in the liquid state) and thence makes the heat of forma-
tion -60.4 (where the carbon is taken in the form of diamond); but
Nov 8 of the same year he gave the latter figure as -61.0. In 1893
June 12 Berthelot and Matignon (Comptes rendus CXVI. 1333) made the
heat of combustion +315.5 and that of formation -58.1.

Now Thomsen, on other grounds, has determined the heat of for-
mation of a bond between H and C to be 15 Kilocalories per gramme-
atom. He finds the heat of formation of a single bond between
two atoms of Carbon to be 14, and that of [del]a[/del] [ins]two[/ins] bonds between
[del]the for two atoms of carbon to be 14.2, so that the second bond[/del]
[del]would seem to have no dynamic value to speak of, and that[/del]
of three bonds between two atoms of carbon to be 0! According
to that a triple bond may be something but dynamically it is
the same as none at all. He also finds that to convert [del]an[/del] [ins]a gramme-[/ins]atom
of amorphous solid carbon into [ins]its state in[/ins] dissociated carbon gas, absorbs
38.4 kilocalories. According to these figures, if acetylene has the
constitution H-C≡C-H, as commonly assumed, its heat of for-
mation should be

$$\begin{array}{cc}
\text{2(H-C)} & 30 \\
\text{C}\equiv\text{C} & \frac{0}{30} \\
\text{2C to gas} & \frac{-76.8}{-46.8} \\
\text{Calculated heat formation} & \\
\text{of acetylene} & \\
\text{Found} & -47.8
\end{array}$$

This agreement is excellent; for this calculation leaves everything
out of account which is not represented by bonds. Hence, this
strongly favors the ordinary hypothesis that acetylene is H-C≡C-H.
But the principles of thermochemistry are not in a very satisfactory
state of development, as the value 0 for a triple carbon bond illu-
stantiates.
