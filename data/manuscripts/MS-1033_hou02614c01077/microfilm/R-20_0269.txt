The carbide of glucinum, Be₂C, was made by Paul Lebeau in 1895
(Comptes rendus, CXXI. 496), but it evolves methane, CH₄, when treated
with water.

The carbides of Yttrium [ins]YC₂[/ins] and Lanthanum [ins]LaC₂[/ins] were made by Richard in
1895 (Berichte der deutschen chemischen Gesellschaft XXVIII, p. 2419) but
Moissan [del]and Etard[/del] showed that YC₂ (Comptes rendus CXXII. 573) treated with water
gives a gas of which only 72% is C₂H₂; and [date]1896 July 20[/date] Moissan
(Comptes rendus CXXIII. 148) showed that LaC₂ treated with water gives a
gas consisting of 71% C₂H₂, 28% CH₄ and 1% C₂H₆.

Moissan showed (Comptes rendus CXXII. 357) that CeC₂ [ins]when[/ins] decomposed
by water evolves a gas of which 75% is C₂H₂.

Moissan showed (Comptes rendus CXXIII. 651) that the carbide of Zirconium,
ZrC is not attacked by water, [del]even when hot.[/del]

Moissan showed (Comptes rendus CXXII. 1297) that the carbide of vanadium,
VC is not attacked by water or steam even at red heat.

Moissan, [date]1896 July, 6[/date] (Comptes rendus CXXIII. 15) reports the existence of a
carbide of Tungsten, with a specific gravity of 16.06, W₂C.

Moissan and Etard (Comptes rendus CXXII. 573) say, that the carbide of thorium
ThC₂ (Sp. gr. 8.96 at 18°) is attacked by water and evolves a gas of which 47% [del]is[/del]

Moissan (Comptes rendus CXXII. 421) says that the carbide of manganese, Mn₃C
treated with water evolves a mixture of equal volumes of hydrogen and marsh gas.

Moissan (Comptes rendus CXXII. 274) says that the carbide of uranium, U₂C₃
(U = 240) slowly decomposes water evolving a mixture of gases containing C₂H₂.

Concerning other carbides we have no definite information, but the
smell of the gas evolved by cast iron seems to show that it has a trace of
acetylene. Berthelot in 1866 stated that chromous salts in ammoniacal
solution absorb acetylene and make [u]chromosacetyl[/u]. [ins]very likely[/ins] His statement about
the composition of the substance has no weight. It probably is a carbide
of chromium evolving acetylene on treatment with water. Thallic salts,
he says, "show some symptoms of reaction;" and from what we know of
the relation of thallium to other elements it is probable there is a carbide
of thallium which evolves a mixture of gases containing a considerable
amount of acetylene. Berthelot says that nickelous, cobaltous, ferrous,
manganous, and stannous salts do not absorb acetylene.
