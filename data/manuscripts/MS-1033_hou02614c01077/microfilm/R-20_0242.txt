In [date]1839[/date] copper gas-pipes were in use in the streets of New York. A workman engaged with others in replacing some of these, took up one of the old pipes, raised it to his mouth, and blew through it. Instantly there was an explosion which shattered his jaw so that he died in a few hours. Some pieces of the pipe were sent to Dr. John Torrey for examination. He found a brown explosive powder in them. He inferred from experiments made by him that it was a compound of copper with a hydrocarbon. But Mr. Chilton [del]claimed[/del] was supposed to have a prior right to investigate the question, and therefore Dr. Torrey went no further. (See his paper in American Gaslight Journal for [date]Oct 1, Vol I. p. 55[/date].)

In [date]1852[/date] Rudolf Böttger commenced researches on the action of illuminating gas on saline solutions and published a memoir on the subject in [date]1858[/date] (Jahresbericht des physikalischen Vereins zu Frankfort am Main, reprinted Dingler's polytechnisches Journal. CLII. 22). He showed that when ordinary lighting gas is led into [del]an[/del] [ins]an[/ins] ammoniacal solution of a cuprous salt an explosive compound is formed. He showed that this was a compound of copper with some hydrocarbon. But he showed that it was not formed by any hydrocarbon known to exist in the gas, and from the small amount of the precipitate, was probably some unknown hydrocarbon existing in small proportion in the gas. This was a clever investigation and must soon have led Böttger to the rediscovery of acetylene.
