heat is nothing but motion. Hence, if a chemical change takes place
by which heat is imparted to outside bodies, that change must
be of such a nature as to make the bodies take situations to which
more motion belongs, that is, situations toward which they are
more attracted. That comes to this, that if a chemical change be
such that it satisfies the natural tendencies to motion heat must
be evolved. Accordingly, most chemical [del]bodies[/del] compounds are
of such a nature that when the separate elements are brought
into these combinations, heat will be evolved. Such [del]bo[/del] compounds
are called exothermic. If on the contrary heat has to be imparted
to the elements to make them come into the combination, the com-
pound is called endothermic. No compound can be endothermic
in reference to the immediate decomposition which could occur. For
if it were, the decomposition would immediately take place. For exam-
ple, if a stone falls to the ground and lies there, its motion is imparted
to surrounding bodies, generally in the form of heat. Its lying down
on the ground is an exothermic situation. If it is thrown up from
the ground and in coming down falls on a table and lies there, this
situation is endothermic compared with the situation on the
ground. For if it were to fall from the table to the ground and lie
there, heat would be imparted to surrounding bodies. But the situa-
tion on the table is exothermic in regard to any displacement which
could immediately take place; for it can only leave the table by being
raised up, and that lifting would be work for a steam engine which
would destroy heat.
