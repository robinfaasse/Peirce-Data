Chapter 1.

THE ASSOCIATION OF IDEAS[ins]e[/ins]

In the absence of external impressions, thoughts chase one another through the mind
in a sort of Bacchic train. Each suggests another. After a while the clear train of thought
is broken, the ideas remain scattered for a time, and then reconcentrate in another train.
Psychologists recognize that the suggestion of one idea by another may take place according
to either one of two different principles: for an idea may suggest another like it, or it
may suggest[del]a[/del] another which has been connected with it in experience. Thus, the thoughts
of Niagara may suggest a hero or anything else that is grand, and so similar to the cataract,
or it may suggest a crowd of importunate hack-drivers, which is connected with the place in
every visitor's experience.

Association of the latter kind, association by contiguity as it is called, is the more
typical. In it the characteristics of mental association are more [ins]s[/ins]trongly marked.
Association by similarity is related to association by contiguity [del]as our inward[/del]
[del]conciousness is related to outward experience : the one association is due to a connection[/del]
[del]in outward experience, the other to a connection in own feelings. Many psychologists have[/del]
[del]proposed to reduce association by similarity to a special kind of association by contiguity;[/del]
[del]Few have been inclined to reduce the latter principle to the former.[/del]
