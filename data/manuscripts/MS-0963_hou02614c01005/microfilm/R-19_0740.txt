Under the head of association by causality, two widely different [del]things are confounded.[/del]

An effect cannot directly suggest its cause, as such: but if while we are thinking of a

phenomenon, the idea of cause is in any way suggested, we naturally think of the cause

of the phenomenon present to the mind. This is a case of Syllogistic association.

On the other hand, the suggestion by a cause of its effect, [del]is a[/del] [ins]fundamentally[/ins] important

case of the tendency of the mind to find in nature relations [ins]analogous[/ins] to intellectual

relations. The general rule of association by contiguity is that from any idea the mind

tends to pass to one that has followed it in experience. The idea of causality is that

one event is in reality associated with another, so as to follow after it, in reality

and not merely in the mind. But this is a matter which must be studied later.


Besides, it is mind which must serve to explain matter, and not matter mind. Causality

is to be explained by association, not association by causality. The very conception of

causality has its origin in our tendency to seek relations in nature analogous to intellectual

relations. As ideas are associated and naturally follow one another, so we expect to

find real phenomena naturally associated and following one another. This mental tendency

however, is a subject to be discovered which we must study at large in another chapter.
