The linear topical singularities of surfaces are either boundaries
or lines of splitting, which may be called [u]schists[/u]. The punctual
topical singularities of surfaces are either topical singularities of the
linear singularities or they are isolated. The latter are either [ins]punctures,[/ins] cus-
pidal (?) or stitchings of sheets. I do not admit the cuspidal
points as topical singularities, though I may possibly be wrong; nor
do I admit that boundaries can have extremities, for the extremities
of a cut in the middle of a surface seem not to be topically singu-
lar. But perhaps I am wrong. Since space has no topical singu-
larity, solid spaces can have no topical singularities except limiting
surfaces and their singularities, interrupting lines and their
furcations, and interrupting [ins]topically, nonsingular,[/ins] points.

[del]By the[/del] I divide surfaces into two great classes, the artiad and
the perissid, that is, those whose cyclosis is even and odd. By a
[u]fornix[/u], or tunnel-bridge, I mean a part which may be cut round
and (like the bar of an anchor ring) and the two boundaries
so created separately closed over without separating the surface. An
artiad surface may be defined as one such that when all fornices have
thus been destroyed, every unlimited line on it divides it into two
regions. A perissid surface is one of which this is not true, provided
the line on it be of a certain kind, which I call a perissid line.
A plane, according to Projective Geometry, which I follow, is perissid;
but according to the Theory of Functions, Quaternions, [del]and[/del] my
father's geometry [ins]and hydrodynamics, if not all physics (for visual optics [ins](I mean optics as it enters into geometry where the propagation is left out of account)[/ins] cannot be said to agree with the doctrine of light)[/ins] it is artiad.
