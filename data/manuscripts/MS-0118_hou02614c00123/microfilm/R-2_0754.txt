The section of the absolute by a plane, as
well as any segment of it, is [del]at[/del] infinite.
A sector is also infinite. But [del]the area[/del]
of a triangle inscribed in the absolute is
finite. (In fact, a triangle cannot be infinite)
For it may partly fall short of and partly over-
lap a former position but cannot more than
cover it. Moreover, all such triangles are plainly
equal; since any one
may be superposed on
any other. All the angles
of such triangles are zero. If such a triangle is separated
into two by [del]the[/del] line from the vertex to an opposite
side, their angles are supplements. [del]Their[/del] The areas
of such triangles are [del]also[/del] determined as follows. Let
$\alpha$ and $\beta$ be [ins]respectively[/ins] the angles of two triangles whose
other vertices are on the absolute. Place the triangles
side by side with their finite angles at coincident
vertex. From the quadrilateral so formed we
may subtract a triangle having three vertices
on the absolute or else we may subtract the
quadrilateral from the triangle. The result [del]in either
case[/del] will be a triangle with two vertices on the absolute
