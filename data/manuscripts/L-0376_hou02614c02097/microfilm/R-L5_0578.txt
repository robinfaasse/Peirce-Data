Again, we might look to the nebulae. Until recent years I
believe it was customary to consider these objects much more
remote than the stars; but now considerable doubt has
been cast upon this belief, partly because there never was any
sufficient reason for believing them to be very remote, and
partly because in some of them a proper motion has been
suspected. There is no doubt, however, but that they are
grouped about the two poles of the Milky Way, and are
in a sense on opposite sides of the heavens. I realize, of course,
that before these considerations can have much weight it
must be shown that a [del]number[/del] [ins]pairs[/ins] of [ins]objects[/ins] correspond exactly
to one another, on opposite sides of the heavens. If you can find
[del]me[/del] an exhaustive catalogue of stars, or, preferably, nebulae,
I shall be pleased to make a most thorough examination
of it, in the hope of finding such correspondences. We
should have to consider, then, the probability that such
coincidences were accidental. This probability would, of course,
be vastly greater for stars than for nebulae, so that I
should prefer the latter.
