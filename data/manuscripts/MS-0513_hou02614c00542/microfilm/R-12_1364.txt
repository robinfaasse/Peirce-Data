The application of the Boolean calculus to relatives was made by me in [date]1870[/date]. The chief difficulty of the conception was to grasp the idea of a universe of pairs. I was only successful with dyadic relations, and have never been completely successful with [del]anything else[/del] [ins]the triadic doctrine[/ins]. Nor has anybody else. Probably the reason is that we do not grasp the conception of the universe as a universe of modes of distribution. In my memoir of [date]1870[/date] I had two kinds of involution
$l^s$ lover of every servant (if there be any)
$^ls$ lover only of servants (if of anything)

DeMorgan had expressed "lover only of servants" in the form "lover every way of servants." That is, conceiving every individual to have different ways of loving its different objects, all those modes of loving are distributed among servants in $^ls$, while in $l^s$ all the servants are distributed among those modes of loving. But in $^ls$ it is not said that there is [u]every[/u] distribution of all modes of loving among servants, but only that there is [u]some[/u] distribution of all modes of loving among servants, and so in $l^s$ there is some distribution of all servants among modes of loving. Let [del]a[/del] [ins]f mean "is a friend of" and[/ins] [del]"is[/del] "—annoys—". Then, $l^s \prec a^f$ implies that every distribution of all servants among modes of loving (as the correlates of those relations) is a distribution of all the friends the correlates of the servants among the modes of annoyance of the lovers, or else [ins]the former[/ins] [del]that[/del] distribution is not carried out. But when we come to triadic logic, we deal with a universe [del]in[/del] which includes every logically possible mode of distribution.
