When a [del]triple[/del] [ins]triadic[/ins] relative occurs here and there while the majority
of the relatives are dyadic, we can use [del]the[/del] one index with a quantifier
for that one.

[del]Otherwise,[/del] we shall have to employ [u]heavy parentheses[/u] with the
understanding that multiplication is not associative through
them. Thus, for $\Sigma_i \Pi_j \Sigma_k R_{ijk}$ we may write $\text{O}[(\text{N}\text{O})\text{J}\text{O}]$.
Or we can write $\text{O}(\Sigma_k R_k \text{J}\text{O})$.

[u]XIII[/u]

But there is another difficulty about the representation of
triple relatives which is so important that a separate section
had better be consigned to [del]the study of[/del] it. For it is a difficulty
which is felt not [del]only[/del] [ins]merely[/ins] in the Dyadic Algebra but in the General
Algebra, too. One might look rather to the Dyadic Algebra for some
help about it.

It is that when we come to triadic relatives, the operations
of relative multiplication and addition are by no means sufficient.

As long as we are dealing with a dyadic relation, we have only to
conceive of a table of double entry with one row and one column for
each individual in the universe, and then each square of intersection
will be filled or empty according as the individual of that row is or
is not in the given relation to the individual of that column.

[fig]Grid diagram with numbered rows and columns[/fig] Now we cannot describe every single arrangement. We can only
describe those which present some regularity and that in so far as
that regularity determines them. Such regularities will consist in a row or column
being full or not full, empty or not empty, — or full except on dexter diagonal etc.
