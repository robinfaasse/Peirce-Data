The whole truth of the matter seems to be that, taking any collection of hecceities whatever, there is a quality which is common and peculiar to these objects. These words convey the idea; but how is that idea to be accurately stated? The adversary of the proposition may select, not only any hecceity, but may make any selections of hecceities he likes. Then a quality can be found which shall belong to all those hecceities, and it may be such that after it has been selected any hecceity chosen shall be without that quality, unless the hecceity is such that it possesses [del]even[/del] any quality that can be named unless some one of the first hecceities was without it. A collection is something which is in a triple relation to every hecceity with reference to a logical quality, there being two logical qualities, [u]inclusion[/u] and [u]exclusion[/u]; and every collection with the range of mathematical possibility has a quality which belongs to every hecceity the collection includes and to nothing which it excludes. But this is no more than saying that a quality is a relation of [del]everything to one[/del] [ins]every hecceity[/ins] something in a universe of two objects.
