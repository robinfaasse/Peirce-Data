I believe we can now formulate the rule of mathematical possibility. Let us say that the [del]subjecturiency[/del] [ins]valency[/ins] of an index is 0 if that index denotes a haecceity, is 1 if [del]the it[/del] it denotes a monadic quality, is two if it denotes a dyadic relation, is 3 if it denotes a triadic relation, etc. Then let the proposition whose truth [del]through[/del] [ins]in[/ins] the universe of mathematical possibility is to be tested to be so stated that I and T are everywhere substituted for their definitions while there are no other letters on the line except 0, U, q, r. If any other letter occurs it is to be got rid of by means of its definition, if its definition implies any proposition expressible in terms of I, T, 0, U, q and r. Otherwise, we substitute for it, say t_i, $\Sigma$_x q_xi; or say for t_ij, $\Sigma$_x r_xij etc. Then, if the last $\Sigma$ of the quantifier is not followed by more Ts than its subjecturiency, or if it can be separated into [del]terms[/del] [ins]aggregants[/ins] so that in no one aggregant there is a greater number, then the proposition is true, unless it reduces to [del]either[/del] zero [del]or one[/del] by the principle of contradiction or by means of the present rule. On the other hand, a proposition is false, if its negative is true. I am not quite sure this rule is entirely correct, but I believe it to be nearly so.
It must be added, however, that all haecceities are alike independently of their characters and consequently the truth of a proposition which begins with $\Sigma$_i is the same as if it began with $\Pi$_i. The same is true of all pairs of haecceities which are of the same form, either A:A or A:B, and so of higher sets.
