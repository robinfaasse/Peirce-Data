[del]Guessing[/del] We may be aided by previous knowledge in forming our hypotheses. In that case, they will not be pure guesses, but will be compounds of deductions from general rules we already know, applied to the facts under observation, for one ingredient, and pure guess for the other ingredient. Thus, suppose the [ins]surprising[/ins] facts which puzzle us are the actions of a certain man on a certain occasion, and [del]we our[/del] conjecture relates to the state of belief that caused such conduct. If we have no previous knowledge of the man, anyone state of belief that [del]would[/del] [ins]will[/ins] account for his conduct might be as good a guess as any other; but if we know that he is particularly inclined, or particularly disinclined, to extravagant beliefs, or to any other special limit of belief, we still have to guess; only we shall [del]make our[/del] select our guess from a smaller number of possible hypotheses.
