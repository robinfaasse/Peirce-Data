Guessing) We may be aided by previous knowledge in forming our
hypotheses. In that case, they will not be pure guesses, but will be
compounds of deductions from general rules we already know, applied
to the facts under observation, for one ingredient, and pure guess for the
other ingredients. Thus, suppose the [del]observed[/del] [ins]surprising[/ins] facts which puzzle us are
the actions of a certain man on a certain occasion, and [del]we[/del] our
conjecture relates to the state of belief that caused such conduct. If
we have no previous knowledge of the man, any one state of belief that
[fig]figure[/fig] [del][u]will[/u][/del] [ins]would[/ins] account for his conduct might be as good a guess as any other;
but if we know that he is particularly inclined, or particularly disinclined,
to extravagant beliefs, or to any other special kind of belief, we still
have a guess; only we shall [del]make our[/del] select our guess from a smaller
number of possible hypotheses.
