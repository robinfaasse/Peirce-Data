I fully agree with you that there should be different types for different forms of books. I should like to read Keat's "Odes" in light-faced and delicate letter. It harmonizes with the spirit of his thought and expression. For the same reason I like to read Old English authors in Black Letter. I like to read history, scientific [ins]/[/ins] work, and indeed all work that is addressed to the understanding, in strong solid plain letter. To my notion, the charm of a book is in the unobtrusiveness of its printing. When I observe only the thought of the author, and think nothing at all of the printer, and my eye is not diverted by any peculiarity of type[del],[/del][ins];[/ins]--either in the way of rudeness or of elegance--that to me is good printing. Unfortunately this does not seem to be the motive of the modern type-cutter. He is altogether too anxious to show you how truthfully he can make a curve, how sharp he can cut a line. He is not half so anxious to help you to read as he is to show his own skill. This makes the mechanical accuracy which you hate quite as cordially as I do.
