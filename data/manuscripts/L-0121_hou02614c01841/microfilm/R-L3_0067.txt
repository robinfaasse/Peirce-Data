I cannot trace with clearness, the changes in Arabic fig-
ures. Those made by the first printers are almost unreadable.
The figures that Caxton used in his monogram of "74" is unmeaning
to the modern reader, and for a hundred years after, all Arabic
figures were very irregular. Our books on typography skip this
subject entirely. The only information I find is that given by
Dr. Taylor in the "Alphabet," 2 vols. octavo, London, [date]1883[/date]. In
volume 2, page 266, he gives the XIIth and XIVth century forms. He
refers for successful English forms to Wattenbach, -- "Anleitung zur
laleonische Palaeographie, p.88; and by Friedlein, Gerbert, die
Geometrie des Boethius, und die indischen Ziffern, plate vi."

I do not share your preference for Baskerville's types. It
shows too much of his skill as a writing master; too much of hair
line, too much of delicacy. Beautiful, admirable no doubt, but
still bordering on a weakness that is almost effeminate. I pre-
fer the robust, thoroughly masculine style of the first Caslon, and
of the Frenchman, P. S. Fournier. They made types that were legi-
ble. They were not so anxious to show their skill in making true
curves and sharp hair lines, as they were to help the reader. In
other words, they wanted to help the reader, but not to show them-
selves.
