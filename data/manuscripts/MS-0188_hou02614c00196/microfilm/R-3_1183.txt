[u]Introduction[/u].

Practical Arithmetic, formerly called Augrim, with which the student is already acquainted, cannot be denied to be a branch of mathematics. But it is almost wholly [ins]a[/ins] mechanical art, and affords no conception of mathematical thought; while geometry, with which the present volume is [ins]mainly[/ins] occupied, is the most enchanting [del]part of the science[/del] [ins]among the domains of the Queen of the Sciences[/ins].

The business of the mathematician lies with exact ideas, or hypotheses, which he first frames, upon the suggestion of some practical problem, then traces out their consequences, and ultimately generalizes.

The advantages of the study consist, on the one hand, in its furnishing rules for answering a great many questions, especially such as relate to quantity, and, on the other hand, in its training the mind to abstract imagination, to accurate ratiocination, and to complicated generalization.
