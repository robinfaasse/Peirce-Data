[del]On the other hand,
The fact is that every such
belief is founded upon an induction,
and that every induction is an
inference from a sample to the 
whole lot from which it is drawn.
Now it is of the very essence
of an induction that it is
merely probable, and that its
degree of probability is always 
determined by the circumstances
under which the sample was drawn.
The character of the sample is
known; its resemblance to the 
whole lot is only probable. 
Probability is always relative 
to the information which we 
actually possess. That it may 
be snowing at this moment
in London is to me a probability
of a certain degree; it is to a
Londoner a certainty. What 
is probable, is that which is 
not known to be true, but which
is such that all that is known
is consistent with its being 
true, and furthermore is such 
as to incline the mind to think
it true.
Probable belief is not then 
strictly speaking knowledge;
it is only the best substitute 
for knowledge which under the 
circumstances we can get. 
But to believe a thing to be
true, in the ordinary sense
of the word, is to believe that 
it is true and that we 
know it to be true. If we 
really believe it, we don't
stop to talk about its probability,
but we treat it as a certainty.
Is there then any such 
thing as a rational belief 
of an inductive kind? It 
seems to me that if we define
belief as a frame of mind
equivalent to knowledge, there 
is not. If I have reason
to think that a certain event
has happened or will happen, 
but if I also know that there
is a possibility of my being
mistaken, then my mind is 
divided, and I cannot be
said to believe in the full 
sense of the word. In fact 
a wise man will not believe
anything which he does not
know to be true. He will 
admit that some things are 
more probable than others, 
but he will never take 
any of these probable things 
for certainties. He will 
act upon them, because he
must act, and they are the 
best guides he has, but 
he will always remember 
that he may be wrong.
The word belief is indeed 
often used in a looser sense.
We say that we believe a 
thing when we only mean 
that we think it more 
probable than not. But if 
this is all we mean, then
belief is only a name for
an estimate of probability,
and it has no special 
rational character of its 
own. The rational process 
is the induction itself, which 
leads to the estimate; the 
belief is only the state 
of mind which results.
If this is so, then the 
attempt to base philosophy
on a system of universal 
and necessary beliefs is 
doomed to failure. For no 
belief can be both universal 
and rational unless it 
expresses a truth which is 
already known to be true; 
and if it expresses such 
a truth, then it is not 
a belief at all, but a 
piece of knowledge. Philosophy
can only be founded on 
knowledge; and knowledge
must begin with the 
recognition of facts.
It would seem then that 
there is no place for 
belief in a rational system 
of thought. Everything must 
be either known or 
estimated at its true 
probability. But the history
of human thought shows 
that men have always 
felt the need of something 
more than this. They have
always craved for a 
certainty which should 
go beyond the limits of 
what can be strictly 
proved. This craving is 
the source of all religions 
and of most philosophies.
It is the desire to 
find a meaning in 
the world which shall 
be more than a mere 
calculus of chances.
The question is whether 
this craving is itself 
rational, or whether it 
is merely a human 
weakness which we 
must learn to overcome.
If we say it is rational, 
we must find some 
ground for it which 
is not inductive. We 
must find some source 
of knowledge which is 
not derived from 
experience. This is 
the path of the 
transcendentalist. If we 
say it is a weakness, 
then we must be 
content with the 
limited and partial 
certainties which 
science can give us.
This is the path of 
the empiricist.
The choice between these 
two paths is the 
fundamental choice 
of philosophy. Each 
has its own difficulties 
and its own rewards.
The transcendentalist 
promises us a 
certainty which is 
full and satisfying, 
but he often fails 
to show how his 
conclusions are 
connected with the 
facts of experience.
The empiricist gives us 
a method which is 
solid and reliable, 
but he leaves us 
in a world which 
is cold and 
indifferent to our 
deepest needs.
Perhaps the truth 
is that both are 
necessary. Perhaps 
we must learn to 
live with the 
tension between our 
craving for certainty 
and our knowledge 
of our own 
limitations. Perhaps 
belief is not something 
which we can 
either justify or 
reject by a 
simple logical 
process, but something 
which grows out 
of the whole 
complexity of our 
life and experience.
However this may be, 
it is clear that 
the analysis of 
belief is one of 
the most important 
and difficult tasks 
of philosophy. It 
requires a careful 
examination of the 
nature of evidence, 
the structure of 
inference, and the 
psychology of 
the human mind. 
It is a task 
which is never 
finished, because 
every new discovery 
in science or 
history brings 
with it new 
questions and 
new challenges to 
our established 
ways of thinking.[/del]
