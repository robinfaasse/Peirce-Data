[u]Nº 8.[/u] If you are to are to draw as [del]impassible[/del] [ins]sharp[/ins] a line between signs of the existence of relations and signs of functions, your formulae do very well. [del]Perhaps[/del] I should express them thus
If $x \odot \overline{a}$ then either $x \odot z$ or else $x \odot a$ is false.
If $a \odot \overline{y}$ then either $1 \odot y$ or else $a \odot y$ is false.

But I do not think an algebra ought to have any sign that is exclusively propositional or of the nature of a copula. The mere fact that the expression is written down is all the copula needed. [del]The writing down of any expression, $m$, ought to assert a corresponding proposition.[/del] Such expressions as $(\odot 6) \odot a$ ought to have a meaning. I should therefore make $a$ equivalent to $1 \odot a$ and $\overline{a}$ to $a \odot Z$. Then $a \odot b$, or $a \odot b$ is false, would be equivalent to $(a \odot b) \odot Z$. But you will say that this is not pure mathematics. I doubt it; I should not have "false" mean anything but that one is not permitted by the rules to write it. Mathematics, however pure, must recognize that.

Milford, Pa. [date]1904 April 7[/date] V₂

My dear Dr. Huntington:

Your MS. only reached me last night, and I am sorry to have to report that when I first came to look at it this morning, [del]the step not being out of my brain, I stupidly made[/del] some blundering pencillings on the margin. I can only beg pardon, the circumstance chagrins me not a little.

My pencillings on pp. 1 and 5 are well enough.

To those on p. 2, it is to be added that while the conception of a class is mere lumber to jostle against in the dark unless one is to deal with what is outside a class as well as with what is within it, yet a complete algebra even of non-relative logic does require the recognition of one great distinction among "entities", namely, between those that are no more than creations of thought and those that may be involved in experience; or in your notation (only putting $x \odot y$ in place of your $x \odot y$, which [del]will lead to confusion[/del] [ins]is known to lead to it[/ins],) those [del]such as $x \odot y$[/del] which you will call propositions, [del]and those such as $y$ for each of which there is no such knowledge[/del], the [del]unexperiencer[/del] [ins]unexperienced[/ins]. Even in the purest mathematical development something like this distinction must appear.
