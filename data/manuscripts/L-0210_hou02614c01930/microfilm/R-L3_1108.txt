35 Fairfax Hall,
Cambridge, Mass.
February 7, 1904.

My dear Mr. Peirce, --

I have only recently got around to the detailed examination of the proof of the distributive law in the algebra of logic, which you were good enough to send me a few weeks ago, and I want to thank you again for your kindness, which will prove of great value to me.

You had stated the references for each step of the proof so carefully that I had no difficulty in following the argument at the first reading. There is no doubt in my mind that the demonstration is perfectly complete, if one grants the necessary assumptions from the "algebra of the copula", namely:

A) if a < b is false, then there is an element x, not 0, such that
x < a and x < b; and
_   _
B) if a < b, then inversely b < a.

Of these two assumptions, the first one, (A), is the only one which is essential, as the other can be readily derived from it.

I have not had a chance to read your chapter on the algebra of the copula, to track up this assumption to its source; what interests me especially at the moment is the discovery that, in my list of postulates for the algebra of logic, [del]ixfxxxxxxxi[/del] I can substitute this assumption (A) in place of (a special case of) the distributive law, and thus simplify the list decidedly. With your permission, I should like very much to [del]the[/del] utilize your demonstration in my paper, which I am just writing out for the Transactions -- making profound acknowledgment of my indebtedness, of course, and perhaps adding some note on the history of the theorem. As is usual in such controversies, it seems that both parties -- yourself and Professor Schroeder -- were quite correct. May I ask you to glance over the preliminary draft of the introduction to my paper, which I enclose with this, especially p.5, and tell me whether you have any objection to this form of publication of your result.

In my ignorance of the literature, I should like to ask you also whether any other names than those of Boole, yourself, and Schroeder need be mentioned in my introductory lines.

Thanking you again for your very great kindness in sending me your demonstration, and trusting that you will permit [ins]me[/ins] to make use of it,

Believe me,

Very sincerely yours,
Edward V. Huntington.

To Professor Charles S. Peirce,
Milford, Pa.
