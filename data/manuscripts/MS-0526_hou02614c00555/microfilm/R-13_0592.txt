[u]Logic of Relatives[/u] No 2

A pronoun, according to ancient grammarians, is a word used in place of a noun. Their medieval successors, especially Duns Scotus, saw that, on the contrary, the personal and demonstrative pronouns indicate things [u]directly[/u], while common nouns do so only indirectly, by means of descriptions applicable to the things intended; so that a noun may rather be said to be a word which, by the aid of a description, imperfectly fulfils the office of a demonstrative pronoun, namely to directly indicate objects of an experience common to speaker and listener. When this had been pointed out, the definition of the pronoun was changed accordingly; and for three hundred years was correctly stated in all Latin grammars. Then came the revival of learning and the reformation, and the followers of Scotus, the Dunses as they were called, became objects at first of detestation and then of contempt; and the grammarians took vengeance upon them by refusing to recognize [del]the[/del] truth that they had brought to light. So, the [del]the[/del] ancient definition [del]has[/del] was revived; and it is only of late years that it is getting again giving way to Scotism and good sense.

A relative pronoun indicates a thing indirectly, yet without [ins]directly[/ins] resorting to a description, by directly indicating some other pronoun or noun which indicates the same.
