[u]A plane triangle[/u] is a figure formed by any three points, called its [del]vertices[/del] [ins]called its enclosure[/ins], being one of the four regions of points, into which the three rays through pairs of these points separate the points of their plane.

The [del]region of rays[/del] [ins]exclosure[/ins] of a [ins]plane[/ins] triangle is the multitude of rays in its plane which do not pass through [del]its enclosure[/del] [ins]any point of its enclosure[/ins].

The external angles of a [ins]plane[/ins] triangle are those [ins]plane[/ins] angles at the vertices [ins]between its rays[/ins] which do not sweep over [del]its[/del] [ins]any point of[/ins] [ins]its[/ins] enclosure.

The [del]external[/del] [ins]exclosure[/ins] sides of a [ins]plane[/ins] triangle are those lengths along the rays between the vertices which do not stretch [del]through[/del] [ins]across[/ins] any ray of its exclosure.

The internal angles of a [ins]plane[/ins] triangle are those [ins]plane[/ins] angles at the vertices from one ray of the triangle to another each of which sweeps over [ins]all the points of[/ins] the enclosure of the triangle.

The interior sides of a [ins]plane[/ins] triangle are these lengths along the rays [del]of the triangle[/del] [ins]on the rays of[/ins] the exclosure of the triangle from one vertex to another each of which stretches across [del]the exclosure of the triangle[/del]. (They are called interior because they form a [del]perissid[/del] line which does not separate the planes into two regions but lies within the one region.)
