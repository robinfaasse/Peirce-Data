the grammatical meanings of the same words. A fact
may concern a number of subjects. Thus, if we say "John
loves Jane," John and Jane are equally subjects.

If two subjects are selected in different ways it makes
a great difference which is selected first.

Thus if we say "Every house has a tenant," we may
precisely express this as follows: Take any object, A, no
matter what; then an object, B, may be found, such
that, either A is not a house or [del]B[/del] it has B as tenant.
That is, B, not being selected until after A has been
selected, may be taken accordingly, so as to make the
proposition true. [del]But to say[/del] To reverse the [ins]order of[/ins] selection
would be to assert much more. We can [del]take[/del] [ins]find[/ins] an object,
B, such that, no matter what object A be taken, either
A is not a house, or it has B for a tenant. That would
say that all houses have one and the same tenant.

We should make it a rule, whenever we have to
enunciate a proposition about a number of sub-
jects which are differently selected, to take pains so
to express ourselves that there can be no mistake as to
the order in which the different subjects are to be selected.
