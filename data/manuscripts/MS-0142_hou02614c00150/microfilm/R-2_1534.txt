[u]NOTES ON TOPICAL GEOMETRY.[/u]

§1. [u]Of Multitude[/u].

Given a universe of reality or fiction and a general description, there may be [del]something[/del] [ins]some[/ins] in that universe that answers that description. There may be some reason or explanation of its occurrence there; but that reason does not constitute its occurrence, for in that case it would not be a thing. Its occurrence is an arbitrary act,—perhaps a rational one, but still, as act, arbitrary. An arbitrary act is [del]ipso facto[/del] [u]hic et nunc[/u], and cannot be generalized without losing its essential character as an act. The object that occurs is therefore not a mere specification of the general description; for that would always leave it general. It is a unit in virtue of its arbitrary occurrence. [del]The same[/del] [ins]A like[/ins] arbitrary action may make [del]objects[/del] [ins]things[/ins] of quite different descriptions [del]the same[/del] identical, as when a thing undergoes change. It may also make things entirely alike different from one another. They must, indeed, be in different relations; but these relations are themselves reactions involving arbitrariness. Different things being, arbitrary occurrences, occur independently of one another. That is, the destruction of [del]one[/del] [ins]some[/ins] does not [u]ipso facto[/u] constitute the destruction of any other.

A collection is not a thing, but an [u]ens rationis[/u], since its identity is constituted by the identities of other things, namely, of the things that it contains as members. At the same time, it partakes of the nature of a thing, because being constituted by things, and not by any general description, it has a numerical identity, like a thing; and one collection may be exactly like another except in its arbitrary reactions.
