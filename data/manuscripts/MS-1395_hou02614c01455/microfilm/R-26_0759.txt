a surprisingly English character.

The temper of the man was admirable. He never
made one of those reclamations of priority into which sci-
entific vanity is so often betrayed; but many times
published notes to show that his own results were not
so new as he and the scientific public had believed
them to be. He did much to bring into notice the works of
other physicists, among them the Americans, Rowland
and Root. He found himself several times engaged in
controversies with redoubtable antagonists, such as
Clausius, Bertrand, and perhaps one may add Land.
He always so conducted these as to show that the desire
to know the truth and to publish it was his sole
motive; and he avoided every approach to perso-
nality as he would a pestilential infection.
