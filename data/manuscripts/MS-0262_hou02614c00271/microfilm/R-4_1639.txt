ON THE REAL QUALITATIVE CHARACTERS OF PLANE CURVES. BY C. S. PEIRCE.

I consider a plane having only real points and lines, being persuaded that imaginary algebra
and geometry are merely special varieties of continuity, and not the general case. I confine my-
self, at first, to unicursal curves. By a unicursal curve I mean one of which any two different
elements are described, the one earlier the other later. Between an earlier and a later element
an intermediate element can always be found, later than the earlier and earlier than the later.
It might seem, at first sight, as if this condition sufficed to define the continuity of the curve
because in case of a jump the last element of the earlier branch and the first element of the la-
ter would be two different and simultaneous elements, which would be contrary to the hypothesis.
But there need not be any assignable last element of the earlier branch (as in the case of a spi-
ral) nor first element of the later branch, so that in order to define continuity, we find our-
selves forced to introduce the idea of "between" not merely in the time of the process of descrip-
tion of the curve, but also, in some form, in the extension of space. As another example of the
necessity of this, consider that there is nothing contradictory in the supposition that a point
passes an infinity of times round one oval and after that an infinity of times round another and
remote oval, without there being any assignable instant at which the leap from one to the other
took place. But we can restrict our curve to being continuous by the following condition. Certain
elements of the curve can be found such that every one of them but the first is the next one of
them after another of them and every one of them but the last is the [del]is the[/del] next preceding some
one of them, and every other element of the curve is [ins]successive ones[/ins] between some two of these in the time of de-
scription and also in position in space. If the curve is self-returning, the generating tangent
and point moves round and round the oval, so that every element is after every element, but the
order of three, as A-B-C, not C-B-A, is determined by taking "after" to mean "after in any one
revolution." To determine whether an element is between two others in space reference can be had
to any system of coordinates, by which the question is reduced to whether a point is between two
others on a fixed line drawn in a determinate direction
