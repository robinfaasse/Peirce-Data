Although metrical characters are not to be considered in this paper, it will be indispensible
to distinguish between the finite and infinitesimal distance of points and inclination of lines.
An infinite distance between points is projectively indistinguishable from an infinitesimal dis-
tance. We must also distinguish between positive and negative directions.

The element of a curve is a tangent with the incident point upon it. There are five ways in
which two such elements may be related, viz.: 1st, the point of neither is on the line of the o-
ther; 2nd, the point of one lies in the line of the other, in which case I say that the former
"rakes" the latter; 3rd, the points of the two coincide in a binode; 4th, the lines of the two
coincide in a bitangent; 5th, the two completely coincide. The first relation is the usual one.
The second is a relation between two finite parts or the whole of the curve. The third and fourth
may be called positional singularities, because definable by the relative position of two elements.
The fifth may be regarded as a compound of a binode and a bitangent infinitely near to one another
In the generation of the curve by the moving element, there is a positive direction along the line
of the element in which it never ceases to move, and a positive direction round the point of the
element in which it never ceases to turn. A cusp may be regarded as an infinitesimal loop, where
the point of the generating element becomes stationary while the line turns completely round it,
