ON THE REAL QUALITATIVE CHARACTERS OF PLANE CURVES. BY C. S. Peirce.

I consider a plane having only real points and lines, being privately persuaded that imaginary algebra and geometry deal with merely special forms of continuity, which are not entitled to the preeminent position now accorded to them. I confine myself to unicursal self-returning curves. A unicursal curve is one which has these three characters: 1st, it can be so generated that of any two elements one shall be earlier the other later; 2nd, between an earlier and a later element a third can always be found later than the earlier and earlier than the later; 3rd, if the curve conceived as separated into two parts, one described earlier the other later, there is a [del]las[/del] last element of the earlier part and a first element of the later part. The last condition [del]exclude[/del] excludes, for example, taking a complete spiral as a part of a unicursal curve, since a spiral has no determinate tangent at the inner end and no determinate point of incidence at the outer end. A self-returning curve has every element later than every element, but in applying the definition of a unicursal curve we must understand earlier and later to mean earlier and later in any one revolution round the self-returning curve.

By the element of the curve, I mean the tangent with the point of incidence. There are five ways in which two such elements may be related, viz.: The point of neither is in the line of the other; 2nd, the point of one lies in the line of the other, in which case I say that the point enfilades the line, or rather, that the [del]element[/del] [ins]element[/ins] to which the point belongs enfilades the element to which the line belongs. [I am not very well satisfied with this terminology.]; 3rd, the points of the two elements coincide in a binode; 4th, the lines of the two elements coincide in a bitangent; 5th, the two elements completely coincide. The last case constitutes a higher singularity which I shall not consider.

When a branch of the curve passes through a binode or bitangent, we have to distinguish between the two sides of the other branch, the outer or convex, and the inner or concave side. An element on the outside of a binode enfilades [ins]elements[/ins] [del]two points[/del] more than an element on the inside; an element on the outside of a bitangent is enfiladed by two poin[ins]t[/ins] elements less than an element on the inside. And the two elements enfiladed in the case of the binode and enfilading in the case of the bitangent lie on opposite sides of the singularity.
