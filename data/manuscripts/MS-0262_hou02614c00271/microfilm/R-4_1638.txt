ON THE REAL QUALITATIVE CHARACTERS OF PLANE CURVES. BY C. S. PEIRCE.

I consider a plane having only real points and lines, being persuaded that imaginary algebra
and geometry deal with merely special varieties of continuity and not with the truly general case.
By a unicursal curve I mean one in which of any two different elements one is earlier and the
other later, in any determinate direction of describing it. Between an earlier and a later element
a third can always be found later than the earlier and earlier than the later. It might seem,
at first sight, as if this condition sufficed to define the continuity of the curve, because, in
case of a jump, the last element of the earlier branch and the first element of the later one
would be simultaneous and yet different, contrary to hypothesis. But the difficulty is that there
need not be any assignable last element of the earlier nor first element of the later branch.
Consider, for example, a spiral generated by an inward motion; its last point is determinate, but
any line drawn through that point is a tangent, and no one can be named as the last. Again, there
is nothing contradictory in the supposition that a point passed an infinity of times round one
oval, and that it is afterward found to have passed an infinity of times round a remote oval,
without any possibility of saying that the leap from one oval to the other took place at one point
rather than another. In such a case, there is no last nor first. This is a point not at all clear
to ordinary sensuous reasoning; but the study of it by the logic of relatives enables me to speak
with certainty about it. It appears, then, that the continuity of a curve can only be secured by
reference to forms assumed to be continuous.
