[u]Notes on B. Peirce's Linear Associative Algebra.[/u]
By C. S. Peirce.

§ 1. [del]The[/del] [ins]This[/ins] definition of mathematics was entirely novel in [date]1870[/date], when it was first put forth. Since then all important writers on the foundations of mathematics have been led to similar definitions, no doubt more or less influenced, directly or indirectly, by Peirce. After broad and deep study of the question, I am definitively convinced that the definition here given is better than any of the modifications of it. The chief points to be considered are as follows:

1[ins]st[/ins], Is not the definition too broad because mathematics is limited to deductions from a certain kind of [del]premises[/del] hypotheses; namely, from such as are perfectly definite and somewhat complicated?

2[ins]d[/ins], Is not the definition too broad, because mathematical reasoning is schematic while philosophical deductions deal with pure concepts?

3[ins]d[/ins], Is not the definition too broad in that all sciences draw necessary conclusions? Should we not say that mathematics is the logic of necessary inference?

4[ins]th[/ins], Is not the definition too narrow in that some of the highest achievements of mathematics have consisted in the formation of mathematical hypotheses?
