[u]Chapter I[/u]

[u]One, Two, and Three[/u].

Logic must begin with analyzing the meanings of certain words, which we shall take up in due order.

The first of these is the word '[u]is[/u]', as when we say, Julius Caesar [u]is[/u] dead, a griffin [u]is[/u] a fabulous animal, a four-sided triangle [u]is[/u] an absurdity, height [u]is[/u] the distance from the ground, nothing is that which does not exist. These examples suffice to show that we apply this word to whatever we give a name to, whether it really exists or not, or whether we consider it as existing or not.

The word [u]is[/u] is called by logicians the [u]copula[/u] because it joins subject and predicate. That which is, in the sense of the copula, was termed [u]ens[/u] (pl: [del]entia[/del] [ins]entia[/ins]) by the schoolmen, and the corresponding abstract noun used was [u]entitas[/u]. In this as in many other cases, we have taken in English, the abstract noun in [del]the[/del] a concrete sense, and we can consequently speak of entities.
