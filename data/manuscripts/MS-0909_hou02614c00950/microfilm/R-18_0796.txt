The next kind of mental synthesis to be explained, is our attributing different predicates to the same subject, or finding that certain feelings, which have no internal affinity, nevertheless belong together in fact. I suppose that the link, in such synthesis is the movement of nervous energy. For it is such a movement which conveys the sense of reality, and when we find ourselves compelled to think that two ideas are connected in reality, it would seem that it must be because one is replaced after a commotion of sense by the other.

Finally, the synthetic consciousness, or consciousness of learning, the most important faculty of the mind, is to be explained by the most characteristic property of the nervous system, its power of taking habits.

Thus, the three fundamental kinds of consciousness, simple consciousness, dual consciousness and synthetic consciousness, are to be explained by the three chief functions of the nervous system, its simple irritability, the movement of nervous energy, and the synthetic action of the nerves, especially habit.

The essentials of the power of taking habits are these. First, over whatever path a nervecell has discharged itself, it is somewhat more likely to discharge itself a second time. If this rule had the absolute inviolability of a physical law, if it were that over the path along which a nerve-cell had once discharged itself, it must forever discharge itself, there would be no room for the gradual development of habit, and mediation or thirdness, which is the essence of mind, would have no place in the universe. But while mechanical laws have nothing to do with probabilities or tendencies, it is the slight tendency to act in a given way that is peculiarly characteristic of a physiological principle. I do not now stop to ask how this peculiarity is to be explained; but I wish to draw attention to its all-important consequences. The second essential part of the power of taking habits, is forgetfulness or the tendency of old habits, old tendencies to discharge energy over particular paths, to become obliterated. [del]Let us see what is the necessary result of the action of these two laws or principles.[/del] The third essential ingredient of the same power is the tendency to fatigue, which, as it seems to me, physiologists have [del]hitherto[/del] [ins]sometimes[/ins] failed correctly to define. There is, no doubt, a total exhaustion of a nerve-cell, when it refuses to respond to any stimulus whatever; but the primary and more important effect of fatigue is that a cell ceases to discharge itself in one channel, and begins to discharge itself with a certain energy in another. It necessarily results from the action of these three principles, that the actions of the nervous system are directed toward ends, which is the [del]unique[/del] [ins]essential[/ins] property of mind.
