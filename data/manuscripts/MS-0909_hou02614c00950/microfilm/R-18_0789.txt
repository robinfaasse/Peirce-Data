If we compare these thirds degenerate in the first degree with genuine thirds, we are struck with their mechanical character. Genuine thirdness is rational. Thus, the difference between the genie's son being accidentally hit and being aimed at lies in the intention of the action. [del]Consider[/del] Consider a case in which there is genuine thirdness, independent of our thought. Thus, two forces are combined according to the parallelogram of forces. Now by the parallelogram of forces, any force may be resolved into any three directions, the components being creations of the mind. But when two real forces are combined the difference is that these have a real existence in the laws of nature. The real thirdness lies then in nature's having a rational [ins]or intelligible[/ins] constitution. The degenerate secondness is that which lies merely in the mind but not in the thing; that is very different from the real intelligibility which constitutes the genuine thirdness. Diamond and corundum both scratch glass; they are thus made alike by a fact which presents itself as if it were ultimate and unintelligible. But let this be explained, and brought under any law of nature, and the statement that one scratches glass will virtually involve the other's doing so, and in so far the thirdness will be made genuine. Mercury and Venus are alike in revolving round the sun. As far as this depends on the two individual facts that both are small bodies near the sun, it is accidental; but that once granted, the motion is the result of a universal law, which would not have the character it has if only some bodies were [del]influenced by it.[/del] [ins]under its influence.[/ins] [del]The[/del] [ins]This[/ins] observation does not [ins]however[/ins] appear to be true with regard to the reflective thirds; and I must leave that as a point requiring further elucidation.

Of thirds degenerate in the second degree, the following are the principal types. First there may be three separate things having relations of reason between them. The dramatist Marlow had something of that character of diction in which Shakespeare and Bacon agree. This is a trivial example of one of the most important varieties of thirds. In natural history intermediate types serve to bring out the resemblance between forms whose similarity might otherwise escape attention, or not be duly appreciated. In portraiture, photographs mediate between the original and the likeness. In science, a diagram or analogue of the observed fact leads on to a further analogy. The relations of reason need not be all resemblances. Thus, we may have use for such a remark as that Washington was eminently free from the faults in which most great soldiers resemble one another. Or all three relations may be comparisons as a centaur is a mixture of a man and a horse. Philadelphia lies between Washington and New York.
