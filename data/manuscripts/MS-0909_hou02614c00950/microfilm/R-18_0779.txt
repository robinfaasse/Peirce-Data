[u]CHAPTER I. TRICHOTOMY.[/u]

Perhaps it might be well to begin by noticing how different numbers have found their cham-
pions. Two was extolled by Peter Ramus, Four by Pythagoras, Five by Sir Thomas Browne, and so on.
For my part, I am a determined enemy of no innocent number ; I consider them all excellent in the
their way, but I am forced to confess to finding the number three more useful in philosophy than
the others. Indeed, I make so much use of three-fold divisions in my speculations [ins]that it seems bes[/ins]
best to commence by making a slight preliminary study of the conceptions upon which all such di-
visions must rest. I mean no more than the ideas of First, Second, and Third, - ideas so broad that
they may be looked upon rather as moods or tones of thought, rather than determinate notions, but
which have great significance for all that. Viewed as numerals, to be applied to what objects we
like, they are indeed thin skeletons of thought, if not mere words. If we only wanted to make enu-
merations, it would be out of place to ask for the significance of the numbers we should have to
use ; but then the distinctions of philosophy are supposed to attempt something far more than
that ; they are intended to go down to the very essence of things, and if we are to make one sin-
gle three-fold [del]philosophical[/del] [ins]philosophical[/ins] distinction, it behooves us to ask beforehand what are the kinds of
objects that are first, second, and third, not merely as being so counted, but in their own true
characters. That there are such ideas of the really First, Second, and Third, we shall presently
find reason to admit.

The First is that whose being is simply in itself, not referring [ins]to anything[/ins] [del]nor lying[/del] behind anything.
The Second is that which is what it is by force of something to which it is second. The third is
that which is what it is owing to things between which it mediates and which it brings into relati
relation with one another.

The idea of the absolutely First must be entirely separated from all conception of or referen
reference to to anything else ; for what involves a second is itself a second to that second.
The First must therefore be present and immediate, otherwise it would be second to a representa-
tion. It must be fresh and new, for if old it is second to its former state. It must be initiative
original, spontaneous, and free ; otherwise it is second to a determining cause. It is also some-
thing vivid and conscious ; it must be so to avoid being object or second of some feeling or
phenomenon. It precedes all synthesis and all differentiation : it has no unity and no parts. It
cannot be articulately thought ; assert it, and it has already lost its characteristic innocence
