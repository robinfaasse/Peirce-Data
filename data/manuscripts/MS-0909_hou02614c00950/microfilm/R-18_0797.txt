I remark that the biologists themselves have little sympathy for such an attempt to put their doctrine in an abstract undress. The whole tendency of their mental organization is toward detail; and they seem to think that there is error in analysis. But this must not deter us; we have to remember that the Newtonian philosophy, upon which all modern science is founded consists in substituting for the infinitely complicated problems of nature, simpler ones resembling those as nearly as may be, which are within the grasp of the mind. As we are accustomed, in analytical mechanics, to leave out of account various considerations without which it would be utterly impossible to gain any conception of the reasons of the phenomena presented to our observation, so I propose to state what appears to be the most essential elements of the theory of natural selection in as diagrammatic a form as possible, neglecting altogether a multitude of circumstances which however important they may be as modifying the application of the principle of natural selection do not seem to form any part of that principle.

The doctrine of natural selection is a theory proposed to show how the action of chance can lead to [del]lead to[/del] adaptations, or say generally, can lead in any determinate direction. Imagine a million persons each with a dollar in his pocket sit down to play at any fair game of chance, each betting a dollar on each risk. They are supposed to make their bets independently of one another, and at the very first throw of the dice one half of them will lose their dollars and will go out of the game. Those that remain will have each two dollars.
