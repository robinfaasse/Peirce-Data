In all these cases, the secondness lies not in the fact, but in the mind. In the case of the rela-
tions [ins]of reason[/ins], the mind makes the synthesis of the two objects; in the relations of identity, the mind
sets the thing over against itself. All the degenerate seconds may well be called internal seconds,
in opposition to the external seconds which are constituted by external fact, and are true actions
of one thing upon another.

Among thirds, there are two degrees of degeneracy. The first is where there is in the fact
itself no thirdness or mediation, but where there is true duality; the second degree is where the
there is not even true secondness in the fact itself. Each of these degrees of degeneracy is found
in many different forms.

Of thirds degenerate in the first degree, I may first notice that of an object in two real
relations to two others. A pin fastens two things together, by sticking through one and also [del]throu[/del]
through the other. Either might be annihilated and the pin would continue to stick through the
one which remained. A mixture brings its ingredients together by containing each. We may term
these accidental thirds. "How did I slay thy son? asked the merchant, and the genie replied, "When
thou threwest away the date-stone, it smote my son who was passing at the time, on the breast,
and he died forthright." Here there were two independent facts, first that the merchant threw
away the date-stone, and second that the date-stone struck the genie's son. Had the date-stone
been aimed at the genie's son, the case would be different; there would have been a relation
of aiming which essentially supposes the aimer, the thing aimed, and the object aimed at. Even
without evil intention, a man may be responsible for the immediate effects of his actions; but
these are those in which the effect follows the cause not in a sporadic case here and there, but
in the usual [del]or frequent[/del] [ins]regular[/ins] course of nature. The three things are then brought together by the law
of nature, and not merely because there is something that suggests putting them together. But even
where there is no responsibility, there is a real connection of cause and effect.

An[del]$$$$$$$$$$$$$$$$$[/del] [ins]example of another family[/ins] of thirds degenerate in the first degree
is that of a man looking at himself in a looking-glass. Here, if we regard the light as a third
object besides the man and the mirror, no doubt there is genuine thirdness. But leaving that out
of account, the only fact is that the man and the glass are in a somewhat complicated dual relation
to one another. Namely the glass imparts to the man the temporary power of self-vision. We may
call this a case of a reflective third. Any dual relation may be so regarded as involving a third.
If A loves B, then by the medium of B, A is a co-lover with himself. The difference is that in
some cases, this way of regarding the matter leads to some convenient classification of facts,
and in other cases not.

Very much of the same sort are cases where a resemblance or other relation of reason between
first and second are constituted by their real relations to a third.
