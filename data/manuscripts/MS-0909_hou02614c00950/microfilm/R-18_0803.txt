CHAPTER I. TRICHOTOMY. In these guesses at the nature of things, I make [del]so[/del] much use of three-fold divisions, that it will be useful [ins]to[/ins] make a little preliminary study of the conceptions upon which all such divisions must rest. I mean no more than the ideas of First, Second, and Third,--ideas so broad that they may almost be considered as moods or tones of thought, rather than determinate notions, but which have great significance for all that. Viewed as mere numerals, to be applied to what objects we like, they are indeed thin skeletons of thought, if not mere words. If we only wanted to make enumerations, it would be out of place to ask for the significance of the numbers we should have to use; but the distinctions of philosophy are supposed to attempt something far more than enumeration; they are [del]supposed[/del] [ins]intended[/ins] to go down to the very essence of things, and if we are to make a single three-fold distinction, it behooves to ask beforehand, what are the kinds of [del]obje[/del] objects that are first, second, and third, not merely as being so counted, but in their own true characters. That there are such ideas of the really First, Second, and Third, we shall presently see.

The First is that whose being is [del]right[/del] [ins]simply[/ins] in itself, not referring to anything nor [ins]lying[/ins] behind anything. The Second is that which is what it is by force of something to which it is second. The Third is that which is what it is owing to things between which it mediates and which it brings into relation.

The idea of the absolutely first must be entirely separated from all idea [ins]of[/ins] or reference to anything else; for what involves a second is itself a second to that second. The First must [del]then[/del] therefore be present and immediate, otherwise it would be second to a representation. It must be fresh and new, for if old it is second to its former state. It must be initiative, original, spontaneous, and free; otherwise it is second to a determining cause. It is also something vivid and conscious; otherwise it would have to be thought as object or second of some feeling or phenomenon. It precedes all synthesis and all differentiation. It has no unity and no parts. It cannot be articulately thought. Assert it, and it has already lost its characteristic innocence; for assertion always implies a denial of something else. Stop to think of it, and it has flown. What the world was to Adam on the day he opened his eyes to it, before he had drawn any distinctions, or had become conscious of his own existence,--that is first, present, immediate, fresh, new, initiative, original, spontaneous, free, vivid, conscious, and eva[ins]ne[/ins]scent. Only, remember that every description of it must be false to it.

Just as the first is not absolutely first if thought along with a second, so likewise to [del]thin[/del] think the Second we must banish every third. The Second is, therefore, the absolute last. But we need not, and must not, banish the idea of the first from the second; on the contrary, the Second is precisely that which cannot be without the first.
