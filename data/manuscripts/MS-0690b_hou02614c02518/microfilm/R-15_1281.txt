I must now take leave of this problem, because I am desir-
ous of illustrating the application of my method to a case in which
all the evidence in our possession is extremely insecure. Nothing
could fulfill this condition more perfectly than the facts in the
life of Pythagoras, concerning whom almost all our information is
derived from three writers living about five hundred years later, of
whom two are known to be about as careless as any in antiquity,
while the third and principal witness is a superstitious romancer
whose supernatural narratives are given as simple historical truth.
Moreover, as far as we can descry, there is no possibility of testing
any hypothesis. In such a case, we must renounce at the outset
any attempt to reach anything approaching to certainty. Yet we are
not to aim at verisimilitudes, which are merits in romances, but not
in scientific results. What, then, are we to do? [ins]We are to embody (to say)[/ins]
or rather, to ensoul, all the pertinent facts, that is, [ins]<sub>^</sub>[/ins] the facts
[u]that those writers make those statements[/u], in such a hypothesis as
best unifies them, and will serve as a source of experiential pre-
dictions, whenever, in the future, it may be in our power to verify
or refute any predictions on the subject.
