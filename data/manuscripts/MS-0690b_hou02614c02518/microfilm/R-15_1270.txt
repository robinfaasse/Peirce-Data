What he does is fully to admit the justice of the objections, to
intimate that they are not new to him, and to go on to certain Hegel-
ish reflections. That intimation that he knew already that the
doctrine of ideas was untenable is one which, veracity apart, I do
not believe that Plato would have dared to make if he had not already
dropped hints sufficient to make it highly credible. If it was
credible to his disciples, it ought to be believed by us. If we
believe it, while we must recognize that a philosopher ought to
hesitate long before making such a recantation public, yet it becomes
very likely that a large part of the Parmenides was already written
before Aristotle joined the school. Now in stylometric inquiries,
what we are investigating is the time at which the body of the text
was written, not the time in which it was put into shape. But how
does Plato, under the limitations of impersonality which the form of
the Socratic dialogue imposed upon him, contrive to convey that in-
timation that he knew of those objections beforehand? He has to
introduce himself into the dialogue. But not in his own person.
It would be a great collapse of his dignity to do that. He in-
geniously appears as Parmenides. But how is it to be hinted that
the Parmenides is himself? Hinted I say; for it must be no more
than a hint. Parmenides must be made to teach, not his known his-
torical doctrines, but those of Plato which were allied to them; and
