The main events of Plato's life are little disputed. He
sprang from a highly aristocratic stock, and though he did not med-
dle with Athenian politics, he was all his life one of the most im-
portant personages of the Greek world. Aristotle tells us that
even before joining the company of Socrates he had been influenced
by the Heraclitan Cratylus. After the death of Socrates, Plato
accompanied the other disciples to the neighboring town of Megara,
where he was no doubt in constant intercourse with the logician
Euclides. Subsequently, being in very easy circumstances, and de-
sirous of finishing his education, he made extensive voyages to
Egypt, to Cyrene, the point of Africa nearest Greece, where mathe-
matics was in advanced cultivation, to Greek Italy, where there were
the Pythagoreans and the Eleatics, and finally to Syracuse. There
he seems to have been unable to contain his disgust at Dionysius the
elder, who handed him over to the Lacedemonians. He was carried to
Aegina as a prisoner, where he is said to have been exposed for sale
in the slave-market. He was ransomed and returning to Athens opened
his school in the Academy. On the death of Dionysius, in the latter
part of 368 B.C., Dionysius the younger invited Plato to Syracuse.
