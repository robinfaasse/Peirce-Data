But his passion for mystification was extreme. Once as we were
leaving a mosque together, where he had been reading a chapter to
the congregation at the invitation of the imâm, who, though not ac-
quainted with him, had at once remarked his [del]beautiful[/del] [ins]exquisite[/ins] Arabic, I could
not restrain myself from saying to him, "How did you ever go to work
to learn to speak such a language so much better than those whose
native tongue it is, and who have been specially trained in it be-
sides?" Now if he had told the truth, he would have said, "It
is because I have a genius for it:" but if he had said what seemed
to him to be the truth, he would have said, 'It is the fruit of hard
work day and night incessantly for long years.' But what he did
say was, "Oh, I hardly know. It was spoken a good deal at my fa-
ther's table." Now his father was an English innkeeper; and it is
possible that Palmer's teacher in Arabic often dined at the inn and
spoke Arabic to Palmer. Drake, the companion of his wanderings in
Arabia, where they lived with the Bedwîn, told me that the day they
left London, as they were walking down Regent Street, Palmer saw in
a toy-shop window one of those snakes composed of a sort of helix, or
winding staircase of paper, or some similar material, -- they are
common things. He, at once, exclaimed that that was just what he
needed, and went in and bought it. So when they were with the wild
Arabs, Palmer would bring it out and play with it, and was regarded
by every Bedwî as the most wonderful of snake charmers; and it was
really of the greatest service in helping him to manage those people.
