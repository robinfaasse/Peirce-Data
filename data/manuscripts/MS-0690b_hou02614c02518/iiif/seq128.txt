it, in the next place, undertaken at great effort? Why, the whole
party probably strolled out of Athens on a moonlight evening and

tudinarian Herodicus recommended, as a constitutional, that a man
should walk from Athens to Megara and when he reached the walls
should turn and walk back, without stopping. By the way, when the
companions were more than half way to Megara they would pass through
Eleusis. Now Cicero says nothing about Plato's ever having made a
journey to Eleusis; but are we, therefore, to believe that Plato
was not initiated into the Eleusinian mysteries? I will be so bold
as to conjecture that Plato even took at sometime the far more con-
siderable journey to the Olympian festival. And, in the third
place, would this journey be made for the sake of learning? If so,
why say that those who narrate it accuse Plato of cowardice?
Plato was probably as often in Megara as an average New Yorker in
Hoboken.
