it more than probable that much of this dialogue was composed before
Aristotle's coming; and probably no better stylometric date could
be given than 367 B.C.
There remains but one more dialogue of which the date is
supposed to be indicated by an allusion which it contains. This is
the Theaetetus, which has a proem so disconnected from the body of
the dialogue that the suspicion that it might have been prefixed
long after the dialogue was written is ready to assert itself at the
least difficulty. This proem seems to have but two purposes, at
most. One is to say something which will be agreeable to the be-
reaved family of Theaetetus, supposing that he is a real person.
That purpose is clearly betrayed when this wholly unknown Theaetetus
is declared to have been an eminent man, ελλόγιμον. The other, which
is doubtful, is either to give assurance that the dialogue is his-
torical, as some think, or, as seems more probable, simply to connect
the proem naturally with the dialogue by explaining how the latter
