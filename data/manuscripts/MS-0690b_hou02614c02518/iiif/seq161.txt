        Our seventh prediction was that after the expiration of
the long lull, about 377 B.C. we should expect his works to be marked
by their ambitious character.  Certainly, nothing could be more
ambitious than the [u]Republic[/u].
        Our eighth prediction was that after Plato's return from
his second voyage to Syracuse, in 367 B.C., we should expect for a
considerable time no more moral dialogues.  This is verified by the
characters of the Parmenides, Critias, and Timaeus.
        Our ninth prediction was that Aristotle's advent would be
marked by a new departure.  That is most complete.  For the Theae-
tetus affords but slight hints of what was coming.  I must confess
that it is difficult to believe that the Theaetetus was written
before the Phaedrus.  That is a phenomenon of which our hypothesis,
as it stands, affords no explanation.  I must, at least, suppose
that the Phaedrus was written in 373 B.C. instead of 372 B.C., and
that the Theaetetus was written in 372 B.C. instead of 374 B.C.
        Our tenth prediction was that after 361 B.C. Plato's phil-
osophy would show the effect of the exact mind of Archytas.  In re-
gard to that, the Philebus which our table places in 360 B.C. is to
my apprehension the greatest of Plato's works.  But it is not until
the Sophist that we are struck by his striving after exactitude.
But probably this dialogue is dated too late in the table.
