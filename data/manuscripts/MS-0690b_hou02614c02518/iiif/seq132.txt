But, for my part, I think his disgust with Athens would be so deep
that nothing would have brought him back but a serious danger to
the city, which would remind him of his duty to serve in her army.
This would be in 394 B.C., when war broke out with Lacedemon.
Diogenes quotes Aristoxenus, who wrote a life of Plato, as saying
that he was present at a battle of Corinth. Although the testimony
is worth next to nothing, because he seems to confound Plato with
Socrates, yet, as there was a battle of Corinth just at the time when
we might expect that Plato would be disposed to volunteer, I think
we may admit the hypothesis, and look for some allusion to it in the
dialogues. He seems to have[ins]made[/ins] some serious studies in Mathematics
under Theodorus in Cyrene; and therefore we should expect little
allusion to mathematics in earlier dialogues. Thirdly, one would
expect that, after his return from his first voyages, his dialogues
would contain some references to Cyrene, Egypt, Syracuse, Aegina,
etc. Fourthly, when he first opened the Academy he would be likely
to write with great diligence a number of dialogues to serve the
purposes of the school. Fifthly, he must, after the treatment he
had received from Dionysius, have entertained a deep reprobation of
the character of autocrats. Sixthly, after the Academy was well
running and his first dialogues, written on that occasion, were pub-
