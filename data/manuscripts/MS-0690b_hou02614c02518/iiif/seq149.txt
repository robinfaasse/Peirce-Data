But Plato makes Socrates say: "We must remember that Tisias and
Gorgias regarded probability as superior to truth, and used it to
make the little appear great and the great little; and I shall never
forget how Prodicus laughed at this device." Unless this part of
the dialogue were entirely rewritten, this could not have been sub-
sequently inserted, for it is woven into the context. The [u]Phaedrus[/u]
must, then, be subsequent to 380 B.C. On the other hand, in order
to show that it is earlier than 378 B.C. Thompson refers to an un-
paralleled phenomenon presented by this dialogue, namely, that in it
Plato copies [u]in extenso[/u] an entire oration which he attributes to
Lysias, who may be said to have been, until his death in 378 B.C.,
the leader of the Athenian bar, -- as far as it is possible to apply
this modern term to the ancient fact. It is argued that this au-
dacious act must have been perpetrated before the death of Lysias,
although one would think that that barrister would have found some
remedy in Athenian law. In order to explain this quotation, it
has been suggested that the oration of Lysias was a [u]jeu d'esprit[/u], --
a burlesque upon the speech attributed to Socrates in the [u]Symposium[/u].
This is extremely ingenious; but Lysias had not for many, many years
