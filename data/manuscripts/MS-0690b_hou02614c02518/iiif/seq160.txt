Our second prediction was that after his sojourn in Megara
we should find illustrations of Megaric quibbling. That we do find
in the Euthydemus, of which the corrected date is 391 B.C., and,
therefore, no doubt, several years after Plato's return to Athens.

Our third prediction was that after Plato's return from
his first voyages his dialogues would contain allusions to the
countries he had visited. This prediction utterly fails. For
as far as I remember, there is no reference of the sort except to
the early closing of the gates of Aegina in the Cratylus.

Our fourth and sixth predictions were that on the opening
of the Academy in 387 B.C. Plato would rapidly produce several dia-
logues after which there would be an unproductive interval of some
ten years. Now if we advance the Gorgias a year or so, we have the
Meno, Gorgias, Cratylus and Symposium composed within the first two
or three years of the opening of the Academy; and if we except the
Phaedo, as an inspiration which could not wait, [ins]nothing[/ins] else seems to have
been produced until the ten years from the opening of the Academy
were nearly or quite fulfilled. Of course some of the Minor dia-
logues not on the list may have come in here, especially the Menex-
enus, which is evidently a pure exhibition or advertisement.

Our fifth prediction was that his return would be marked
by his sudden disgust with tyrants. The disgust is marked enough;
but as to its sudden appearance, I am not prepared to maintain that
we do find that.
