The [u]Meno[/u] is a supplement to the Protagoras, and was there-
fore presumably composed later. Yet Plato here drags in by head
and shoulders an allusion to the bribery of Ismenias, which took
place 395 B.C. He had to introduce, as one of the characters of
the dialogue, Anytus, the chief of the accusers of Socrates. Now
Anytus had, ten years before the accusation of Socrates, been him-
self accused of corruption and only escaped by bribing the judges.
According to Diogenes Laertius, he was afterwards banished for the
part he took in the persecution of Socrates. He was of humble
origin, being the son of a tanner. In the Meno, Socrates says,
"Here is Anytus, son of a rich and astute father, who became rich,
not as a matter of course or by anybody's gift, as Ismenias the
Theban has lately piled up the wealth of Polycrates, but by his own
talent and industry." Further down Socrates says: "Anytus seems
to be vexed; and well he may be. For he imagines that I am defam-
ing gentlemen; and he also imagines that he is a gentleman himself.
