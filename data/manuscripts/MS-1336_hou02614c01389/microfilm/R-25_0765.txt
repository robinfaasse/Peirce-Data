[u]1.[/u]

[u]Philosophy[/u] is the study whose purpose it is to determine what is the rational attitude toward things, in general.

It is not the most abstract of the sciences, as it is often said to be. It rests upon experience; for to decide what our sentiments ought to be toward things in general without taking any account of human experience of life, would be most foolish. As resting on experience, philosophy is less abstract than mathematics, which does not rest upon experience. Mathematics begins by formulating certain "hypotheses". These may be suggested by experience; but how far they agree with experience, it is no part of the mathematician's business to determine. He develops his hypotheses, and shows what consequences follow from them: that is all. It is true that the procedure of the mathematician is "experimental." That is, he experiments with the diagrams which he constructs according to his hypotheses. But no "experience" is called for. Experience is that which is quite irresistibly forced in upon us in the course of life. Some truths are forced in upon the mathematician in the study of his hypotheses; but they are not forced in upon him in the course of life; for his hypothesis is [del]merely[/del] quite voluntarily, and as it were, arbitrarily set up.
