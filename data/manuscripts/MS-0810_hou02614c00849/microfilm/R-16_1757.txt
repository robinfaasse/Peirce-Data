[u]Conclusion[/u]

It may be well to conclude with a brief recapitulation of the fundamental principles of the logic of relatives.

[del]All[/del] Terms, then, are either [u]absolute[/u] or [u]relative[/u]. Relative terms are either [u]simple relatives[/u] or [u]conjugatives[/u]. Conjugatives are reducible to relatives of two correlates. The most important division of simple relatives is into [u]concurrents[/u] and [u]opponents[/u]. The former imply an agreement in some respects among objects, the latter set [ins]the relate[/ins] over against its correlate. "Man that is —" is a concurrent, "Husband of —" is an opponent. The division of conjugatives needs further investigation.

Every simple relative has its converse, so that simple relatives generally occur in pairs; as "lover of —" and "loved by —" Conjugatives of two correlates occur in sixes; as "giver of — to —," "giver to — of —," "presented by — with —," "presented with — by —," "given to — by —," "given by — to —."

A correlate may either be [u]undistributed[/u], (as in "lover of a woman") or [u]distributed[/u] (as in "lover of every woman"). If distributed it is so [u]positively[/u] (as in "lover of every woman") or [u]negatively[/u] (as in "lover of nothing but a woman").
