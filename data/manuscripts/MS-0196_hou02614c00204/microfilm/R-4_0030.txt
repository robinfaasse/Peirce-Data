We find, then, that when the arc is increased
by any length $\theta$, the quantity at its extremity
is multiplied by $e^{\theta i}$; and since when the
arc is zero that quantity is $1$, it follows that
if $\theta$ be the arc the quantity at its extremity
is [del]$B$[/del] $e^{\theta i}$.

We now have two ways of writing an imaginary.
One by rectangular coordinates
$$y + xi$$
The other by polar coordinates
$$R e^{\theta i}$$
where $R = \sqrt{y^2 + x^2}$.

We may also write it
$$R \left( \frac{y}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}} + \frac{xi}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}} \right)$$

The values of [del]$\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}}$[/del] and $\frac{y}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}}$ for given values
of $\theta$ are obtained very simply from the
above formula.
$$\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}} = \theta - \frac{1}{3!} \theta^3 + \frac{1}{5!} \theta^5 - \text{etc}$$
$$\frac{y}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}} = 1 - \frac{1}{2!} \theta^2 + \frac{1}{4!} \theta^4 - \frac{1}{6!} \theta^6 + \text{etc}$$

These are called sine $\theta$ and cosine $\theta$, generally
written $\sin \theta$ and $\cos \theta$. The arc $\theta = \int_0^{\sin \theta} \frac{d}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}$
