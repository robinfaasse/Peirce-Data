In order to [del]ascertain[/del] [ins]test[/ins] a given syllogism we must first reduce it to three propositions containing three terms. In order to do this, we may be obliged to change the phraseology; and perhaps to add a proposition which is intended without being expressed. For example, we read that "With some of them God was not well pleased; for they were overthrown in the wilderness." Here it seems to be implied that "no one overthrown in the wilderness pleases God." Hence we write the syllogism thus:—

You observe that a term need not consist of one word only; thus [u]person overthrown in the wilderness[/u] makes [del]but[/del] [ins]collectively[/ins] one term inasmuch as it is [del]all[/del] the subject of [del]a[/del] [ins]one[/ins] proposition & predicate of another. So [u]person pleasing to God[/u] makes but one term.

So it is said; He that is of God heareth my words; Ye therefore hear them not, because ye are not of God. The syllogism is

All men of God are hearers of me
[del]Some[/del] [ins]None[/ins] of you are [del]not[/del] hearers of me
$\therefore$ Some of you are not [ins]men[/ins] of God.

Here "men of God" and "hearers of my words" are each one term. Having first then arranged the syllogism in three propositions & having accurately observed that there are but three terms not apparently only but in meaning, the next step is to find that term which is subject of one proposition & predicate of another. There will either be three such terms or only one. If there are three the syllogism is [u]Theophrastean[/u]; if there is but one, it is [u]Aristotelean[/u]. It is very seldom that you will meet with a Theophrastean syllogism; if you do you will find that it contains two propositions which are Universal Negative or Particular Affirmative. By transposing the terms of either of these you will turn it into an Aristotelean syllogism. There are therefore two Aristotelean syllogisms into which you can thus convert it. If these are good, the Theophrastean is good; if not, not, and it belongs to the same two figures [del]of[/del] as the two Aristotelean syllogisms into which it is converted. [del]The case[/del] [ins]It[/ins] is not likely, however, that such a syllogism would ever be brought to your notice.
