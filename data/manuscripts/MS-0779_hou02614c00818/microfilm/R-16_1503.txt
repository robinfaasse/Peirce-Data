The sentence, then, is [del]certainly[/del] [ins]only[/ins] a definition. Now Aristotle is so scrupulously precise in important definitions, that nobody could have any acquaintance with him who should imagine that he had introduced the double negative, "no man can be instanced who is not mortal," into his definition of universal affirmation without a controlling reason for doing so. Why [del]could[/del] might he not have said that [del]All men are[/del] 'Every man is mortal' means 'Take any man you can and he will be mortal'? Evidently, because he has almost defined Particular propositions as being the precise contradictories of Universal propositions, so that 'Every man is mortal' and 'Some man is mortal' could, under no circumstances, both be false. But in Greek it would be, if possible, more unnatural in English to understand the Particular as meaning [del]Eith[/del] 'If there be any man there is a man that is not mortal,' owing to there being in that language no handy way of [del]expressing[/del] [ins]marking[/ins] the distinction which some of our logicians pretend to discern between saying 'Some man is white' and 'There exists a white man'; for ἄνθρωπός τις λευκός [ins]or ἔστιν ἄνθρωπος λευκός[/ins] means no more the one than the other. Therefore, he was almost forced to say that the impossibility of finding a contrary instance sufficed to justify a universal assertion.
