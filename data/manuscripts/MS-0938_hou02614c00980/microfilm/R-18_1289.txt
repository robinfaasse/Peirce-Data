Mr. Balfour's [ins]British Association[/ins] address is about what might have been expected, and does not enrich mankind with any new conceptions. It is at its best in a passage about intimations in scientific minds of truths not proved, as instanced particularly by the antipathy to the idea of action at a distance on the part of Faraday, who is compared, in his treatment of nature, to a magistrate sharply cross-examining a witness. There is a rich originality indeed in the comparison of the sagacity of a man so submissive to the teachings of experiment with such a judge's oh-I-know-better-ness. However, it certainly is remarkable how some of the greatest scientific discoverers have had a sort of prevision of what they were destined to contribute to established truth. It is not [del]surprising, indeed,[/del] [ins]indeed,[/ins] as a vague proposition, that man's mind, developed, as it has been under the influence of nature, should have some power of phosphorescing, as it were, with nature's light, to which it has been [ins]in an occult way[/ins] exposed; moreover had there been no general resemblance between what it was natural for man to conceive and nature's ways, no step could ever have been taken in nomological science. But such unquantified reflections as these only excite our curiosity to know what vestiges of such a power may be discernible upon the sands of scientific history. Will not somebody look into this, and tell us of them? There must, one would guess, have been some share of good luck in Faraday's success.
