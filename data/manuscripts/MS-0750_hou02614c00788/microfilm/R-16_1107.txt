<u>Logic. I.</u>

The end of logic is to distinguish between good and bad reasoning; and therefore the very first question that must be answered is, wherein does this distinction consist? Here we strike a controversy at the outset; for some of the best logicians of today, — Sigwart, for instance, — hold that the essence of the distinction is a difference of feeling. But I maintain that that is the most fatal doctrine [ins]that[/ins] [del]that[/del] no matter how much disposed men might be to be satisfied with a bad argument, it would not be good, for all that. The very utmost [del]that[/del] to which such perversity of thinking could lead would be the utter destruction of all possibility of reasoning; but granted that there <u>is</u> any such thing as reasoning, then I say there is a distinction between good and bad reasoning, and [del]this distinction[/del] whether a reasoning be good or bad is not at all a question of how men feel or think. It is a question of <u>fact</u>.

This position, which is decisive of many others, requires elucidation. I will grant, with all my heart, that if a man feels perfectly satisfied with any proposition, he cannot inquire further into the truth of it. He can pretend to do so, as we see writers on morals and religion pretending to reason about matters their minds are quite made up about; but it is mere pretence.
