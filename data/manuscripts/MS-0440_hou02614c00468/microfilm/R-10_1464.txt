It would therefore be natural to formulate it as follows:

Anything of the nature of M has the character $\Pi$, taken haphazard;

S approaches in a certain degree to having the character $\Pi$;

$\therefore$ Probably, S approaches in [del]also[/del] like degree to [del]having[/del] [ins]being of[/ins] the nature of M.

This formulation makes this figure an induction of qualities; and in
my essay in the Johns Hopkins Studies, I confessed myself bound to
admit that such it was. But a man must indeed be of the [u]semper eadem[/u]
persuasion if 17 years of hard study can teach him nothing. In [date]1878[/date]
I had argued strongly against regarding this figure as an Induction
of Qualities. I now see plainly that I was [del]no[/del] right. That seductive but
superficial doctrine of the perfect balance of Logical Breadth and Depth
which I early imbibed from Kantian logicians, here led me astray. The
fact that the depth of two terms cannot be mutually exclusive as the breadth
may be is conclusive against the perfect balance. There can be no Induction
of Qualities because there can be no random sampling of qualities, since they are
innumerable. Moreover, [del]of[/del] [ins]equal[/ins] degrees of resemblances, are purely subjective, and
not facts, at all. Any two things are just as much alike as any other two in
