[u]Chapter[/u] [u]Brocardian Geometry[/u].

[u]Art[/u] . [u]Definition[/u] . [u]Brocardian Geometry[/u] (named from one of its eminent student Henri Brocard) is the geometry [del]or[/del] study of [del]particular[/del] points, rays, and circles having remarkable metrical relations to a triangle. [del]It is confined to a plane.[/del]

[u]Mem[/u]. Much of this [ins]part[/ins] [del]geo[/del] of geometry is only about ten years old. There is, at present, a rage for it among some mathematicians; [del]and[/del] it has [del]a[/del] high disciplinary value; [del]and is interesting.[/del] It has not yet developed into any important mathematical theory; and it can be passed by without much loss. In this chapter only some specimens of it are given, [del]mainly extracts[/del] [ins]excellent[/ins] in the collection of which the treatises of William J. M'Clelland, R. Lachlan, and John Wellesley Russell have been drawn upon.

[u]Scholium[/u] . Distances in Brocardian geometry are signate, and AB is written to signify [ins]the length[/ins] the negative of BA. In like manner [math]\angle AOB = - \angle BOA[/math]

[u]Definitions[/u] . The ray through two points is called their join.

. The point of intersection of two rays is called their meet.

. Three or more rays having a common point are said to be concurrent
