It has only been in modern times, say since the discovery of America, that an art of computation has been admitted to be a [ins]by[/ins] part of arithmetic. For [del]for[/del] arithmetic was [del]to be[/del] meant a science, and a science whose sole purpose was general knowledge, the knowledge of the nature and relations of numbers. Computation, on the other hand, is an art; not even a practical science, but a mere art, by which men have in all ages earned their living. No man performs the functions of a man of science for pay: it is not possible. Accordingly, the art of using the Arabic figures had a name of its own. In English, it was called "augrim". I, [ins]for my poor part,[/ins] regret the disuse of this designation, for it is a word of Chaucer's, and carries that scent of apple-blossoms that is diffused through the Canterbury Tales. It was a euphonization of "algorithm." Though the more pleasing form is obsolete, the word "algorithm" is still applied by mathematicians to the operation of any peculiar rule of computation, such as that for finding the greatest common divisor of two numbers. The philologers are frantic to have the mathematicians change the form of this word, on the ground that the [u]th[/u] is an erroneous transliteration of the Arabic original.
