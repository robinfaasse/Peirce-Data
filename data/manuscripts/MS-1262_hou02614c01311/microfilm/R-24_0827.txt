[pn]8[/pn] Seven and a half centuries after the little book of El-Khwarezmiy was published, his system was extended to fractions. To do this required little or no inventiveness. A mere inadvertency in doing such a sum as the division of 1234567800 by 200000 would bring it about. The pen itself, without incessant care, would force it upon the inventor. If any invention could be perfect from the first, this should be the one. Let us, then, examine its history.

In minutiae like this, one must not trust implicitly to any history of mathematics, nor to any superficial or inconsiderate examination of the old arithmetic-books. Great care and prudent logic are required. We find then that at the close of the middle ages, the prevalent idea was, and preconceived ideas were more powerful then in mathematics than they are now in metaphysics, that there were but two kinds of fractions, the vulgar and the philosophical. The vulgar were what we know as such. The philosophical were minutes, or sixtieths; seconds, or sixtieths of [del]sixtieths[/del] [ins]minutes[/ins]; and thirds, or sixtieths of seconds. There might be fourths, fifths, etc.; but there was no need of them. The real meaning of this was that the word [u]third[/u] meant vulgarly [fraction]1/3[/fraction] but with astronomers and others [fraction]1/216000[/fraction]; but [del]words[/del] facts were greatly confounded in these times, and the impression on men's minds was that there was no other system of fractions but these two. The first step was taken by the astronomer George of Peurbach, but was only published eighty years after his death in 1461. This consisted in a table of sines calculated for a radius of 600000, which thus became, the virtual [ins]in effect, the[/ins] common denominator of all the [del]sines[/del] [ins]fractions[/ins] of which the tabulated sines were the numerators; so that the sexagesimal idea was infected with a decimal modification.
