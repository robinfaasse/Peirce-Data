[pn]G[/pn] Richerus puts an emphasis upon the circumstance that this
[pn]54[/pn] abacus was adapted to a pedagogical purpose. Now what
does he mean by saying that the borrowing of the counters by
the 27 divisions of the abacus would express either the multi-
plication or the division of them by each of the numbers? It
seems to me that what is meant is, for example, that a beginner
would be taught to multiply 27 by 37 in this shape:

[fig]Abacus diagram with columns numbered 9 to 1 showing multiplication of 27 by 37[/fig]

There are nine columns, each having a units' place, a tens' place, and a
hundreds' place; and for the beginner, a number placed in any column
denotes the product of that number [ins]multiplied[/ins] by the digit at the head of the column.
[del][At a later stage of the instruction, it may denote the quotient of the number entered di-[/del]
[del]vided by the heading digit.][/del] The beginner, then, first places counters to repre-
sent 27 and 37; next he represents 27 x 7 and 27 x 30 by putting 27
into the 7 and 3 columns. Then he successively substitutes for these four
figures, the products that they represent 140, 49, 600, and 210, and
adding, gets 999. If the product exceeds a thousand the [del]first[/del] [ins]units'[/ins] place
of the column headed 2 is regarded as the thousands' place of the
column headed 1, etc., which will occasion very little confusion.
The reason Gerbert ordered a thousand counters, was that it was a
round number [del]that would[/del] [ins]sufficient to[/ins] enable him to propose, as an exercise,
the addition of any three numbers of 27 figures each. It was a
liberal allowance.
