At the time Alkhwarizmî's little book about computation was put forth, the Arabs were of all peoples the one where the notation for numbers was the worst confounded. Let us run over what [ins]Dr.[/ins] Moritz Cantor, the [del]very same[/del] [ins]sanest of the[/ins] historians of mathematics, says on this subject. He remarks, to begin with, that the Arabians were particularly fond of writing out the names of numbers at full length, and in particular cites a text-book of arithmetic in which this method, "if method it can be called," is [ins]found[/ins] employed throughout. Then there were the "dîwânî figures," which were abbreviations of the numeral words, the units being placed between the tens and the hundreds. In the next place, the Arabians frequently denoted the numbers 1, 2, 3, ... 8, 9, 10, 20, 30, ... 80, 90, 100, 200, 300, 400, by the twenty-two letters of the Arabic alphabet in their order. Only, as that order was not very fixedly settled, this method was a little uncertain. Subsequently, they availed themselves of the six letters which receive diacritical marks to denote the hundreds from 500 to 1000. It is now well settled, [ins]except in certain regions,[/ins] that the letters in their present [del]and[/del] well-fixed order represent 1, 2, 400, 500, 3, 8, 600, 4, 700, 900, 7, 60, 300, 90, 800, 9, 900, 70, 1000, 80, 100, 20, 30, 40, 50, 5, 6, 10.
