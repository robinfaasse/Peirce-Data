It is of the utmost importance that we should not
err in saying that the Rule is a premiss in the usual
sense; and for that purpose we must be sure that we
[del]can[/del] really distinguish between the principle of
a process of inference and a premiss. We might have
a logic which took account of a great many facts
not generally taken account of; that would be what
Kant calls a particular logic. We should not have
to supply so many premisses to such a logic. Sup-
pose on the other hand, we try to have as few [ins]logical[/ins] princi-
ples as we can, and yet have enough to perform every
inference; that is enough to arrive at every [del]lately[/del] con-
clusion which could anyhow be obtained [del]x[/del] from the
same aggregate of [del]premisses[/del] [ins]leading[/ins] [del]and[/del] principles, - only by
such [ins]leading[/ins] principles [del]and[/del] would enable us to get along with
the fewest possible. It is plain that since we should then
have to have some processes of inference, we should
be obliged to have some principles of logic. Now
these fewest facts which logic must in any case take
cognizance of, is what I mean by the principles
of logic. It is plain that all men are mortal is
not one of these. It is also plain that the fact that
Socrates is a man gives the certain proposition Socrates

is mortal, when it is taken in connection with cer-
tain logical facts & also with all men are mortal.
Hence it is plain that in this usual sense the
Rule All men are mortal is a premiss of the
syllogism
All men are mortal,
Socrates is a man;
