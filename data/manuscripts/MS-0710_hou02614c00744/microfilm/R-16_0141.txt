[del]No[/del] Notes on Kempe's Paper. [marginalia]Ms. 710[/marginalia]

He continues to use the term 'indistinguishable'
(properly on applicable to things [del]of[/del] [ins]between[/ins] which there
is no means of saying which is which) to mean
'exactly alike, but distinguishable'. But he
now, at least, defines the terms, which he failed
to do in his Theory of Math. Forms.

He does not say that $ab\cdot c = ba\cdot c$. Presu-
mably this equation does not in general hold.

Law I. From $ab\cdot p$ $cp\cdot d$ we infer
[del]and[/del] $\begin{array}{cc} ad\cdot q & bc\cdot q \\ c\cdot b\cdot r & da\cdot r \end{array}$ (but there is no reason to suppose $q=r$)

Law II From $ab\cdot p$ $cp\cdot d$ we infer
$\begin{array}{cc} bc\cdot q & aq\cdot d \\ ca\cdot r & br\cdot d \end{array}$

Law III From $aa\cdot c$ we infer $a=c$

Law IV $ac\cdot a$

By Law V he means to say (having quite forgotten
to say that $ab\cdot c$ is possible) that the letters denote
[del]mere po[/del] logical possibilities, so that whatever
Laws I to IV do not make impossible is necessary.
