To the Editor of The Nation: [date]1901[/date]

The [u]Oxford Dictionary[/u] offers no explanation of the origin of the word 'futchel', the pieces to which the pole of a wagon is attached. But it seems that our cartwrights call it 'furchel', and it is so printed in Dufief's [u]Nature Displayed[/u]. Supposing this to be the word, and supposing that is derived from the Latin through the French, the Latin word [del]should be[/del] [ins]could hardly[/ins] be anything but [u]furcale[/u]. Now in Ducange under the word [u]forale[/u] is thus defined: "Pars currus, lignum scilicet [del]perforatum[/del] [ins]perforatum[/ins], in quo ponuntur furcalia. Ita Interpres Joannis de Garlandia, in Synonymis:
Temo, longale, trabale, [del]foral[/del] [ins]furcale[/ins], forale."
It may, then, be assumed, till some objection is raised, that 'futchel' comes from [u]furcale[/u] through a French [u]fourchel[/u]. Is this word in the dictionary?
F.
