To the Editor of the Evening Post
Sir —

As to the matter of good English translations from
Latin, how can a boy be expected to study Latin and
English, not only at the same time, but in the same act?
[del]Latin[/del] Is it not true that Latin is so superior to English,
especially in those qualities now most popularly admired,
concision and energy, that it is simply impossible for any
man whose mind is filled with Latin to write vernacular
English, at all? How many powerful writers of really
vernacular English has history to show? They can be
numbered on the fingers of one hand. Every one of them
wrote English so well because he did not know Latin, — did
not [del]really[/del] know it in its force and implications. Defoe
wrote English; [ins]Bunyan wrote English;[/ins] [del]if[/del] Gen [del]l[/del] Grant wrote English. They had no other
tongue. Ruskin is not English; it is Carlyle refined. Car-
lyle is not English; it is Scotch doctored with an imitation
of Thomas Nash. The inversions, the odd constructions, are
the usual Celtic syntax. The style of Nash, with its magni-
ficence and its sonorousness, is an avowed imitation of Aretino.
It is most beautiful; but it has nothing English about it. If you
wish to teach boys to write genuine English [del]and to understand its power[/del], you must
take them in [del]the[/del] hours when Latin is quite out of mind. C.
