Having now seen the notices of my "Outlines of Logic," I am glad to find that my critic is in such reception to the main idea of the book. If we point the so called indirect, the direct, however, fall into a non-deductive category (Geometric, Elements) which the little volume abundantly illustrates in some of the later syllogisms. The preface also will say, in effect, that all reasoning and inference are contained in the syllogism — the term is used in its best sense, "training in formal logic prepares a student to tackle all the essay side problems of daily reasoning." My written text is simply this: Aristotelian Logic still holds a place in university curricula; I see myself paid to teach it at St. Bees; and as I have found that the majority of text books presented the subject in such form as to fill the student with aversion, I sought to write the subject in concise, lucid manner so to command esteem and awaken interest.

With us, as in most institutions, the English and Scotch school, inductive, probable, and hypothetical arguments — to join with analogy, etc. — are taken up in more advanced classes. I have, myself, I think, probably the largest collection, with in manuscript, that exists; but the addition of these would have made altogether too bulky a volume. Logic, in the widest sense of the term, indeed includes all the intellectual processes of the human mind; but the presentation of these in a volume would result in a treatise — such a one as I hope to publish, by, I hope, [del]F[/del] [ins]P[/ins]ublisher, and the like.
