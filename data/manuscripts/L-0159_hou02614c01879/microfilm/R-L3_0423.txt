[u]To the Editor of the Evening Post[/u]

Sir:

It seems to me that the remedy for the state of things described by Col. George Bliss in his letter to Gen. Tracy, is to contrive a way in which attending to politics shall become a part of a man's business. Will the following suggestion, even if not itself acceptable, perhaps bring out another which maybe so?

Let a census of the city be taken according to, say, 1000 trades or professions, and also by districts. On that basis, let a list of [del]say[/del] 300 business interests, to be called trades, be made, in such a way that [del]they represent[/del] [ins]the different "trades"[/ins] [del]about[/del] roughly equal parts of the population. [ins]The very large classes would be divided by districts so as to effect this equality[/ins] Let one of 300 different days of the year be the election-day for each trade. Let each trade on its election-day assemble in one hall in the morning, pass any resolutions they please, with discussion, and at 3 in the afternoon [ins]entering office one week after election[/ins] proceed to elect a "syndic". Let this syndic [del]have certain[/del] [ins]judicial[/ins] powers in regard to commercial suits, etc. No man to serve as syndic for two successive years. Let [del]the[/del] [ins]a[/ins] council of the 300 syndics form a municipal assembly to decide upon questions [del]of policy[/del] connected with the administration of municipal affairs, such as the amount to be appropriated for street-cleaning, the question of interfering with "Seeley dinners", and many other questions, which are certainly [del]questions[/del] [ins]matters[/ins] of policy although they relate mainly to the expenditures [del]upon[/del] [ins]by[/ins] municipal departments, and in so far are [del]questions[/del] [ins]matters[/ins] of administration.

P.
