S. I think I said or implied that a certain class of objections
to these theories were considered. What I complained had not been
considered was the logic of the question. A theory is supposed to explain
something. Both these theories are intended to explain apparent
action at a distance. To do that we must either resort to impact or
to hydrostatic pressure. The Le Sage theory resorts to the former, the vortex
atom theory to the latter. But are impact and hydrostatic pressure
more intelligible than action at a distance? If not [del]they are[/del] but the
reverse, the grave objection to both theories is that they are attempts to
explain the more intelligible by the less intelligible. Impact and hydro-
static pressure are familiar everyday experiences. Action at a distance
is not. Hence the reluctance of men to accept the latter as fundamental.
But unless physics, mathematics, & logic are all on the wrong track,
the most intelligible conceptions, — although they are most difficult, —
are those through continuity. From this point of view impact is the
most unintelligible & incredible thing. In fact, we know of no impact.
The molecules of gases only encounter one another, that is, come within
the sphere of marked attraction (or repulsion). So with solids, whose atoms
move freely in orbits. If we believe liquids are composed of molecules,
their hydrostatic pressure is really action at a distance. So this appears
that action at a distance ought to be used to explain impact & hydrostatic
pressure, and not the reverse.

P.S. I haven't the slightest objection to defending my views at length
and in propria persona.
