Editor of The Evening Post:

Sir:

In the very profound and comprehensive review of Dr. Henry Smith Williams' "Story of Nineteenth Century Science" in the Evening Post of Dec. 28, there is one sentence which it seems to me must have been subjected to the "bad proof reading" of which the reviewer had a little before complained. It reads thus; "Because Faraday had no mathematical training and was consequently unable to think clearly about action at a distance, he was led to develop another way of thinking about the forces of electricity which not only is extremely attractive to a mathematical mind, but also gave rise to Maxwell's theory of electricity, etc." What puzzles me is to understand how a lack of mathematical training could have been the cause of the development of a way of thinking extremely attractive to a mathematical mind. This seems like a "non sequitur."

In this connection I would like to inquire where I can find a concise exposition of the theory or doctrine of "action at a distance" which is mentioned several times by the reviewer.

ANTHONY OVER.
