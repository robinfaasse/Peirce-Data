<u>There are a number of ladies employed in the Survey who</u>
<u>seldom appear at the Office, and the result of whose work is not</u>
<u>commensurate with their pay. Other ladies have work sent them to</u>
<u>be done at home.</u>

<u>There are, also, a number of old or decrepit men at the of-</u>
<u>fice who, either, do not or cannot render adequate service. It is</u>
<u>hard to turn out old and worthy people, but under the law I see</u>
<u>no escape, unless some fund is provided to pension them. It is</u>
<u>not, in my opinion, proper to pay them out of the Survey fund.</u>

<u>The difficulty of getting rid of these people is very great.</u>
<u>They have friends who will make interest for them, while the In-</u>
<u>spector, who is trying to do his duty, is not backed by the public,</u>
<u>and is, indeed, often abused for doing what he is bound to do. Upon</u>
<u>the whole, the office is in a condition of laxity in this respect.</u>

<u>The fact of the case is, that the majority of these irre-</u>
<u>gularly paid persons should be discharged. They are in</u>
<u>every case with people, from whom the Nation gets no equivalent,</u>
<u>and under a proper system, I feel confident, more than half, under</u>
<u>which we now labor, could be dispensed with.</u>
