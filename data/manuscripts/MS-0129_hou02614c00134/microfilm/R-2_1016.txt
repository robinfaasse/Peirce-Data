[u]Section 2. Of the three Types of Measurement.[/u]

[u]Scholium[/u]: Measurement is either [u]modular[/u], otherwise called arithmetical, or [ins]it is[/ins] [u]signate[/u], otherwise called [u]algebraical[/u], or it is [del]quaternion[/del] [ins]imaginary[/ins], or it is [u]quaternional[/u]. Modular measurement is measurement proper. (The words imply this; for "modular" [del]has[/del] [ins]means[/ins] little more than relating to a [u]modus[/u], or measure, the proper adjective "modal" being usurped by another idea.) In this, no regard is paid to the direction in which measurement is made. Signate measurement, is a measure with + or - prefixed to show in which of two opposite ways the measurement is made. The sign is no part of the measure proper, but signate measurement is of great value when there are but two ways of measurement possible. [del]In other cases[/del] [ins]The attempt to artificially[/ins] extend it to other cases gives rise to endless trouble. Imaginary measurement is equivalent to the measure proper multiplied by [u]e[/u][sup]nπi[/sup], or [u]i[/u][sup]2n[/sup], where [u]i[/u][sup]2[/sup] = -1. This is satisfactory, in many cases, when we are dealing with two dimensions. Quaternional measurement is the measure proper multiplied by a peculiar multiple quantity, called a [u]versor[/u], by which all the directions of space are distinguished. The original work upon which the present book is founded was the first to employ imaginary measurement in the service of geometry, or at any rate, of elementary geometry.
