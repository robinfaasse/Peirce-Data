[u]Chapter I. Of the Principles of Spatial Measurements.[/u]
[u]Section 1. The Nature of Space Measurement.[/u]
[del]Art.[/del]
[u]Definition[/u]. Space-measures are numbers, attached to limited spatial continuous extensions, in such a way
[u]1[sup]st[/sup][/u], between [del]two[/del] [ins]any two[/ins] limited continua, in dimensions of the same kind and number, there is a numerical ratio of measures;
[u]2[sup]nd[/sup][/u], no continuum has two unequal measures, but every one has the same measure in whatever order its parts be passed over; yet it may be a rule in certain kinds of measurement that [del]certain[/del] [ins]a[/ins] finite measure equals zero; (Thus 24 hours equals zero in reckoning the time of day.)
[u]3[sup]rd[/sup][/u], the measure of every whole is the sum of the measures of its homogeneous parts;
[u]4[sup]th[/sup][/u], the comparison of two continua as equal, or, if unequal, as to which is the greater, is effected by moving about "rigid" bodies, and assuming [del]the[/del] [ins]any two[/ins] places occupied at different times by the same part of a rigid body to be equal, and the place occupied by a part to be less than that occupied by the whole.
