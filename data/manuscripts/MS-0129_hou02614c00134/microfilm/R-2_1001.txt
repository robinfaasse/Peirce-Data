Chapter . Of the Principles of Spatial Measurement.

[u]Section I. Of the Nature of Spatial Measurement.[/u]

[u]Art . Analysis[/u] . We now come to the subject of [ins]the[/ins] measurement [del]of space[/del] [ins]the parts of space[/ins]. Here, as in all mathematics, we deal with an idea; but that idea is framed upon the suggestions of observation. We have to note carefully what the facts of observation are. There are a number of questions to be considered which will best be numbered.

Qu: 1. What is a measure? It is a number which we find it convenient and useful to attach to a part of space.

Qu: 2. What is the ordinary method of comparing two places in respect to their equality? We place a rigid body in the first place and mark the boundary of the space upon the body. We then [del]transfer[/del] move the body to the second place, [del]which[/del] if that body precisely fills it, we pronounce equal to the other, but if the body falls wholly within the bounds of [del]a[/del] [ins]the[/ins] second place we pronounce the latter [ins]the[/ins] [del]less[/del] [ins]less[/ins], etc.

Qu: 3. What is a "rigid" body? It is a body which general observation causes us to conceive as not altering when it is moved about.
