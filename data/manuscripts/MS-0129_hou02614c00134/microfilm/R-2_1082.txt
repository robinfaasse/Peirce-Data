[u]Theorem[/u]. [u]The metron has just six degrees of freedom[/u].

[u]Demonstration[/u]. Every particle of the metron must be capable of being brought to any point, which requires three degrees of freedom. Every straight fibre through that particle must then be capable of being brought into any ray through that point, which requires two additional degrees of freedom. Every flat film [del]through[/del] [ins]containing[/ins] that fibre must then be capable of being brought into any plane through that ray, which requires an additional degree of freedom. Thus, six degrees of freedom, at least, are required.

But after three particles of the metron are fixed in three points [ins][A], [B], [C], not in one ray,[/ins], if there could be any further motion, let [S] be the position of a particle which could be moved. This point, [S], cannot be in the plane (ABC). For if it could the ray {AS} would intersect the ray {BC}. And [del]this[/del] [ins]the particle at this[/ins] intersection could not [del]be moved nearer to [B] or to [C][/del] [ins]by the last theorem be moved out of {BC} nor could it[/ins] without altering the distances. Hence, [S] must be out of the plane (ABC). In its motion it must [ins]by the last theorem[/ins] remain in the plane (ABS), in the plane (ACS), and in the plane (BCS). But a particle in space has but three degrees of freedom. To restrict it to one plane, leaves it with [ins]only[/ins] two degrees of freedom; to restrict it to a second, leaves it but with one; to restrict it to a third leaves it with no motion at all. Hence, the metron has no more than six degrees of freedom.
