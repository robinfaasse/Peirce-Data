[u]Chapter[/u] [u]The Foundations of Linear and Angular Measurement[/u].

[u]Art. 1[/u]. [u]Analysis 1[/u]. Suppose that a thing can be changed in some sensible respect. Suppose, for example, that a room can be made lighter by turning on more burners, [ins]or[/ins] [del]or[/del] darker by [del]turning them off[/del]. Then, we have a continuous series of possible, or conceivable, [u]states[/u]. Our senses will generally inform us whether two things are in the same sensible state or not, and in the latter case in which of two [del]opposite[/del] ways they differ.

[u]Definition 1[/u]. [del]A[/del] [ins]By a[/ins] state [ins]we shall mean[/ins] [del]is[/del] an exact sensible character capable of variation in two opposite ways, in a [u]series[/u].

[del]Analysis[/del] [ins][u]Definition[/u][/ins] [u]2[/u]. By an [u]interval[/u] we shall mean the special unlikeness between two states of the same series.

[u]Analysis, continued[/u]. Our senses will, [del]in a rough way[/del] [ins]afford us some rough[/ins] comparisons between the interval between two states and the interval between two other states. Thus, any one would say there was less difference between a starlight night and a moonlit night, than between the light of a theatre and [ins]broad day[/ins].

But it is often most important to make such comparisons of intervals exact. To effect this, we have resort to measuring instruments. Such an instrument must pronounce [ins]any[/ins] [del]one[/del] interval of the series to be greater, less, or equal to another; and [ins]by enabling us to add[/ins] together intervals which have the same limit but do not overlap, must supply numbers to all intervals. Such numbers are called [u]measures[/u].
