I have no knowledge how far the merciless vortex
of evolution has overtaken you in your struggle against
the difficulties of life. But surely you have at least
10 years of good working-space before you. And
systematically husbanded you may achieve therein all
you desire, provided you attend properly to hygienics.

I rejoice to find that with Pythagoras, the divine,
you hold that "it is not the first that is perfect, but the
last". Existence seems to me rationally endurable
only by recognizing that whatever worth life has attained
was gained by slow and toilsome victory over the powers
of destruction. True love is an offspring of the precariousness
of what is precious in life. And there is no real consolation
for the loss of it.

Pessimism and utmost asceticism are the logical
outcome of the view that this existence of ours is an
emanation of some pre-existing state of perfection.
Schopenhauer was my first teacher in philosophy, and I
was one of his first pupils, in fact one of his first discoverers.
For several years of my student-life I was haunted by a keen
sense of the wanton absurdity of having toilsomely to liberate
oneself from the entanglements of this mostly wretched
existence, when the veritable home of one's innermost being
consisted in a pre-existing state of blissful quietude.

I believe now that the "little eddies in this great stream"
are themselves what intellect has brought along; are the
individualized embodiment of all its life-worthy results, and
as such most precious; nothing more so.

Platonic or Concord transcendentalism, in which I often
revel, seems to me helpful in the shaping of the ideal of
human life, hurtful when it aims at dissolving the "little eddies
in the great stream". Yours in human fellowship and [del]for[/del]
the enthusiastic and loving endeavor for its higher
evolution
Edmund Montgomery
