My dear Professor Peirce

It may have seemed rather presumptuous on my part to have [del]tried[/del] [ins]endeavoured[/ins] to forecast some ultimate conclusions to which your published thought seems to tend. But my remarks were merely meant as sympathetic feelers tentatively stretched forth as a sign of the interest I am taking in your work. They were not meant in any way as fore-knowledge or as criticism.

The supreme task of philosophy is undoubtedly the rational unification of the freedom of our inner life and its ethical aspirations and doings with what Kant called "the mechanism of nature". He himself grappled with the problem from the wrong side. By seeking for spontaneity or freedom solely in the "intelligible sphere", he failed to find legitimate leverage for "free causation" among natural phenomena. You have discerned the only way that can lead to a genuine monistic interpretation, and this is scientifically to disprove the validity of the mechanical theory, to show that it is incompetent to account even for the least complicated physical occurrences.

I well understand how the magnitude and intricacy of the plan you have formed to give adequate expression to your views overwhelms you at times with a feeling of helpless despondency; and it was a sigh of lassitude, which on that account seemed to accompany the enunciation of your optimistic convictions, that led me to warn you against overworking.
