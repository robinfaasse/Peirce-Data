[u]Chapter I. Of the Place of Philosophy among the Sciences[/u].

[u]Art: 1[/u]. There are three orders of Skill, or art, namely:
[u]First[/u], the Cunning which makes a part of the artist's being, and cannot be expressed:
[u]Second[/u], the Wisdom which can be laid down in maxims, but cannot be [del]some[/del] deduced by reasoning. Thus, we may say, Love your neighbor, or, Be pure in heart; and secondary reasons for such a mode of life may be given. But he who is pure in heart for the sake of something else, is not pure in heart, at all.
[u]Third[/u], the Theory which sets out with a definite purpose, and assuming certain conditions to be present, finds out a rule for attaining that purpose.
Such a theory inevitably grows into a science among an intellectual people. The Egyptians had nothing more like a science than this, because they had no [del]taste[/del] [ins]impulse[/ins] for generalization but rather a distaste for it. But the Babylonians and Greeks could not help considering together how to carry out similar designs, and so formed a regular Art. Then, finding analogous principles in different arts, they [del]reached[/del] [ins]come to attach[/ins] a value to Science. Finally, discarding all idea of a practical purpose, the Greeks rose to Pure Science. Pure Science has a natural tendency to degenerate into nonsense, pedantry, and hair-splitting.
