[u]Art. 2.[/u] In [del]science, the[/del] [ins]the development of science, a[/ins] point is soon reached at which men have to receive special training, and be armed with special instruments in order to collect their facts. Thus, arise special classes of minds fitted for different kinds of inquiries. It now becomes necessary to distribute science in general into departments. It thus becomes necessary to classify the sciences according to the different sorts of observations which they make. [del]Such[/del] [ins]Modifica-[/ins]tions in the details of such classification arise in course of time. Spectroscopy and photography become applied to astronomy; and [ins]partly[/ins] because an observatory is more costly than a spectroscopic and photographic outfit, it is the astronomer who takes up those arts, not the physicist who takes up astronomy. Philologists will soon be availing themselves of acoustical apparatus; and though books are cheap and easy to use compared with such instruments, we may be sure that the study of language by that means will remain in the hands of the philologists and not be turned over to the physicists.

The reason is, that the segregation of different knots of students according as they are interested in more specialized or more generalized observations, turns out to be more decisive and deep than that between men who use different kinds of instruments.
