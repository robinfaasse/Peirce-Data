[u]Unsymmetrical, or incommutative[/u].

(8d) $x \propto y = \overline{x} \cdot y = \overline{\overline{x} \cdot y}$.
(8e) $x \propto y = \overline{x} \cdot y = \overline{x} \propto \overline{y} = x \propto \overline{y}$

Informations admitting [u]two[/u], and excluding [u]two[/u], sets of values.

Four of these are simply, $x$, $\overline{x}$, $y$, $\overline{y}$. The other two are
(8f) $x = y$; $x \cdot y \cdot \overline{x} \cdot \overline{y}$, $([x \propto y] \propto [y \propto x] \propto f) = \text{f} \cdot \overline{g} (x = y) \propto y \propto x$,
$(x \propto y) \cdot (y \propto x)$, $\{ [x \propto y] \propto y \} \propto x = \{ [y \propto x] \propto x \} \propto y$,
$(x \cdot y) \cdot \overline{(x \cdot y)}$, $(x \cdot y) \cdot (\overline{x} \cdot \overline{y})$, $(x \cdot \lambda y) \cdot (x \cdot \mathbb{T} y)$,
$(x \propto y) \cdot (x \propto y)$, $(x \propto y) \cdot (x \propto y)$.

This sign was introduced by Dr. Robert Recorde, celebrated as
the author of the first arithmetic in English, The Ground of Artes.
This sign occurs in his Whetstone of Witte, 1557, a treatise
on algebra (so called on rip of his mistaking [del]cos[/del], the de-
signation of the unknown quantity, for the Latin word
for a whetstone: it was really derived through the Italian,
from the vulgar Latin cosa, for caussa, [ins]meaning[/ins] the matter
in hand.) It was chosen because the two lines exhibit
