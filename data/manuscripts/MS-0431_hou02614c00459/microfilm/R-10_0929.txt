For since anything may be substituted for $y$ in
(1), substitute for it an absurd proposition, and that
both $x$ and $\overline{x}$ should be true together is reduced to
absurdity.

From the second clause of the definition we deduce the
[u]Principle of Excluded Middle[/u], namely, that
Either $x$ or $\overline{x}$ [del]must[/del] [ins]is[/ins] [del]be[/del] [ins]to be[/ins] true.

[pn](4)[/pn] For since in (2), anything can be substituted for $z$,
let a true proposition be substituted for it, when it will
follow that either $y$ or $\overline{y}$ is true.

If in (1) and (2) we substitute, as we may, $\overline{x}$ for
$x$, we get

[pn](5)[/pn] If $\overline{\overline{x}}$ and $\overline{x}$, then necessarily $y$;
[pn](6)[/pn] If $z$, then necessarily either $\overline{\overline{x}}$ or $\overline{x}$.

From (1) and (6) it follows that
[pn](7)[/pn] If $z$ and $x$, then necessarily either $y$ or $\overline{\overline{x}}$

From (2) and (5) it follows that
[pn](8)[/pn] If $\overline{\overline{x}}$ and $z$, then necessarily, either $y$ or $x$.
