[u]*[/u] I use this word to mean changes of place, which still leave one thing in each place. The mathematicians call them "substitutions."
[/marginalia]

[u]*[/u] In regard to addition of transpositions, if we first interpret it when we have to deal with a triad of values, it will be easy, afterward, to extend the [del]definition[/del] [ins]idea[/ins] to any other system of values. I have seen such astounding blunders committed by the very greatest and exactest minds, — such as Cayley's boggling over a dual algebra or my father's in a paper written on purpose to deal with it, laying himself open to the obvious correction which somebody [ins]some years[/ins] [del]years[/del] later took the trouble to print, — that I have come to the conclusion that it is hopeless to attempt to set bounds to that human stupidity is not capable of surpassing; but I can hardly believe that anybody who has read this chapter intelligently should, for two minutes, while wide awake, imagine that a sum of transpositions should itself be a transposition. For all possible transpositions are represented by single letters; and it is the very idea of a multiple algebra that no polynomial involving unlike letters can be a mere multiple of one letter.
