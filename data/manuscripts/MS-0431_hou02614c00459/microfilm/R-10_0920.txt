[pn]70[/pn] (3)

If [u]x[/u] is V, so is [u]x̄[/u].
If [u]x̄[/u] is V, so is [u]x[/u].

But these can be regarded as consequences of the two principles following:

(4) [del](1)[/del] [u]x[/u] and [u]x̄[/u] cannot both be V.
(5) [del](2)[/del] Either [u]x[/u] or [u]x̄[/u] must be V.

For the "cannot" and "must" imply that this is so, whatever term of the algebra be substituted for [u]x[/u]. Consequently, putting [u]x̄[/u] in place of [u]x[/u] in (4) and (5), we have

(6) [del](3)[/del] [u]x̄[/u] and [u]x̿[/u] cannot both be V.
(7) [del](4)[/del] Either [u]x̄[/u] or [u]x̿[/u] must be V.

Now from (4) and (7), the truth of (2) follows; and from (5) and (6), the truth of (3) follows. For if [u]x[/u] is V, then, by (4) [u]x̄[/u] is not V, in which case, by (7), [u]x̿[/u] is V; and if [u]x̄[/u] is V, then, by (6), [u]x̿[/u] [del]or[/del] is not V, in which case, by (5), [u]x[/u] is V.

Propositions (6) and (7) may be regarded as defining the status, V being any individual and definite object.
