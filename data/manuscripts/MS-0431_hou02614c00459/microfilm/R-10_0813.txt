that, if $Q$ is an idempotent quantity, so is $1-Q$. For
$(1-Q)^2 = 1 - 2Q + Q^2 = 1 - 2Q + Q = 1 - Q$.
In the present case, $1$ is, to all intents and purposes, unity; that
is, multiplied by or into any quantity, it gives that quantity.
Hence, if $Q$ is one of our idempotents $1-Q$ is necessarily
another. Of course, zero is idempotent, and therefore, two of
our eight idempotents are $0$ and $1-0$, or $1$. The other six
are simply given by
$Q = \frac{1}{3}(g^0 l + g^1 m + g^2 n)$,
where $g$ is any cube root of unity, real or imaginary. The
idempotents are $Q$ and $1-Q$, $Q$ having [ins]any[/ins] one of the three
values so given. We may distinguish the different $Q$s as $Q_0, Q_1, Q_2$,
where $Q_0$ will be the one involving the real cube root.

Let us now form the multiplication table of our $Q$s.
It is obvious that since $Q^2 = Q$ gives $(Q-1)Q = Q(Q-1) = 0$,
we have $Q(1-Q) = (1-Q)Q = 0$ for each of the $Q$s. Mul-
tiplying two [del]$Q$s we have$[/del] [del]$\frac{1}{3}l + \frac{1}{3^2}(g^0+g^1+g^2)l^2 + \frac{1}{3^2}(g^0+g^1+g^2)lm + \text{etc.}$[/del]
$\frac{1}{9}(g^0 l + g^1 m + g^2 n)(g^0 l + g^1 m + g^2 n) = \frac{1}{9} [del]l[/del] [ins]3[/ins] g^0 l$.
