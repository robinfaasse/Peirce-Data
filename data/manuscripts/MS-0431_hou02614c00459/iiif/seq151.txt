(4) [ins](4)[/ins] x and x̅ cannot both be V.
(5) [ins](5)[/ins] Either x or x̅ [del]is v[/del] must be V.
For the "cannot" and "must" imply that this is so, whatever
term of the algebra be substituted for x. Consequently, putting x̅
in place of x in (4) and (5), we have,
(6) [ins](6)[/ins] x̅ and x̅̅ cannot both be V.
(7) [ins](7)[/ins] Either x̅ or x̅̅ must be v.
Now from (4) and (7), the truth of (2) follows; and from
(5) and (6), the truth of (3) follows. For if x is V, then, by (4)
x̅ is not V, in which case, by (7), x̅̅ is V; and if x̅̅ is V,
then, by (6), x̅ [del]can[/del] is not V, in which case, by (5), x is V.
Propositions (6) and (7) may be regarded as defining the
obelus, V being any individual and definite object.
