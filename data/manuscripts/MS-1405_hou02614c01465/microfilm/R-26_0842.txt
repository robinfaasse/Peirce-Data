[pn]p. 56. note.[/pn] He ought to have stated the very reasonable opinion
of the highest authority, Brugsch, about the expression
[u]baa'npet[/u]. It is literally iron of heaven. For though Schivartz
doubts whether [u]baa'[/u] in a particular prescription in the
medical papyrus means iron or not, there proof is overwhelming
that it did often mean that in the old empire. Originally
it meant "strong." Brugsch thinks the term [u]ba'npet[/u] was
originally applied to meteoric iron, then to any very good
iron, finally to any iron. He says [u]benipe[/u] means iron in
Coptic.

As for [u]resmehitba'[/u], that is, "iron of the north and south,"
Mr. Terrien de Lacouperie is the only person I ever heard
of who thought that had anything to do with the magnet.
There can be not the slightest doubt about the correct reading
nor about the translation "iron of the north and south." It
occurs in the account of the mummification of King Unas.
Two little bars of "iron of the north and south" are inserted
between the teeth and two little lumps of "butter of the
north and butter of the south" are inserted between the
lips. As he was King of the North and South, there
seems sufficient appropriateness in this. Presumably
one bar came (or they pretended it came) from the
North and the other from the South. It is most charac-
teristic of Terrien de la Couperie to take this single passage
and say in the teeth of everything we know about Egypt
that it meant [del]"must have"[/del] [ins]"undoubtedly"[/ins] (or some strong expression
which I dont remember) the lode-stone. The Egyptians would
not have put any imported thing between the teeth of King Unas.
