[u]The Nature of our Knowledge of the Infinite[/u] [marginalia][date]1859 Oct. 23.[/date][/marginalia]

[pn]B[/pn]
I have shown [u]1859 July 25.[/u] that all things are apprehended by us; still we [del]dare[/del] [ins]are[/ins] not conscious of the infinite. To solve this paradox.

"The unthought is not," I said. I meant the potentially unthought;—the unthinkable. I did not intend of course to advance the doctrine that when we sleep all things are annihilated; because when we wake we are convinced that they have been.

The infinite therefore is thinkable; still we cannot think it in an instant or in all time.

This, then, is the nature of the infinite. Its thinkability gives it existence, while our impotence makes it unthinkable.

Still, this paradox remains. If we cannot think the infinite how can we have a thought in which it is introduced? Here the cases are two. 1[sup]st[/sup] where we have the same idea of the infinite as of the finite 2[sup]d[/sup] where we have a different idea of it.

A case of the first is where we say half an infinitely long line is half the original length of the line. Here we merely introduce the premise that infinity is a degree—part of the definition of infinity.
