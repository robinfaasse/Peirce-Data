[u]The Rule[/u].

Consider whether the meaning of the principal verb [ins]or person[/ins] does not imply impediments tending to prevent the action its signifies from causing the state of things denoted by the infinitive, and whether these impediments are of an emotional [del]in[/del] kind, not due to a deliberate act of the will, or not.

If no such impediments are implied, no preposition is used;
if the resistance is of an emotional kind, [u]à[/u] is used;
if the resistance is of any other kind, [u]de[/u] is used.

The following
[u]Classification of the Significations of the Principal Verbs[/u]
will show how the rule is to be applied.

[u]Class I[/u]. The signification of the principal verb permits its subject to be different from that of the infinitive.

[u]Family 1[/u]. The principal verb expresses something done because that which the infinitive denotes is supposed to have happened. Since it is metaphysically impossible that a cause should be subsequent to its effect, the rule requires [u]de[/u].

[u]Examples[/u].
Voët accusait Descartes [u]d'être[/u] un athée.
Il s'accuse [u]d'avoir[/u] tué le temps.
La cour a convaincu Dreyfus [u]d'avoir[/u] vendu des secrets d'état.
Je le soupçonne [del]de[/del] [ins]d'avoir[/ins] [del]détourné[/del] l'argent.
