[pn]PV[/pn] [u]MNEMONIC RULE[/u]
[u]for the use of [del]à[/del] [ins]à[/ins] and de with French infinitives following personal verbs.[/u]

Historically, various principles have determined the different constructions of different verbs, and the action of those principles has been largely fortuitous, so that any single rule covering all cases must necessarily be artificial and either too complicated to be remembered or else vague. Nevertheless, if judiciously founded on psychological principles, it may be a great aid to the memory. It is as if the locks on the doors of a large house were of various makes. Any one skeleton key that would open them all would fit many of them very imperfectly, and require coaxing in order to work at all. Still, if one could manage to open all the doors with it, one might be only too glad to have it. In the same way, the rule I am about to give will only fit some cases by some bending. Yet I trust it will help those who learn to use it.

[u]Preliminary Explanations[/u].

1. The rule is not designed to apply (a) to impersonal verbs, nor (b) to participles, nor (c) to verbs taking [u]pour[/u], [u]par[/u], [u]avec[/u], etc.

2. When the principal verb is negative or pronominal but can be used in the same sense without the negation or reflexive pronoun, it takes the same construction.

3. Verbs in [del]unusual[/del] [ins]or figurative[/ins] acceptations follow the construction of their nearest [del]usual[/del] [ins]and literal[/ins] acceptations.

4. Of verbs sometimes taking [u]à[/u] and sometimes [u]de[/u], a few have widely different meanings in the two cases. In using the others famous writers violate every possible rule. Yet I believe one construction would by nice care be preferred to the other in most cases.
