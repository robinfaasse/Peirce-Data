To the Editor of The Nation.

Sir:

Will you kindly permit me to correct the three following statements about what I say in my 'Logic', reviewed in your issue of [date]Sept. 18[/date].

1. "He declares his intention of treating logic in a thoroughly objectivistic manner." The reviewer explains [del]—[/del] if I understand him aright — that this means setting up an empirical criterion of truth as distinguished from an a priori or rationalistic criterion.[del]/// //////[/del]

2. "He talks of 'the three laws of thought' in the good old way, the somewhat obsolescent way".

3. "He speaks of 'the test of Inconceivability', thus making it something to be trusted to."


1. I did not declare any intention with reference to logic in general, but only with reference to deduction; the term I used was not "objectivistic", [del]y[/del] but "objective"; and I was not talking about tests of truth at all, but only explaining that in presenting the ordinary matter of deduction I had tried to talk as much as possible about the relations of things and as little as possible about the arrangement of the words in which we speak of them: e.g., that instead of saying that a person commits the fallacy of undistributed middle in the second figure I had merely said that he makes the blunder of supposing that [ins]two[/ins] things [del]d[/del] must be identical [del]merely[/del] because they are similar.

2.
