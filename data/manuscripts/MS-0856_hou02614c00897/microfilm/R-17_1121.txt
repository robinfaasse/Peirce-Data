[pn]15[/pn] [fig]pointing hand icon[/fig] A law must unite two logical
characters that are ordinarily, and
in their own natures inconsistent, or at least
opposed. Namely, it must, on the one hand,
be entirely [u]general[/u], and yet, on the other hand,
it must be perfectly [u]precise[/u] and [u]explicit[/u] in
its requirements, not allowing a deviation
by a hair's-breadth. Now in order that this
should be realized, that the requirement of
the law, though applying to all cases, how-
ever utterly unlike they may be, should
nevertheless be [del]carried out[/del] without the
smallest deviation, it is necessary that
there should be something in the circum-
stances of each case which can serve as
an exact pattern or standard for the
exact conformity of the action to the law,
and the law must not require anything
that there will not be an exact pattern or
standard for in all circumstances. To ren-
der this clear, I will illustrate it in two
examples. Suppose there is a Law according
to which one of any two bodies receives an ac-
celeration regardless of any other body. Then,
