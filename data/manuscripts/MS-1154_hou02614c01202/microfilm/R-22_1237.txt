A yellowish-orange [ins]pigment[/ins] mixed with a yellowish-green [ins]pigment[/ins] will not give a true yellow; but it will give an olive or buff color which may be imitated by mixing a yellow pigment with lamp-black.

If, instead of pigments, two lights be mixed, by being projected upon the same spot on a screen, the color of the mixed light, unlike the color of the [del]mixed[/del] pigments, depends entirely and accurately upon the colors of the constituent parts, quite irrespective of the wavelengths of the waves which may compose them. By mixing the green of the spectrum, near the line E, with the red of the spectrum, any of the intermediate colors can be imitated; [del]only[/del] the mixtures will be a little less chromatic than the pure spectral colors. By mixing the same green with the violet-blue any of the intermediate colors may be produced. On the other hand, nothing [ins]much[/ins] like the green of the spectrum can be produced by mixing the yellow and blue. The mixture will be very nearly white; and according to the precise yellow and blue taken, may be greenish or may be rose-colored. In consequence of these facts, red, green, and violet blue, [del]may[/del] ought to be regarded as the fundamental colors by the mixture of which all colors can be produced. A vermilion, a Schweinfurth green, and an artificial ultramarine are often used as standard colors; so that colors may be defined by the proportions of those three required to be mixed in a rotating disk [ins]in order[/ins] to match the color spoken of.
