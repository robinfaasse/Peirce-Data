<u>Chapter I. Thinking as cerebration.</u>

Can mind be defined in terms of mechanics? If the mind will do something that a machine cannot do, since mechanics can describe in general terms what machines are capable of doing, it should be able to describe what [del]the[/del] effects mental action produces that machines cannot produce. A man is sitting in church listening to the sermon, in complete quiescence: the thunders of the pulpit affect his action not the least: but somebody comes behind him and whispers some faint sounds, "Your house is on fire", the man is instantly roused to energetic muscular work. There is, in the first place, no relation between the mechanical energy of the whisper and that of the muscular contractions; but this is often the case in pure mechanical action. Thus, [ins]a[/ins] slender wooden [del]cylinders[/del] [ins]is[/ins] sometimes set up on end to detect earthquake-shocks; but, if it falls, it falls with the same force, whether the overturning shock was less or more violent. So a piece o phosphorus burns with the same energy whether the fricton that set it on fire were less or greater, so long as it was sufficient.
