[u]Greek Pronunciation[/u]

I will here give a system of pronunciation, I believe it to be a closer approximation to the ancient pronunciation [del]than has yet been given, and[/del] let it be remembered that I have gained my spurs in the matter of phonetics, having in 1864 given an account of English pronunciation in Shakespeare's day, in sharp conflict with current views, but as confirmed in the main by the later researches of Alexander J. Ellis, and of the few particulars in which he differs from me I can show him in at least half I was right. For since 1864 I have learned much both about phonetics and [del]about[/del] reasoning. In forty years I must have been a sad dunce not to have done so.

All this I insist upon because I cannot now give the reasons for the pronunciation I believe to have been the ancient [del]one[/del] [ins]original[/ins]. A book would be needed to contain them. I shall merely [del]not[/del] [ins]set[/ins] down formations of a theory which is founded upon quite independent facts. I should not care to set down, even for my future self, a thing without proofs, had it not at any rate a certain value, even if it does not represent the pronunciation that the ancients actually used. For it will be found to be quite fully worth the trouble of learning it, for the sake the understanding it certainly does afford of Greek, — of Greek poetry, especially, but also in much larger measure than one would anticipate, of Greek prose.

The first thing to bear in mind in learning this system is that the quantities must be strictly preserved. The logaoedic rhythm, I suppose, came nearest to the rhythm of prose. Say the Eupolidean verse, or perhaps still better the Laconic verse which should be read in walking, so that the left foot comes down upon each principal arsis of a dipody.
