αι had also two sounds, the one more or less like German [u]ei[/u], more distinctly diphthongal than our [u]i[/u] long. The other rather like our [u]i[/u] long when it is much shortened as in [u]satisfied[/u] rapidly pronounced. Say in a light way "It was a mightily satisfied sleigh ride party." Here the last syllable of satisfied and that of sleigh-ride will be short sounds something between the [u]er[/u] of [u]butter[/u] and the [u]i[/u] of [u]rid[/u]. οι had also two sounds. The long one was closer than our [u]oi[/u]. The short one was something between German [u]ö[/u] and our [u]i[/u] in [u]pin[/u], with a hint of a diphthongal sound.
In ᾳ, ῃ, ῳ, the ι was [del]dis[/del] heard, so that they were quite different from α, η, ω, though nearer those sounds than to αι, ει, οι.
υι was distinctly a long diphthong
αυ differed little from our [u]ow[/u] in [u]owl[/u].
ευ must have approached the sound it has in Modern Greek, the υ being very close indeed.
The rough breathing was a strong aspiration, more or less like Spanish [u]j[/u].
π was like our [u]p[/u]. β was less explosive, the lips very slightly pressed together, so as to give a Spanish effect in many words. φ was not a true aspirate, as in soap-holder. Nor was it quite a spirant as in modern Greek. It began usually with a slight explosion but passed into a spirant. But this excessively slight.
After σ as in Σάπφω, it was a pure spirant. μ was our [u]m[/u].
τ was the Italian [u]t[/u], which might be used in pronouncing English without attracting much remark. δ was [del]made[/del] with the same organs but with very slight pressure so as often to sound Spanish-like. θ was more like Spanish [u]c[/u] before [u]e[/u] or [u]i[/u] than like our [u]th[/u].
