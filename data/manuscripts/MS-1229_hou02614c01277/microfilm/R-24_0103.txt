ω must have been nearly like our [u]aw[/u] and while o was a true o-sound.
υ in diphthongs probably approximated to the modern Greek pronunciation. And I am inclined to suspect was much like our u in impute, that would explain its rough breathing.
α was nearly our ā, as in pane.
ου was perhaps nearly German ö. [ins]This is doubtful because it is not a Mediterranean vowel.[/ins] But η was like French ê or our e in there. But ε was an obscure sound like the German e in Deutsche. [ins]or rather French é but more obscure[/ins]
ρ was a somewhat rolled r and [ins]ρρ[/ins] decidedly so; while ῥήτωρ had its second ρ not much stronger than these of an English speaking person who pronounces the final r's. Not quite so strong as the French r [del]at the end[/del] in bonheur.
The circumflex accent was much like our own accent (which is by no means a pure affair of stress). The acute accent on a long syllable had a [del]stron[/del] stronger element of inflection.
