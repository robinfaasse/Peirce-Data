Our English accent of words has three ingredients, 1st, intensity
of sound; 2nd, distinctness of enunciation (itself a composite
effect); 3rd, raised pitch, not rising inflection. The last is usu-
ally masked, but appears when the accented syllable belongs
to an emphatic word. It can be heard in pronouncing this:
He will bring his country to defeat. The last syllable has a
falling inflection by a high pitch, if the word defeat is
emphasized. The Greek accent has the same ingredients, but
that of loudness is hardly more marked than in the French of the
old [u]Comédie française[/u]. Although the accent is very marked
it is a small matter compared with the quantity, which must
appear to be the principal thing. I mean that the pronunci-
ation must be such as to give it this prominence.

The Greek was so habituated to eleven types of ac-
centuation, that nothing differently accented [del]sounded[/del]
[del]to[/del] him as a word; and in learning the [del]present[/del] system of
pronunciation here described, the first step should be to
read iambic trimeters of Sophocles while walking so
that the foot shall come down at the arsis (the left foot
at the first arsis of each dipody) while observing the
quantities and accents, until the ear is so thoroughly
habituated to the eleven types that it shall seem diffi-
cult to pronounce any other type, or at least, altogether
strange. The eleven types are as follows:
