Association is of two kinds. For, on the one hand, it may be a natural
disposition, which was from birth destined to develop itself whatever the
[del]child's[/del] outward experiences might be, so long as he was not maimed
nor virtually maimed, say by being imprisoned. This sort of association
by virtue of which certain kinds of ideas become naturally allied, as
[u]crimson and scarlet[/u], is called [u]association by resemblance[/u]. The name
is not a good one, since it implies that the resemblance causes the as-
sociation, while in point of fact it is the association which constitutes
the resemblance. In themselves considered any two sense-qualities are what
they are to themselves alone and have no relation to one another. But
could they be compared by a mind that [del]had no personal[/del] [ins]brought no[/ins] tinge of its own
nature into the comparison, any two ideas [del]would[/del] would appear somewhat
alike and somewhat different. But the human mind attaches a peculiar
value and emphasis to some resemblances, and that consists in this, that
when one [ins]quality[/ins] is brought vividly to consciousness, others will at once have
their vividness increased, some more, some less. Thus, [ins]an idea which[/ins]
may be roughly compared to a composite photograph surges up into vividness,
and this composite idea maybe called a [u]general idea[/u]. It is not properly
a [u]conception[/u]; because a conception is not an idea at all, but a [u]habit[/u].
But the repeated occurrence of a general idea and the experience of its
utility, [del]goes to or tends to[/del] results in the formation or strengthening
of that habit which is the conception; or if the conception is already a
habit thoroughly compacted, the general idea is the [u]mark[/u] of the habit.
