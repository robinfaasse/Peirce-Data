[ins]The first of these is[/ins] [del]only one[/del] [ins]Any,[/ins] meaning that no matter what object be taken of the class, the intention is to assert the verb of that; as when we say, "Any man is mortal;" that is, "take any man you like, and I mean to say that man is mortal." The second indispensible selective pronoun is Some, meaning that a selection from the class can be made so that the verb shall be true of it; as when we say "Some man has been snatched up to heaven, without dying;" that is, "Among men, one, at least, can be chosen such that that man is [del]snatched[/del] translated." The traditional logic recognizes this distinction; and on the basis of it divides ordinary propositions into the universal and the particular.
