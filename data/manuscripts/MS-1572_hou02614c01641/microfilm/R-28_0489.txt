In thinking about relations great confusion often springs from the complications of "some" and "every." When we speak of "some A" we mean that out of the class A an individual is to be suitably chosen. When we speak of "every A," or "any A", we mean the [del]assertion[/del] will hold good for no matter what A. In complicated cases, it is often well to speak and think as if the individuals were taken out of their classes, either suitably, or no matter how, before saying what we have to say of them. It is then to be noticed that if we speak of "some A" and "every B" in the same assertion, we [del]make[/del] [ins]assert[/ins] more if we select the A before selecting the B, than if we reverse the order of selection.

"Some man knows all languages"

asserts much more than

"Every language is known by some man."

The difference is that the former says, a man can be suitably selected, so that taking any language, no matter what, that man knows that language. The latter only says, taking any language, no matter what, after the selection has been made known, a man can be suitably selected, so that that man knows that language.
