The chief objection to the theory of Stuart Mill is, that it is demonstrable that reasoning from a sample to the class [del]of[/del] from which it has been drawn at random would be generally valid, whatever the constitution of the universe might be. Nothing, for instance, could be more irregular than the result of tossing up a penny; and yet if a penny be tossed up a hundred times, this sample would lead us almost invariably to infer that the penny would come up heads and tails about equally often. It is true that this is proved true by means of numbers; and Mill regards the laws of numbers, themselves, as special facts which might be imagined to be false. But his attempt to specify how they could be false [del]was[/del] [ins]is[/ins] not successful. He says we might imagine a world in which, whenever two things were put together, a third [del]necessarily[/del] [ins]invariably[/ins] sprang into existence. But this is no more than frequently happens in the actual world. When we put side by side a lighted candle and an unlighted one, a third thing, to wit the shadow of the latter, invariably appears.
