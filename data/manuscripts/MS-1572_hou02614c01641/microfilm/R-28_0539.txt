I. Retroduction, or abduction. This is otherwise
called reasoning from consequent to antecedent. It
consists in studying the [del]k[/del] known facts with long
absorption until at length a [u]conjecture[/u], explanatory
of them, presents itself with great persuasiveness.

II. Deduction; otherwise, necessary reasoning,
although it may be reasoning about probability. It
has been disguised as syllogism, which is one of the
simpler forms of it, so dressed as quite to conceal
its real [del]nature[/del], which we shall examine first of all.

III. Induction, or experimental [del]reason[/del] inquiry. The
nature of this too has been generally completely turned
inside out, although it has been rightly practised, perhaps
as often as wrongly. It does not like retroduction
pass from the facts to the theory. On the contrary, it
takes the theory obtained by retroduction, applies to it
the processes of deduction, & finds that if that theory
be correct such & such facts ought to be observable
whereupon it examines the facts to see how nearly
they agree with the theory & draws a provisional
conclusion, Provisional since the inductive process
(in its highest form at least) can never be completed
unless to the total overthrow of the retroductive
conclusion.
