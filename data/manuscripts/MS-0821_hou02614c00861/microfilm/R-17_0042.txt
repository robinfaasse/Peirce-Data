[u]Greater & Less[/u]

He defines as follows:
If No part of $M$ is equal to $N$
But a part [del]$N_1$[/del] of $N$ is equal to $M$
the same thing is true of $M' \sim M$ and $N' \sim N$. This, therefore,
is a relation of multitude.

Then $M$ and $N$ are not equal, for since then $M \sim N$
and $N' \sim M$ it follows the $N' \sim N$; and since $M \sim N$
there is a part of $M$ say $M'$ which is [del]$M' \sim N'$[/del] [ins]$M' \sim N$[/ins] and
consequently [del]M' \sim N[/del] [ins]$M' \sim N$[/ins] [del]which[/del] contrary
to hypothesis.

He writes
(1) $a = \overline{\overline{M}}$ $b = \overline{\overline{N}}$ $a < b$ $b > a$
and it is easily proved that
(2) if $a < b$ & $b < c$ then $a < c$
And if $P_1$ is part of $P$ and $a < \overline{\overline{P_1}}$ then $a < \overline{\overline{P}}$
and [del]conversely[/del] if $\overline{\overline{P}} < b$ then $\overline{\overline{P_1}} < b$
He then proves that the three relations
$a = b$ $a < b$ $a > b$
are mutually exclusive.

But that one or other always holds is in this place
"kaum zu beweisen."
