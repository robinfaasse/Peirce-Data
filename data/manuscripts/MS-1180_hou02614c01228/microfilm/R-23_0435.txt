that where there is no reason to the contrary, the odds are about 5 to 1 that a
word in [u]ea[/u] will have the vowel long. This tendency has been sufficiently
strong to produce dialectical or subdialectical variations of pronunciation
in the following words, all of which had the vowel long in Anglo-Saxon: [u]bread[/u],
[u]breath[/u], [u]dead[/u], [u]death[/u], [u]dread[/u], [u]head[/u], [u]reach[/u] (retch), [u]ready[/u], [u]thread[/u]. Of these words,
[u]bread[/u] has the vowel prolonged or drawled by Americans. Salisbury, about
1550, pronounced it short; but Cheke, 1550, Smith, 1568, Gil, 1619, Coote, 1624, all
drawled the vowel, probably even more than we Americans do; for we only
make it half-long, while they call it downright long. In fact, this drawled sound
was the regular pronunciation of [u]ea[/u] from the time when this spelling was adopted
down into the 18[u]th[/u] century. The word [u]breath[/u] was pronounced short in Middle English,
and by all the early orthoëpists who mention it, Bullokar, 1580, Gil, 1619, Butler, 1633.
The Oxford Dictionary is therefore mistaken in asserting (without offering any evidence)
that the vowel has only "recently been shortened," though we may concede that the long
pronunciation was still lingered in the 16[u]th[/u] century, since our spelling seems to support
it. But it must have belonged to an archaic subdialect. Shakespeare twice rhymed
[u]breath[/u], [u]death[/u], [u]bequeath[/u], which would perhaps be made perfect rhymes by [del]prolon[/del] [ins]drawling[/ins]
the vowel of [u]breath[/u], though we have no evidence concerning [u]bequeath[/u]. The word [u]dead[/u]
was sometimes short even in Anglo-Saxon. In Middle English it was as often short as
long. Of the orthoëpists, Smith, 1568, drawled the vowel; Gil, 1619, states that it was sometimes
drawled, sometimes shortened; Butler, 1633, marks it drawled in one passage, but short
in others. Shakespeare seems to make it short when he writes
So thou hast but lost the dregs of life,
The prey of wormes, my body being dead,
The coward conquest of a wretches knife,
To late of thee to be remembered.
