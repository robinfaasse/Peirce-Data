[u]Breath[/u], as we shall see, was short. As to [u]bequeath[/u], evidence is [ins]somewhat deficient, but I have no doubt it was short, as it was in Anglo-Saxon, and is now in some dialects. [del]And so is [u]bequeath[/u] sometimes.[/del][/ins] [del]the earliest:[/del]

[u]Lysander[/u]. And here with all good will, with all my heart,
In [u]Hermias[/u] love I yeeld you up my part;
And yours of [u]Helena[/u], to me bequeath,
Whom I do love, and will do to my death.
[u]Helena[/u]. Never did mockers wast more idle breth.

The spelling of [u]breth[/u] in the First Folio shows how the proof-reader would have pronounced the rhymes.

No more that [u]Thane[/u] of [u]Cawdor[/u] shall deceive
Our Bosome interest: Goe pronounce his present [u]death[/u],
And with his former Title greet [u]Macbeth[/u].

[ins]This is perhaps the most persuasive of all the passages. For if the first syllable of [u]deceive[/u] had the drawled sound of [u]ea[/u], and it is difficult to believe that Shakespeare's ear would have tolerated it where it stands had [u]death[/u] been drawled likewise.[/ins]

Have I not reason (Beldams) as you are?
[del]Saucy, and over-bold, how did[/del]
To Trade, and Trafficke with [u]Macbeth[/u],
In Riddles, and Affaires of [u]death[/u].

It is true that Shakespeare rhymes [u]Macbeth[/u] with [u]heath[/u]. But then the historic quantity of [u]heath[/u] is very doubtful. [del]So[/del] Pope, for example, has

Oft, as in airy wings they skim the [u]heath[/u],
The clamorous lapwings feel the leaden [u]death[/u].

[u]Beattie[/u] has the same rhyme. Drayton, who was just about Shakespeare's age, has

Now at the last gasp of love's latest [u]breath[/u],
When his pulse failing, passion speechless lies,
When faith is kneeling by his bed of [u]death[/u],
And innocence is closing up his eyes, etc.

Attributed to Shakespeare is

Come away, come away, [u]Death[/u],
And in sad cyres let me be laid;
Fly away, fly away, [u]breath[/u];
I am slain by a fair cruel maid.
