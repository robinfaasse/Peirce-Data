My dear Charles;-

I'll do my best to see that your vouchers are assimilated to the precedent of Newberry or some other member of the N.A.

Far be it from me to say that a better knowledge of the constitution of matter will not throw light on cleavage and many other things. It has seemed to me that the centre of inertia of physical investigation in the 20th century would fall within the limits of the constitution of matter.

I'm not so sure that the question now at issue cannot be discussed satisfactorily without any intimate knowledge of the constitution of matter. That some real matter approaches closely to the ideal of isotropy is certain and even if there were no actual case of this kind the thinking mind would interpolate it. In an isotropic mass with an elastic limit, flow must begin on the surfaces of maximum tangential strain. If any change of properties is induced by the strain, it must be on those surfaces. The change must manifest itself either as an increase or as a decrease in the facility of cleavage, so that experience need be called in only to determine whether a cube compressed in one direction is more or less cleavable than before compression.
