84 Broad St. 1897 [date]Feb 22[/date]

My dear Ferdinand:

I have received your note. I am sorry the Survey won't go on, as I have become much interested in the question of cleavage of rocks. However, I shall soon have money of my own and shall then resolve the question by experiments. What the true explanation may be I cannot tell, and though I can make guesses enough, none of them seems to me to have much weight, if any. But of one thing there is no room for doubt, and that is, that your argument has absolutely no weight even as a probable argument. It is very alluring, no doubt, and as long as I gave that degree of thought to it that a man will give to a subject quite beyond the sphere of his concern, I was completely deceived by it. But as soon as I looked into it critically, I saw that it was neither a demonstrative nor a probable argument. It just leaves the question where it was. I did not put it quite so bluntly in my report, and perhaps you would have been better satisfied if I had. But that is just what I think of it, and if you think my opinion about an argument of any value, if I [del]was[/del] [ins]were[/ins] you I would just chuck it overboard.

It may be that as Van Hise thinks, it has something to do with the formation of crystals. But if so, it can only be for the reason given by me, and there should be a relation between the cleavages and the compressibility.

Meantime, if I can get a suitable material, I shall see whether I cannot produce cleavage. Nobody seems to have microscopically examined [del]rock[/del] [ins]or much[/ins] cleavage [del]had been produced, to see how the crystals were arranged. I mean to go to the bottom of the subject.[/del]

Yours faithfully, C. S. Peirce
