To make the formula necessary, then, there must be something
that prevents all the antecedentals from being true at once, in case the
consequental is false. For example, if one of the antecedentals be
identical with the consequental, this is effected.

Let us write the formula thus:
$\overline{P_1} | \overline{P_2} | \overline{P_3} | \dots \overline{P_n} | A$.

Each of the antecedentals $P_1, P_2, P_3$, etc. is in itself a
formula having its consequental and its $0, 1, 2,$ or more
antecedentals. Thus we can write
$P_1 = \overline{Q_{11}} | \overline{Q_{12}} | \overline{Q_{13}} | \dots \overline{Q_{1n_1}} | B_1$,
$P_2 = \overline{Q_{21}} | \overline{Q_{22}} | \overline{Q_{23}} | \dots \overline{Q_{2n_2}} | B_2$,
etc etc.

The $Q$'s may be called the <u>second antecedentals</u> of the
original formula, and the $B$'s the <u>second consequentals</u>. The
$B$s are single letters; but the $Q$s are consequences which
may be written
$Q_{11} = \overline{R_{111}} | \overline{R_{112}} | \overline{R_{113}} | \dots \overline{R_{11m_{11}}} | C_{11}$
$Q_{21} = \overline{R_{211}} | \overline{R_{212}} | \overline{R_{213}} | \dots \overline{R_{21m_{21}}} | C_{21}$
etc. etc. etc.
