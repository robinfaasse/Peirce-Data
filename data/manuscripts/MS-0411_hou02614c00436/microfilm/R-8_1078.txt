These definitions are modern. The premises are called [u]major[/u] and [u]minor[/u] after the extremes they respectively contain. The principle of the validity of rule Barbara has several names. It is called the [u]Nota notae[/u], when stated "Nota notae est nota rei ipsius", or the mark of a mark is the mark of the subject, translating Aristotle's words: "Ὅταν ἕτερον καθ' ἑτέρου κατηγορῆται ὡς καθ' ὑποκειμένου, ὅσα κατὰ τοῦ κατηγορουμένου λέγεται, πάντα καὶ κατὰ τοῦ ὑποκειμένου ῥηθήσεται ([u]Praedic.[/u] iii. p. 1 b 10.) It is called the [u]Dictum de omni[/u] when stated in the form, "Whatever is true of all, is true of each," or as Aristotle awkwardly expresses it, λέγομεν δὲ τὸ κατὰ παντός κατηγορεῖσθαι, ὅταν μηδὲν ᾖ λαβεῖν τῶν τοῦ ὑποκειμένου, καθ' οὗ θάτερον οὐ λεχθήσεται· καὶ τὸ κατὰ μηδενὸς ὡσαύτως. ([u]Anal. pr.[/u] I. i. p 24 b 28.) It is called the [u]regula de quocunque[/u] when expressed [del]to[/del] [ins]in[/ins] hypotheticals. But De Morgan's way of expressing it ([u]On the Syllogism[/u], No II. [u]Cambridge Philosophical Transactions[/u], IX. 104) is the best; when he says the copula is [u]transitive[/u], and expresses a transitive relation. It may also be expressed as above as a substitution; inference has for ages been so regarded. It may also be conceived as a rule for eliminating the middle term between the premises, as an unknown quantity is eliminated between two equations.
