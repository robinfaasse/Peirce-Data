* By a syllogism (Aristotle, Anal. pr. I. i. p. 24 b 18.) is meant [del]an argumentation by[/del] [del]a necessary conclusion[/del] [ins]an argumentation[/ins] which [del]some[/del] from two [del]some[/del] premises something not implied in [del]any[/del] [ins]either[/ins] one of them is concluded as logically necessary. The word [u]Barbara[/u] is one of a series of names for species of syllogism which will be explained in another chapter. Barbara is ordinarily understood to be stated in categorical form thus:

[ins]Every[/ins] [del]All[/del] Q is R;
[ins]Every[/ins] [del]All[/del] P is Q;
<span style="padding-left: 30px;">∴ [ins]Every[/ins] [del]All[/del] P is R.</span>

But there is no [del]real[/del] difference for the logician between a categorical and a hypothetical proposition. The above is the same as

If anything is Q, it is R;
If anything is P, it is Q;
<span style="padding-left: 30px;">∴ If anything is P, it is R.</span>

A syllogism has three propositions, its two premises and the conclusion; and usually three terms, the antecedents and consequents of these propositions. [del]In Barbara,[/del] Of these terms, the one which does not appear in the conclusion is called the [u]middle term[/u]. The one which is consequent of the conclusion is called the [u]major [del]term[/del] [ins]extreme[/ins][/u]. It is no doubt so called because its degree of veritability is (in the case of Barbara) as great or greater than that of the antecedent of the conclusion, which is called the [u]minor extreme[/u].
