§102. [del]The one property[/del] [ins]One of the two properties[/ins] of denial is, that from the denial of A it follows that from A follows [del]a[/del] certain consequents. That is, from the denial of [ins]non-[/ins]A and A taken together, follow certain consequents. These consequents are any propositions we please, including the impossible and absurd. In other words, the assumption that non-A and A are true, at once, is absurd. This is called the [u]principle of contradiction[/u].*

* [del]“τὸ γὰρ αὐτὸ ἅμα ὑπάρχειν τε καὶ μὴ ὑπάρχειν ἀδύνατον τῷ αὐτῷ καὶ κατὰ τὸ αὐτό.”[/del] [ins]“(Metaph. Γ. 3. p. 1005 b 19. “The same thing cannot at once be present and not present in the same subject in the same sense.””[/ins] It is enunciated by Aristotle. Some have thought to find it even earlier; but a mere accidental assertion of a thing like this, which only escapes notice by its excessive obviousness, is of no importance. [del]So[/del] Men have even thought to find it in the mouth of King Lear. “Y and no, too, was no good Divinity.” But this is a misunderstanding. He says: “They flatter’d me like a Dogge.... To say I and no to everything that I said I and no to, was no good Divinity.” Aristotle [del]terms[/del] [ins]entitles[/ins] the principle “[u]πασῶν βεβαιοτάτη ἀρχή[/u],” “the firmest principle of all,” and in the middle ages, it was called [del]“the[/del] [ins]“the[/ins] first principle.” [del]Ramus gave it the name[/del] Pierre de la Ramée (Ramus) the humanistic derider of Aristotle (1515-1572) [ins]following Ammonius and other Greek commentators in giving it[/ins] gave it the name “axiom of contradiction.” The Leibnizians gave it its present name. Many unclear heads call it the principle of identity.
