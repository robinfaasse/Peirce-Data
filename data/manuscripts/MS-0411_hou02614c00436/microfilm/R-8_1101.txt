The precise denial of a proposition [del]is[/del] consists in asserting what makes it false and in asserting nothing more. Suppose A [del]makes the[/del] affirms, "Tom is either a fool or a knave." Then, B, if he wishes precisely to deny this, must not say "Tom is either not a fool or not a knave;" for this would be to assert both too much and too little. It asserts too much in saying that Tom exists, herein asserting to a part of what A says, so that A and B are both [del]wrong[/del] [ins]many[/ins] if there is no such person as Tom. On the other other hand, B asserts too little, because Tom may be a fool without being a knave, or vice versa, in which case A and B may both be right. In strict logic, it always weakens a denial to say too much. Thus, if B had said, "Tom, if there is any such person, is not a fool, and is not a knave, but is a good fellow," he would then assent to something to which A would assent, namely that, "either Tom does not exist, or he is a fool, or he is a knave, or he is a good fellow." The precise denial of a proposition is called in logic the [del]contradictory[/del] [ins]contradictory[/ins] of it, or the diametrical opposite of it [del](referring to a diagram used by Aristotle)[/del], or the [del]negative[/del] [ins]negation[/ins] of it.* We also refer to the [del]negative[/del] [ins]denial[/ins] of A as non-A.

* [u]Contradictory[/u] is the regular term. The Latin word [u]contradictorius[/u] seems to have been made by Boethius (about A.D. 500) expressly for this purpose. It imitates Aristotle's word ἀντίφασις.
