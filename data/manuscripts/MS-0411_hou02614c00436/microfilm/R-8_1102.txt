But [del]besides[/del] [u]ἀντίφασις[/u] there is in Greek a second
word [u]ἀπόφασις[/u], which is often synonymous with
[u]ἀντίφασις[/u], but at other times is opposed to [u]κατάφασις[/u],
affirmation. Our word [u]negation[/u], from the classical
Latin [u]negatio[/u], is used in logic as the representative
of [u]ἀπόφασις[/u]. [u]Negative[/u] is used for [u]ἀποφατικός[/u], though
[u]negatory[/u] would have been better, [del]as well as more[/del]
[del]convenient,[/del] as well as less bad Latin. For we
can hardly avoid using [u]negative[/u] to mean contradic-
tory.

In one passage, Aristotle speaks of propositions
as [u]diametrically opposite[/u] (19b.35). He there refers
to a diagram in which he has arranged four forms
of propositions as here shown

[fig]A square of opposition diagram with crossed diagonal lines[/fig]
There is a just man / There is not a just man
There is not a not-just man / There is a not-just man

The upper left hand and lower right hand propositions,
as well as the upper right hand and lower left hand
propositions, he calls diametrically opposed (κατὰ διάμετρον).
But the same Greek phrase came to be used to signify
the relations of things the most opposed possible, [ins]and[/ins]
