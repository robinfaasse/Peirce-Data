Philosophy, during the nineteenth century, was
mainly the stamping-ground of German theolo-
gical students. I confess I never had much
interest in their philosophy. It was an attempt
to make out how the universe appears from
the Deity’s point of view. Now if Sir Isaac
Newton appeared to himself like a child that
had picked up a few pretty pebbles on the beach
of the great ocean of truth, all subsequent advance
of science has brought more and more reason
for feeling the infinitesimally fragmentary
character of our knowledge of the facts or
the principles of physics, and discourages,
for the present, all endeavors to frame any
idea of the ideas of the Deity. The fact that
