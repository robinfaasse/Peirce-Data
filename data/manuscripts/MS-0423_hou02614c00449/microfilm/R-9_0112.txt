Such an [ins]arithmoidal[/ins] series is just like the series of positive whole numbers. I call it with reference to its grade of multitude, [u]denumerable[/u]. That is, it corresponds one to one to the numbers, yet the count of it cannot be completed. To such a series applies the kind of reasoning called by me the [u]Fermatian inference[/u]. [del]It[/del] [ins]x This[/ins] consists in proving [del]such a then[/del] a proposition to be true of such a series, because otherwise it must be false of an enumerable collection, [del]which[/del] [ins]such falsity by[/ins] reasoning on the principle of the part being less than the whole, [del]shows[/del] [ins]being shown[/ins] to be impossible. Fermat himself called it [u]indefinite descent[/u]. He states his "manière de demonstrer", which he calls "une route tout à fait singulière" as consisting in showing that if the proposition to be proved were false of any number, it would be false of some smaller number. This statement shows a good comprehension of its nature.
