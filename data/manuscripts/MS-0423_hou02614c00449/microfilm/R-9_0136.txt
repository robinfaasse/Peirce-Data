[del]the[/del] many propositions (namely all that are true of the
denumerable but not of the numerable) cannot be
demonstrated in a way which will stand logical
examination unless it be expressly introduced as
a premise that a given multitude is numerable. Now
a logically necessary proposition is of no avail as a
premise. On the whole then there is nothing in logic to
prevent a region from being divided into innumerable
parts about alike.

[del]39[/del] [ins]40[/ins] Now I say that each of those parts contains innu-
merable points. For if that were not the case each of
these parts could be so arranged that every point had
another next after it; and since a continuum has no
molecular constitution the divisions could everywhere be
[ins]made[/ins] between points having other points next them; and so,
after rearranging the parts (no matter how the
continuity might be broken up) all the points would have
points next after them. But this is contrary to the fact
that the points are innumerable. Besides, going back to
the unanalyzed idea of continuity, it is evident that in a
continuum the points are so connected that every part,
irrespective of its magnitude contains innumerable
[del]parts[/del] points. It may be objected that the single points
are parts. But that is not properly true. The single points
are parts of the collection; but they cannot be broken off.
