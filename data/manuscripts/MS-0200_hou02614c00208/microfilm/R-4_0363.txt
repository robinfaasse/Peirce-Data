E 81[/pn] . Aristotle's single tree ceases altogether to be when the time
comes at which it is felled or completely dies. [del]It is then nothing[/del]
[del]at all[/del] It then loses the characters that are its essence; and although
there is a log or stump, the tree, at that time, has no being at all. Now
to assert anything of nothing is no assertion, and no state of facts
can render the assertion false. Accordingly, every predicate is
true of a tree that is no tree. The reductio ad absurdum depends
on this principle. You assume something to [ins]have certain character[/ins] [del]be true[/del]. You then prove
that the [ins]negative[/ins] [del]opposite[/del] [del]is true of[/del] of that character is true of the same
subject. These premises are true: otherwise nothing would follow
from them. But something does follow from them; namely that
that subject is impossible; it is nothing. So, of the tree that has ceased
to be a tree, and is nothing, every predicate is true. It is therefore inde-
