New York, Feb. 15, 1899.

MR. CHARLES S. PEIRCE,
Milford, Penn.

Dear Mr. Peirce:-

I have your letter of the 8th.

The negative vertical reaction in a stiffening truss is always provided for in some way or another and the usual method is to fasten down the ends. I think that probably the best way for me to explain the situation is to send you a copy of my pamphlet on Suspension Bridges. I thought I had sent this to you before, but if I have not done so I think that you will find that it explains fully the physical conditions though the calculations are of a very crude preliminary kind.

It would be out of the question to provide different tracks and different deflections for traffic passing in opposite directions. There are many reasons for this, but two are enough to govern the case; the first is the wind strains which must be resisted and which require a transverse rigidity inconsistent with the division of the bridge into two structures. The second is the demands of the railroad men who wish to be able to use each track for any purpose on emergencies.
