New York, Feb. 6, 1899.

Mr. CHARLES S. PEIRCE,
Milford, Penn.

Dear Mr. Peirce:-

I am glad to hear that the general calculation is approaching completion.

I do not think that you can consider that the stiffening truss ever carries any portion of either the dead or the live load. That is, the sum of the vertical reactions from the cables is always equal to the total weight carried; there are also vertical reactions at the ends of the stiffening truss, these reactions being negative and positive, and balancing each other. The condition which you suppose, that all trains are alike in length and weight, also implies that they are alike in position, while the principal object of a stiffening truss is to prevent disturbances by changes in the position of the live load.

All calculations that I have ever seen on suspension bridges, assume the suspenders to be inextensible; this is not really the case, but the amount of their extension is evidently pretty small.

I write to Mrs. Peirce in regard to the jewels.

Very truly yours, G. S. Morison
