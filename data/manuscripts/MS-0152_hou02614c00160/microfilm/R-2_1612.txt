[u]Section 4. Of topical geometry.[/u]
[u]Art. 11. Division 5.[/u] Every multitude is either
[u]enumerable[/u], or
[u]inenumerable but numerable[/u], or
[u]innumerable[/u].

[u]Definition 6.[/u] A multitude is said to be [u]numerable[/u] if
cardinal or ordinal numbers can be attached to all its
units, a distinct number to each unit. A multitude to which,
[ins]by reason of its vastness,[/ins] numbers cannot be so attached is said to be [u]innumerable[/u].

[u]Definition 7.[/u] A multitude the counting of which can be completed
is said to be [u]enumerable[/u]. A multitude too great for the counting
to be possibly completed is said to be [u]inenumerable[/u].

[u]Illustrations[/u]. The people of the United States are an [u]enumerable[/u]
multitude. A list of them might be drawn up. The cardinal numbers
are an example of a multitude [u]inenumerable but numerable[/u]. A
complete list of such a multitude cannot be drawn up; its [del]units[/del] [ins]units[/ins] can
only be defined by some general rule. The instants in century
or in a second form an [u]innumerable[/u] multitude; no single instant
can be [del]defined,[/del] distinguished from all others, by indicating at each
subdivision of the first lapse of time, in which section the instant in
question is found. An instant can only be distinguished from all
others by some general rule for determining it.

[u]Axiom 1.[/u] If a general precept is intelligible, it may be intel-
ligibly supposed to be completely carried out.
