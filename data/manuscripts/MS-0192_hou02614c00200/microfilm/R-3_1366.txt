[u]Art 2[/u]. We write Exp [u]x[/u] to denote the exponential of [u]x[/u].
It will be remarked that no matter how large a number [u]x[/u]
may be, the series is convergent, that is, it enables us to calculate
Exp [u]x[/u] [del]accurately[/del] [ins]to any required[/ins] number of decimal places. Thus, if [u]x[/u] = 10
the calculation is

$$\begin{aligned}
1 &= 1 \\
x &= 10 \\
\frac{1}{2} x^2 &= 50 \\
\frac{1}{3!} x^3 &= 166.6666666667 \\
\frac{1}{4!} x^4 &= 416.6666666667 \\
\frac{1}{5!} x^5 &= 833.3333333333 \\
\frac{1}{6!} x^6 &= 1388.8888888889 \\
\frac{1}{7!} x^7 &= 1984.1269841270 \\
\frac{1}{8!} x^8 &= 2480.1587301587 \\
\frac{1}{9!} x^9 &= 2755.7318628742 \\
\frac{1}{10!} x^{10} &= 2755.7318628742 \\
\frac{1}{11!} x^{11} &= 2505.2107844311 \\
\frac{1}{12!} x^{12} &= 2087.6756536926 \\
\frac{1}{13!} x^{13} &= \dots
\end{aligned}$$

Each term is now becoming more and more rapidly smaller
than the one before it. When [del]$\frac{1}{100!} x^{100}$ is reached, it will be[/del]
[del]but $\frac{1}{10}$ of the previous term[/del] [ins]$\frac{1}{1000000} x^{1000000}$[/ins] [ins]is reached, it[/ins] will be [del]to begin[/del]
[del]with[/del] [del]$\frac{1}{100000}$[/del] [ins]$\frac{1}{100000}$[/ins] of the [del]previous[/del] term [ins]before it[/ins]. Thus, eventually the terms become perfectly
insignificant, not only singly but collectively.

At the same time, when [u]x[/u] is a large number, it is plain that Exp [u]x[/u]
is immensely larger.
