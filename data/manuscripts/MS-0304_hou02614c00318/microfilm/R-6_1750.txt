[u]Lectures on Pragmatism[/u]
[u]Lecture II.[/u]
[u]On Phenomenology.[/u]

I regret that I found it impracticable to begin [del]not[/del] by a lecture on mathematics, because what I should have had to say would have been not only interesting and of great service to the student of metaphysics, but it would also have served to make the present lecture considerable more convincing; and [del]not[/del] [ins]I am so rich in[/ins] [del]no[/del] arguments [ins]that any[/ins] can be spared, I feel that. But two lectures would have been absolutely indispensable if I had [del]gone into[/del] [ins]discussed the ideas of[/ins] mathematics at all, and there would have been undue compression without three; and after all the gain to the understanding of the question of pragmatism would have been too indirect to justify such a waste of time. So those considerations are among the things that I must do without.

The [del]problem[/del] [ins]task before me[/ins] of the present lecture is to describe what is before the mind when anything is before.
