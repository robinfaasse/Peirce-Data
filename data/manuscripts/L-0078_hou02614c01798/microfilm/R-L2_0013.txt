Gentlemen:

I return you enclosed [del]here[/del]with the contract signed, with my best thanks for the courteous expressions in yours of the 11[ins]th[/ins] inst., which only reached me a few days ago.

While in Cambridge I reached an informal understanding with Prof. James that if my History of Science was ready in time, [del]he would[/del] and I would adapt it to such a purpose (as it seems to me well to do), he would use it with his classes [del]next year[/del], which will lead to its use in Western universities. I do not doubt that a good history of science to replace Whewell, and of the same instinctive and intellectual kind, will have a large sale. Of course a one volume book [del]requires[/del] [ins]must[/ins] be a literary chef d'oeuvre to amount to anything at all. Perhaps I am rash to attempt it. As you suggest that I complete the history on the scale that seems "most effective", I have written a part of what I should say about Egyptian Science on that scale. It breaks off before I have said all I should say about the mathematical papyrus; but there is perhaps enough to show how interesting it will be. You must know that I have made minute researches into a vast number of questions about this history of science, and these, recounted at some length, would have some interest; but they must be omitted in a one volume book. In what I send you, [del]my[/del] [ins]my[/ins] account of how the casing was put on the Great Pyramid & of how the places for the core stones were marked is an instance of original views with which the whole would abound.
