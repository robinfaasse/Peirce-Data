The doctrine of the quantified predicate is, strictly speaking, the doctrine that identity is the primary and fundamental relation expressed by the copula of a proposition. To that doctrine I am thoroughly opposed.

The arguments of the advocates of this position are too shallow to be of any [del]service in[/del] helping us to the truth. Indeed, I do not remember any formal argument in its favor except that of Sir William Hamilton, which is that in logic that which is implicit in thought should be made explicit in language. Now, that is said to be "implicit" in a proposition which can be necessarily inferred from that proposition. But the logic of relatives shows that an innumerable multitude of propositions can be inferred from any proposition whatever; and all of them cannot be explicitly set forth. Sir William Hamilton's postulate would abolish inference, except as a prelogical operation; but in that respect it faithfully represents the doctrine of the quantified predicate itself. It is the chief task of logic gradually to develop [del]who[/del] that which is implicit in thought and [del]to med[/del] step by step to make it explicit, or, at least, to show how to do so; and to begin by postulating that this is done, to pass by the chief purpose of logic.

But Hamilton may sometimes have confused his postulate, as stated, with this: To set forth explicitly in the forms of logic that which is necessarily explicit in the thought. Whether [del]he[/del] really did fall into this confusion, it is not easy, owing to the brevity of his papers, to determine. This amended postulate, however, leaves the [ins]question of the[/ins] quantified predicate just where it was before; for the whole question is, what is [del]necessarily[/del] explicit in thought?
