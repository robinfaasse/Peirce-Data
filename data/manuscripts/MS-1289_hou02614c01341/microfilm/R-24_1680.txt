[pn]II.[/pn]

As soon as a man experiences a longing to know the truth,
he begins to imagine what that truth can be. He very soon finds that
unrestrained imagination is sure to lead him wrong. Nevertheless,
it remains true that imagination alone, — under proper checks, —
can possibly suggest the truth. Hence, the second requisite for
the [ins]successful[/ins] pursuit of science, — coming next after the desire to learn,
is a scientific and fertile imagination. The best of the practical
men are dreamers, too; — witness N. Bonaparte; — but the things
they dream of are like those of the Turk "in his guarded tent."
The scientific man dreams of agencies by which the pheno-
mena of nature might be brought about. He dreams, not of
the possession of force, but of the law of force, etc.

[u]III[/u]

Morality means behaving according to traditional ideas. Hence
morality is essentially conservative. Conservatism becomes a
habit and extends itself to opinions, especially before
speculative and practical opinions become clearly distinguished,
that is to say, in all but the most cultured intellects. Hence, the
[del]whole[/del] [ins]of the community[/ins] moral [del]force[/del] weight will be cast against science; because
science implies a desire to learn; and a desire to learn implies
dissatisfaction with current opinions. Thus, an early de-
velopment of good morals, and still worse, of good manners,
is unfavorable to science.
