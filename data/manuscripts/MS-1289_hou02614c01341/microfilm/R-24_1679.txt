[u]Lessons of the History of Science[/u]

[del]Seem to the to be somewhat as follows:[/del]

[u]I.[/u]

If we endeavor to form our conceptions upon history and experience, we shall find that there are three main classes of men. First, there are men whose delights are sensuous, emotional, or esthetic; and they create Arts. Second, there are men who respect only [del]action and[/del] force, — and force only so far as it is exercised. These are the men of action, the practical men of this world. Third, there are men to whom nothing seems great but Thought. In force, that which they admire and which interests them is not its exercise, but its [u]Law[/u]. These are the Scientific Men. If we seek to define science, as it lives in history, and not [del]by a[/del] [ins]merely to put it into an[/ins] artificial pigeon-hole, we shall conceive it as that about which these men are busied. As such, it does not so much consist in [u]knowing[/u], nor even in "organized knowledge," as it does in diligent inquiring into truth for its own sake. No matter how little a man may know about the object of his research, from the moment he begins a sincere [ins]and earnest[/ins] questioning of nature, he will be recognized by all scientific men as a brother. It is in this sense, that this book [del]is a treat of the history of Science[/del]. The truth is Science & Philosophy have been changed in their cradles. It is what we call science that really consists in the love of learning, while what we call philosophy is apt to think it knows already all that is worth knowing. Now the very first prerequisite to the successful prosecution of Science is the desire to know the truth for its own sake, independant of its utility, and independant of its agreeability. A practical people cannot be a scientific people. It is a case of God or Mammon.
