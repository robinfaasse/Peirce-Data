Likewise we may find a general term of higher order the differences of which will produce the given original series, etc., etc. We thus have an infinite order of general terms the differences of any order producing the next lower.

This infinite series of general terms arising out of one general term has no upper limit. It may have a lower limit by the differences becoming constant.

(B) Different unit intervals under the same general term. But we may also produce an infinity of different series by changing the unit interval between terms under the same general term.

The first infinity of series consists, therefore, in passing from lower to higher general terms. The second infinity consists in passing from one unit interval between terms to another unit interval without changing the general term.

These two different classes of series constitute the bases for quite different methods of summation.

Without the three ideas—of a generic description running through all the individuals, thereby converting them into a class and binding them together; of specific descriptions or particular values discriminating the individuals from each other, and of an order as between the individuals—there could be no such things as general summation formulæ.

[u]FOUR METHODS OF SUMMATION.[/u]

Addition presents itself in arithmetic, algebra, infinitesimal calculus, mechanics, composition of forces, quarternions, and in logic, etc. We will not here seek to inquire what is that underlying element which is common to all these varied forms, though we believe that mathematics is greatly in need of this philosophic research.

We will here call attention to only four sorts of addition or summation.
