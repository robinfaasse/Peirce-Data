However, I now prepare to abandon that
argument and by examining the foundations
of reasoning to show that no argument can
be stronger than this, old and despised as it is.

I begin the analysis of the critic of
reasonings, by dividing all indecomposa-
ble reasonings into three families, the
first comprises those which evidently
[del]pursue[/del] follow [del]a[/del] methods that, rigidly pur-
sued, cannot lead to error. These I call
Deductions. They conclude nothing
that is not asserted in their premises.
I divide this family by two dichotomies
in 4 genera.

The second family comprises those indecomposable reason-
ings that follow methods that can lead into error, but which,
sufficiently persisted in, will eventually correct every error into
which they lead, as can be deductively proved. I call these Induc-
tions, and divide them into 3 genera.

The third family comprises those indecomposable reasonings that
follow methods that can lead into errors which can never correct.
But such errors can be corrected by Inductions; and Inductions war-
rants the conclusion that they always can. I call these Retro-
ductions. (in place of "Abductions" as I formerly called them.) I have
not been led to recognize any logical division of them.
