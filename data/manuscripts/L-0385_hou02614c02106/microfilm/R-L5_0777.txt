My dear Prof. Royce:

You expressed a wish to hear how I liked your Supplementary Essay. I think it a valuable service to philosophers. It is perfectly accurate, admirably clear. Of course it does not touch two ideas fully as important as that of the endless series, the abnumeral numbers, and true (not merely analytical) continuity. But I doubt not it was judicious to give the philosophers only such a dose as they could carry. You also only notice one narrow way in which the idea is applicable to philosophy. There are others fully as illuminative. Perhaps the most suggestive phrase in your book is your "dynamo of ideas." Imagine each ether to be composed of atoms which are vortices in another ether of similar constitution & if the velocity of transmission increases from [del]sound[/del] [ins]light[/ins] to [del]light[/del] [ins]gravity[/ins], the whole endless series might be traversed in a fraction of a second, with purely dynamical causation passing thus into an endless series of soul-ethers under causation purely final or formal. No matter how improbable such a hypothesis, its mere possibility refutes the principal argument for parallelism. That is, it shows that though your share of butchers, bakers, laundresses, & [del]grocers[/del] [ins]grooms[/ins],—keeping house or having us do so, you prefer,—only then you and I could not work way through this logic question to good service for those who in the 21st century may study your future books. The ugly point of this scheme is that we are compelled to try to sell the place, & we might, all of a sudden, succeed.

Underneath your logic which I cannot approve there is a nearly parallel stream of thought perfectly sound & in fact without doubt this was really what has kept you straight so that,—of course I am saying what seems true,—the approximate planes of your conclusions are approximately right. Your statement of the relation of the individual to God is sublime and fits & satisfies the soul in life and in the hour of death. It must stand for age after age. My feeling is that the individual just fills his little place in the revelation of the universal (except for the accidental fragment of universal meaning he hears is no account, like the word to which pills out "Be or not be" and so helps the effect of the drama of Hamlet. If there is [del]glor[/del] so much glee in heaven [del]of[/del] over one sinner that repenteth, what must be the deep [del]and[/del] [ins]unutterable[/ins] felicity to Carnegie of picking up a newspaper in the elevated and so saving his copper. Individuals are cells.

Very faithfully
C. S. Peirce
