I say that for a formula to be false, it is requisite that all
its antecedentals should be true and its consequental false.
To show that this is so, suppose it to be true for every
formula [del]with[/del] the number of whose antecedentals is
[del]n.[/del] [ins][u]n[/u].[/ins] Consider, then, a formula with [u]n[/u]+1 antecedentals.
This is a consequence, and as such can only be false
if its antecedent is true while its consequente is false.
But its consequente, being a formula of [u]n[/u] antecedentals,
can only be false (by hypothesis) if all the antecedentals
are true and the consequental false. Now the [u]n[/u]+1 ante-
cedentals are the antecedent and the [u]n[/u] antecedentals
of the consequent. To make the entire formula false, all
these must be true, while the consequental of the consequent,
which is also the consequental of the whole formula, is
false. Thus the rule, if true for every formula of any number,
[u]n[/u], of antecedentals, is true likewise for every formula having
one antecedental additional. But it is true [ins]in case[/ins] for [u]n[/u]=1;
hence, it is true for every finite value of [u]n[/u]. Accordingly, in
order that a formula should be logically necessary, that is,
true by virtue of its form, it is only requisite that something
about its form should prevent all the antecedentals from
being true when the consequental is not true.
