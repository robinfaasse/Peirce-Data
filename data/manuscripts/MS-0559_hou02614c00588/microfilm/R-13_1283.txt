Conditioning being an unsymmetrical operation, if we assume that
$$\overline{f}\dagger v = v,$$
we must take
$$\overline{v}\dagger f = f.$$

Moreover,
$$\overline{f}\dagger f \quad \text{and} \quad \overline{v}\dagger v$$
must have [del]opposite[/del] [ins]the same[/ins] value. For suppose
$$\overline{f}\dagger f = v$$
$$\overline{v}\dagger v = f.$$
Then, $\overline{x}\dagger y$ would merely have the value the opposite of that of $x$; and would not depend upon $y$ at all. We will, therefore, assume [del]the following[/del] [ins]as a[/ins] definition of conditioning the following three equations
(1) $\overline{f}\dagger y = v,$
(2) $\overline{x}\dagger v = v,$
(3) $\overline{v}\dagger f = f.$

The calculus is thus defined. In developing its rules, I shall endeavor to make my proceedure clearly illustrate my ideas of the logic of such an inquiry.
