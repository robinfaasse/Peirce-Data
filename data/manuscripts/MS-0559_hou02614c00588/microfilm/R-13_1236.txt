Let us now proceed to develope the general properties of the sign of consequence, and it will be found that they are perfectly balanced. Suppose we begin by writing the simplest formula which is necessarily true. This is
(1) $$\text{T}\ \overline{\text{x}}\ \text{T}\ \text{x}.$$
If anything is true, it is true. This is called the [u]principle of identity[/u]; and is usually stated in the form "A is A". [del]It is often[/del] It was formerly often made a "law of thought." Were it so, it would concern psychology not logic. How we do think is beside the question of how we ought to think. The principle of identity is really nothing but a part of what the sign of consequence signifies.*

\* This principle was expressed in the middle ages in the maxim: omne ens est verum. A proposition of the form A is A is called an identical proposition. Leibniz made this principle the first principle of logic. (Nouv. Ess. IV. ii. 1.) I rather think it was Baumgarten that gave the principle its name.
