word [u]subject[/u] until long after the terminology of logic was

the word; nor had any of them the precise conception now

word was [u]suppositum[/u]. The only passage I am able to cite from
a medieval grammar in which [u]subjectum[/u] is used is the
following from the [u]Doctrinale[/u] of Alexander de Villadei.
([u]Notices et Extraits[/u] XXII, 354): "Cum dicitur [u]venit una[/u]
[u]sororum[/u], respectu huius adiectivi [u]una[/u] consideratur
duplex subiectum, scilicet mediatum et immediatum;
mediatum voco istud subiectum [u]sororum[/u]," etc. So here
it is a genitive that is subject. The grammatical use is so
modern that it does not occur in the [u]Port Royal Greek[/u]
[u]Grammar[/u]. Of course, a grammarian of any age might
speak of the subject of a proposition, that being a term of
logic. But the "subject of a verb" is a purely grammatical
term and very late.
