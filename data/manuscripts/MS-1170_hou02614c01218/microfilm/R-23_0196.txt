So much for the ancient usage, except one point that I will refer
to directly. Now Skeat, referring to the late appearance of the
word in French, says the English word was taken directly from
the Latin. Indeed? What kind of Latin, I should be glad to know?
The word was not used at all in medieval Latin later than Marcianus
Capella that I know of & that in no sense that has any obvious con-
nection with an English use (except of course the Latinist Hobbes
who was steeped with classical Latin [del]& such[/del]). It seems to me that
about the earliest [ins]modern[/ins] writers to use the word were Copernicus and Galileo
and that it was especially the influence of the latter that brought it
into use. Although in 1630 the 'Dialogo intorno ai due massimi
sistemi de mondo tolemaico e copernicano' the word was used in
the sense of a reasoned doctrine, or nearly that, yet for a lifetime the
word had been the subject of innumerable actual dialogues in Italy, and
the meaning had had ample time to change from that of the constitution
of the universe [ins](a meaning found in Ptolemy's Almagest)[/ins] to a doctrine concerning that constitution. I do not
believe that before Copernicus it had been used in the sense of a
doctrine. Thus, we have the genealogy of the commonest meaning
of the word in philosophy. But how came mathematicians to talk
of [ins]"systems"[/ins] of forces etc.? It is a word extensively used in metrology
