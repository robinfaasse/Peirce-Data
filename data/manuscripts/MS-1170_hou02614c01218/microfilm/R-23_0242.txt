2. Multitude, degree of [del]maniness[/del] [ins]multitude[/ins]. So used by many logico-mathematicians, in deference to the high authority of G. Cantor. Nevertheless, in this sense the term is unacceptable for these reasons: 1[sup]st[/sup], the phrase is needed [del]to[/del] in the meaning it has borne since Priscian (500 A.D.) in all languages, and if used in another sense is liable to be misunderstood; 2[sup]nd[/sup], the new meaning is perfectly familiar as the strict abstract meaning of the word 'multitude', which better expresses it according to usage and etymology alike; 3[sup]rd[/sup], although in familiar language there is no harm in calling both collections and the multitudes of these collections by the name of 'number', which properly applies only to an expression of [del]multitude or ordinal[/del] [del]position[/del] position in a count, yet such inaccuracy in technical [del]language[/del] terminology (and Cantor's meaning has no support in any other kind of language) is sure to bring about confusion of thought.
