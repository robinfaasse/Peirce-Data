[u]abnumerable[/u] Of a multitude greater than that of the whole numbers. There is [del]a[/del] [ins]a simple[/ins] [del]abnumerable[/del] denumeral series of abnumerable multitudes and no others. The first abnumerable multitude is that of the irrational numbers of the calculus and is the multitude of all possible collections consisting of [ins]conceivable[/ins] members of one denumeral collection, or the multitude of [del]collections[/del] [ins]members[/ins] of collections of whole numbers. Every abnumerable multitude after the first is the multitude of possible collections formed of members of one abnumerable multitude of the next lower grade. The reason there is no multitude greater than all the multitudes of the endless series of abnumerable multitudes is that the individuals of such a whole lose their definite identities and become welded into a [ins]true[/ins] continuum, like the points for which there is room on a line. There is room for any [ins](which involves an absurdity)[/ins] multitude, but were they all actually there, they would form a continuous line and not be a collection of discrete individuals, and consequently not be actually there.
