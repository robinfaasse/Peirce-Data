Some turns of expression virtually punctuate themselves. [del]But there are others which lead us astray.[/del] Thus, the [ins]and[/ins] in the following sentence is warning enough that the predicate is coming:

"Sculpture, painting, and poetry are fields for artistic originality."

Were the [u]and[/u] omitted, a comma would be needed, thus:

"Sculpture, painting, architecture, were the great arts of the renaissance."

On the other hand, certain [del]modes[/del] [ins]turns[/ins] of expressions lead the reader so confidently to expect a comma, that if he does not find it, he will stop, reread, and puzzle over the matter. This is true of the comma after [u]painting[/u], in the last example but one, and of [del]that[/del] [ins]the comma[/ins] after [u]loves[/u] in the following from Wilson:

"Happy is the man who honors, obeys, [del]serves[/del] [ins]loves,[/ins] [del]and[/del] serves his Creator."
