Charmidas, continued. Chaerephides having been praised for his
σωφροσύνη, Socrates asks him what σωφροσύνη is. [Or
rather Socrates says Critias so praised him. He slips in the discussion.]
Socrates says that if Charmides has σωφροσύνη he must know
what it is (159 A); which is a non sequitur. Since the word is extremely
ambiguous, it is of course impossible to give one definition
which covers all uses of it.

Charmides thought it was calmness, or quiet [modesty]. But
Socrates argues that it is a virtue & therefore always superior to its opposite
while calmness or quiet or gentleness is not so desirable as its opposite
quickness [Socrates substitutes slowness for quiet] in reading, writing
playing the lyre, wrestling, boxing, gymnastics, leaping, running, learning,
teaching, remembering, shrewdness, deliberation, or in general.

Charmides then suggests that it is modesty. But Socrates argues that
modesty is not good for a needy man (on the authority of Homer) and
therefore modesty is not a virtue, as σωφροσύνη is.

Charmides then remembers that he has been told that σωφροσύνη is
doing one's own business. Socrates objects that writing your enemies'
names is not doing your own business & yet is not a breach of
σωφροσύνη. Further, a man need not weave and wash his own
clothes, make his own shoes, and his own flask and strigil; which doing
his own business requires. [This is a sorry quibble. It is not necessarily a
man's business to make his own shoes. On the contrary, such conduct would be
contrary to doing one's own [ins]proper[/ins] work.] Socrates himself says that this cannot
be what was meant. Here Critias takes up the parable.
