[u]Hippias minor[/u]. Hippias having said that Achilles was
inclined to speak the truth and Ulysses to lie, Socrates
argues that to lie successfully & consistently a man must
have the knowledge which would enable him to speak the truth,
and therefore the true and the false man are one and the same.
It is difficult to imagine anybody deceived by this transparent sophistry.

Socrates further says, that Achilles lies (in threatening to do what he
had not decided to do) and on Hippias saying that Achilles [del]lies[/del] [ins]speaks falsehood[/ins] uninten-
tionally but Ulysses intentionally, Socrates argues that it is worse to
speak falsehood unintentionally than intentionally (which, by the way,
is the idea of the Modern Greek). He extends this to all sorts of injuries.
His argument is that he who run badly is worse if he does so without
meaning to than if he do so on purpose, and so with wrestling badly,
taking ungraceful attitudes, singing badly, limping, blinking, using
ears, nostrils, & mouth badly, steering badly, — and it is true of a horse
or dog, — shooting badly, giving injurious medicine, — and it is true of
slaves [It manifestly is not true of horses, dogs, or slaves.] He
further argues as follows: Justice is either a power or a knowledge. If it
is a power, whoever is more powerful is more just. But he who
sins voluntarily has more power than he who does wrong unintentionally.
This is incredibly [del]sad[/del] [ins]bad[/ins] reasoning, & seems very like Euthydemus, and
unlike [del]the[/del] Socrates of the best dialogues.

Perhaps the point is to show that Hippias was unable to answer
even such ridiculous & contemptible sophistry, which however
seems quite beneath a man of any dignity at all. [del]But there is bad reasoning all through[/del]

It is rather difficult to believe that Plato wrote it; but still it cannot
be rejected in view of Aristotle's testimony. Of course we cannot be certain.
