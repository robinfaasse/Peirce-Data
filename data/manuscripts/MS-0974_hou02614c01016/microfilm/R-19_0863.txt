<u>Laches</u>, continued from p. 9. The question is, what is courage?
Gen. Laches suggests "[del]remaining[/del] [ins]standing[/ins] at one's post." Socrates objects that
the Scythians fight flying. It defines only the courage of the heavy-armed
soldier, but not that against perils of the sea, of disease, of poverty,
and politics. Laches then says, Courage is endurance of mind. Socrates
objects that courage is a noble quality. But foolish endurance is not so.
If we [del]say[/del] [ins]amend[/ins] the definition and say that Courage is [u]wise[/u] endurance, then

<u>Lysis</u>, continued. The first part, with Lysis, is a simple inductive
argument to show that we shall be trusted so far as we have knowledge.
To which is added that we shall be loved in the same proportion.

[del](a)[/del] (b) The second part ask whether a friend is one who loves or one who is
beloved or whether both are friends. If A loves B and B does not love A there is no
friendship. From this it is concluded [del][most irrationally][/del] that nobody who does
not love in return is loved. But this is shown to be false by the examples of horses, [del]and[/del]
dogs, wine, gymnastics, & wisdom. Whence it is argued that the beloved, not the lover, is
the friend. But this cannot be since nobody is loved by his enemies, nor hated by
his friends.
