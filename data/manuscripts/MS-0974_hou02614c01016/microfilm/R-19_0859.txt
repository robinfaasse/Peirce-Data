[u]Cratylus[/u]. Whether words [del]are[/del] [ins]have[/ins] [del]by[/del] such sounds as are naturally adapted to expressing their meanings.

Protagoras says "man is the measure of things" and Euthydemus that there is no difference between truth and falsehood. Socrates dissents from both.

The dialogue has two parts. In the first part with Hermogenes, Socrates takes the view that [del]names or[/del] [ins]words were[/ins] made by a "legislator" who does so [del]by[/del] with the dialectician.

He goes on to give hypothetical etymologies of a large number (120) nouns. He shows very little knowledge of grammar. A few of his etymologies, perhaps one in every six or eight, are possible.

He then undertakes to give the significance of different letters.

In the second part, which is carried on with Cratylus, Socrates shows how falsehood can come about, by his theory of [del]images[/del] [ins]imitations[/ins] (See Rep X) He also shows that convention and custom are influential in the formation of words. The Legislator must have had some knowledge of things otherwise than through words; and we must do the same.

This dialogue is far superior to any that precedes it, from a scientific point of view.
