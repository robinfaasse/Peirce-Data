The first two expressions are equivalent by the dis-
tribution-principle. The third is equivalent to
$\Sigma_{i} a_{i}$ because $a a = a$, and thus all three are
equivalent to one another.

The theorem [del]enunciated[/del] having thus been proved
true of every proposition having a single index, it
only remains to show that if it be true of every pro-
position having $n$ indices.

Now let $\Phi$ and $*$ stand either for $\Sigma$ and $+$ or
$\Pi$ and $\times$ respectively, so that
$\Phi_{i} U_{i} = U_{1} * U_{2} * U_{3} * \text{etc}$

This may represent any proposition in $n+1$ indices
of which $i$ is the first. When $i$ is fixed in a certain
individual as in $U_{1}$, $U_{2}$, etc. It becomes a pro-
position in $n$ indices. Let $U^{2}$ represent any
legitimate square of $U$. Then the square of the whole
expression with identification of $i$ in the two factors
is
$\Phi_{i} U_{i}^{2} = U_{1}^{2} * U_{2}^{2} * U_{3}^{2}$ etc.
and since by hypothesis
$U_{1} \prec U_{1}^{2}$, $U_{2} \prec U_{2}^{2}$, $U_{3} \prec U_{3}^{2}$ etc.
it follows that
$\Phi_{i} U_{i} \prec \Phi_{i} U_{i}^{2}$.

Thus it is shown that the rule holds for every
proposition in $n+1$ indices, if the first of them
is identified in the two factors. But according to
the rule, the first cannot be diversified unless
all are diversified; and all may be diversified,
by an obvious extension of the formulae relating
to propositions in a single index. The theorem
is therefore proved.

After the involution has once been performed,
further identification and diversification of indices
may be effected by applying the following formulae

[u]For identifications.[/u] [u]For diversifications.[/u]

$\Pi_{i} \Pi_{j} l_{ij} \prec \Pi_{i} l_{ii}$ $\Pi_{i} l_{ii} \prec \Pi_{i} \Sigma_{j} l_{ij}$
$\Sigma_{i} \Pi_{j} l_{ij} \prec \Sigma_{i} l_{ii}$ $\Sigma_{i} l_{ii} \prec \Sigma_{i} \Sigma_{j} l_{ij}$

Besides these formulae, the following are occasionally
useful
$\Pi_{i} m_{i} = \Pi_{i} \Pi_{j} m_{i} m_{j}$ $\Sigma_{i} m_{i} = \Sigma_{i} \Sigma_{j} (m_{i} + m_{j})$.

The following example shows the utility of changing
the indices, even without involution. From the premise
$\Pi_{i} \Pi_{j} (m_{i} l_{ij} + \bar{m}_{i} \bar{l}_{ij})$
let it be required to eliminate $m$. The immediate dropping
of this token would only yield an identical proposition.
But by separating the Boolean into factors and then changing
the indices, we have
$\Pi_{i} \Pi_{j} (m_{i} l_{ij} + \bar{m}_{i} \bar{l}_{ij})$
$= \Pi_{i} \Pi_{j} (m_{i} + \bar{l}_{ij}) (l_{ij} + \bar{m}_{i})$
$= \Pi_{i} \Pi_{j} (m_{i} + \bar{l}_{ij}) \Pi_{j} (l_{ij} + \bar{m}_{i})$
$= \Pi_{i} \Pi_{j} (m_{i} + \bar{l}_{ij}) \Pi_{k} (l_{ik} + \bar{m}_{i})$
$= \Pi_{i} \Pi_{j} \Pi_{k} (m_{i} + \bar{l}_{ij}) (l_{ik} + \bar{m}_{i})$
$= \Pi_{i} \Pi_{j} \Pi_{k} (m_{i} l_{ik} + \bar{m}_{i} \bar{l}_{ij})$
