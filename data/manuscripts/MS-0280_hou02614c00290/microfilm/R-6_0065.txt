But this is neither here nor there: Let us come to the point. Kant sets out from the obvious truth that any cognition is due to a reaction between the mind and a real object; but he seems to think that, when that is granted, it has been admitted that the cognition has two parts, the "matter" of it, due to the object and the "form" of it, which is of purely mental provenance.

We might as well assume that in a note sounded on a violin there is one part due to the violin-string and another due to the bow; or that the pain of having a tooth drawn must consist of two parts, one due to the tooth, and the other to the dentist's pull.
