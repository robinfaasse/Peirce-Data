Thus a proper name in its proper maturity is wholly devoid
of signification and has for its sole function the distinguishing
of a single object from all others, and this renders it particularly
apt for filling [del]the[/del] a blank of a rheme and becoming a 
subject of a proposition. Such a subject is called a [u]Singular[/u]
[u]Subject[/u], and [del]a[/del] [ins]the[/ins] proposition, if so analyzed as to have but this
one subject, is termed a [u]Singular Proposition[/u]. But a word or
phrase which is equivalent to a possible proper name in the
[fig]figure[/fig] first stage, or in the third, may be used as the subject of
a proposition. We thus have in the one case the [u]Particular[/u], in
the other the [u]Universal[/u], [del]Propo[/del] [u]Subject[/u] and [u]Proposition[/u]. The
utterer of [del]the[/del] [ins]a[/ins] particular proposition gives an insufficient,
indefinite indication of the object to which his assertion applies.
