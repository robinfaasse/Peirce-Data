Now to come down to business. In all kinds of delivery, whether
from the pulpit or from the stage, there are two distinct objects to
which artistic effort has to be directed. One is, the general
Tone; the other the action which overlies that tone.

An actor, for instance, attends first to his get up, and de-
cides upon what his general appearance is to be, what the
general pose of his body is to be, [del]and what the[/del] [ins]his[/ins] is to be
the general character of his tones of voice and pronunciation,
whether his movements are to be elegant or otherwise. That
is his first study. He will be weeks about that; and the
particular business and elocution he rightly regards as
matters secondary. The same thing is true in other arts.
It is so, in the general art of dealing with your fellow
men. Every man plays a part all his life. Talleyrand said
of Napoleon: "This extraordinary man actually feigns the
passions he really feels." There the arch-hypocrite Talleyrand,
a man without a soul, betrayed himself. For had he had
any real passions except avarice, he would have known
that every genuine man [ins]if he has any force[/ins] feigns the passions he really feels.
