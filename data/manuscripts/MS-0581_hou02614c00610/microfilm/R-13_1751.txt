Another principle is that all the haecceities are alike
in respect to relations

Let us define a "formal Boolean" as one in which
the only letter on the line is $q$. It may be said to express
a "formal relation" between its indices. Let $\chi$ be such
a relation. Then if we have

[del]$\Pi_{\alpha} \Sigma_{\beta}$[/del]

$\Pi_{\alpha} \Sigma_{\beta} \Pi_{i} \Sigma_{j} \Pi_{\gamma} \overline{q_{\alpha \beta}} + \overline{q_{\gamma \gamma}} + \overline{q_{\beta \beta}} + \overline{q_{\beta \alpha}} + \overline{q_{\beta i}}$

$\Pi_{\alpha} \Sigma_{\beta} \Pi_{i} \Sigma_{j} \overline{q_{\alpha \beta}} \cdot q_{\alpha j} \cdot q_{\gamma i} \cdot \overline{q_{\gamma j}} + q_{\beta i}$

the entire Boolean is formal. But if we consider $\alpha$ and $\beta$
as fixed already, $q_{\alpha}$ and $q_{\beta}$ are letters on the line
different from $q$; and then the Boolean is not formal.

If I say of a collection, $\alpha$, of two things [fig]two dots with arrows pointing to each other[/fig]

$\Pi_{\beta} \Sigma_{i} \Pi_{j} \overline{q_{\alpha i}} \cdot (\overline{q_{\alpha j}} + \overline{q_{\beta j}} + q_{\beta i} \cdot (\overline{q_{\beta j}} + q_{\beta i}))$

There can be nothing peculiar about $i$, since the relation
is purely formal. By this I don't mean that for every $\beta$ any
$i$ would do. Plainly not. What I do mean is simply that

$\Pi_{i} \Pi_{j} \Pi_{k} \Pi_{\beta} \Pi_{\gamma} \Pi_{\delta} \overline{q_{\alpha i}} + \overline{q_{\alpha j}} + \overline{q_{\alpha k}} + \overline{q_{\beta i}} + \overline{q_{\beta j}} + \overline{q_{\beta k}} + \overline{q_{\gamma i}} + \overline{q_{\gamma j}} + \overline{q_{\gamma k}}$
$+ \overline{q_{\delta i}} + q_{\delta i}$

There is nothing peculiar about any individual as to the form
of its relations with other individuals. It may be peculiar in
reference to a special relation.
