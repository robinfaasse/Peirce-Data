5. 'end'

The sound of [u]e[/u] short appears to have undergone no change
since the time of Mulcaster. Individual exceptions to the rule
then existed, however, as now. If the reader is prepared for the shock,
he must be told that by all, high and low, in our Augustan age,
'yet', 'yes', 'yesterday', 'yellow', 'friend', 'any', & 'many' were respectively
pronounced 'yit', 'yis', 'yisterday', 'yallow', 'frind', 'anny', and 'manny'—
arrant Hibernicisms all of them.* Even as late as 1767 the first four
words in the list had not received their present enunciation. [ins]†[/ins] Now,
scarcely less than then, "the quik i, and the gentle passant e ar
so near of kin, as their interchange places with pardon, as in
descryed, or descryid, findeth, or findith, [del]him or her[/del], the error
is no heresie." [ins]‡[/ins] [ins]"But Coote charges his pupil [del]to[/del] "Take heed [del]lest you[/del] also you put
not (e) for (i) in the end of a word as [u]unitee[/u] for [u]unity[/u], nor (id) for (ed) as [u]united[/u] for [u]united[/u], which
is Scottish." [ins]||[/ins][/ins]
