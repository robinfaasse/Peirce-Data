[u]An Inquiry into the Pronunciation of Shakespearian English[/u]

[u]II[/u]
[u]The Vowels[/u]

"When the age of our peple" says Mulcaster "which now use the tung so well, is dead and departed there will another succeede, and with the peple the tung will alter and change. Which change in the full harvest thereof maie prove comparable to this, but sure for this which we now use, it semeth even now to be at the best for substance and the bravest for circumstance, and whatsoever shall becom of the English state, the English tung canot prove fairer, then it is at this daie". The successors of the long forgotten grammarian have looked not to the fulfilment of the unheeded prophecy, and have conceived a deeply rooted opinion of the neglect of rhythmic form on the part of our elder poets. The ear is sick of the changes that have been rung upon the license allowed during the Augustan age of our literature with respect to [ins]the[/ins] matters of rhyme. The truest poets of our day, Tennyson and our own Longfellow and Lowell, are no less distinguished for their matter, than their manner. The jewel of thought must receive an appropriate setting. The form must not be inferior to the in-formed. But by Shakespeare and Milton no rhyme was too faulty for acceptance; they might match 'swear' with 'fear', 'sea' with 'lay', 'lull' with 'full', 'great' with 'heat' in lordly contravention to all prosodaic rules.

Doubtless there was some looseness of rhyme among our old authors, due to the want of any authoritative orthoepic standard and the known shackles of verse, but that it existed to the extent commonly believed is in itself improbable, [del]and will be disproved[/del]
