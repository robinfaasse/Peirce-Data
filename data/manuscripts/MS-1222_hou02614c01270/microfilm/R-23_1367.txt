We do not give great weight to direct authority on this point
for the following reason. The whole vocal interval between [u]sh[/u] and [u]s[/u]
is filled up with innumerable possible sounds which, both with re-
spect to their formation in the mouth and the sound itself in the
ear, differ not at all in kind, but only in [ins]the[/ins] degree of proximity of
the tongue to the palate & teeth. The sound of [u]-tion[/u] was once un-
doubtedly that which the French now give to it. During the change
to [u]shun[/u] it probably rested for generations on some of these
intermediate semi-vocals. Now, in all the grammars and diction-
aries down to the middle of the last century [u]-tion[/u] and [u]-sion[/u]
are still described as sounding [u]sion[/u] or [u]syon[/u], although from
a chance remark in De la Touche's [u]L'Art de bien parler[/u]
[u]Français[/u] Amsterdam [date]1704[/date] we discover that ever since then at
the least they were pronounced exactly as they now are.
Moreover the orthöepists of those days used ordinarily to describe
[u]sh[/u] as equivalent to [u]s[/u]. How natural, then, for them to call
[u]shi[/u], [u]si[/u]. † For this reason, we should rely principally on in-
dications rather than statements regarding this difficult subject.
These lead us to conclude

1[sup]st[/sup] that [u]-tion[/u] & [u]-sion[/u] might be sounded in two syllables or
in one.

2[sup]nd[/sup] that they had not the consonantal sound of [u]sh[/u] in
[u]shun[/u].

† Wilkins Essay p 372. Wallis pp 38, 65.
