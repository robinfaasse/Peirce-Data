The pronunciation of 'Rome' has been a stumbling block for
more than one Antiquarian. In King John Act III. Sc. I. occurs
the line;
"That I have room with Rome to curse awhile,"
from which we might infer that 'Rome' was intended to be pro-
nounced 'Roome'. But in Henry VI. Part I. Act III. Sc. I., Winches-
ter says, "Rome shall remedy this," to which Warwick's reply is, "Roam
thither then"; from which the inference would be that 'Rome' was
pronounced as it is now. Shakespearean critics have argued on the
one side that 'Rome' was pronounced 'room', and on the other that
'room' was pronounced 'roam', and the latter argument seems to
have the better show of reason from the fact that 'room' is sometimes
found spelt 'rome' in writers anterior to Shakespeare. But this mode
of spelling proves nothing, for its professors studied orthography when
[u]oo[/u] and [u]o[/u] were confusedly written for the same sound. It would be
quite as strong an argument to say that since 'room' was sometimes
spelled 'roum', (Anglo-Saxon 'rum') and since 'four' was pronounced
like 'old', that 'room' also might have been. But what would Mul-
caster reply to such an hypothesis? "School, tool, Groom, bloom,
Hoop, coop. If custom had not won this why not ou. Because of the
sound which these diphthongs have sometimes upon the o, sometimes
upon the, u. I will note the o, sounding upon himself, with the
streight accent, because that o leadeth the lesse number. +++
Roum, boum, loum." [ins]*[/ins] But not only Mulcaster, but all the old gram-
