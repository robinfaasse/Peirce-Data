The orthoepy of Shakespeare's day is intimately connected with
its orthography. A short vowel occasioned to a far greater extent than
now the doubling of the succeeding consonant, as 'vowell', and 'runne'.
Exceptions to the general rules of spelling were commonly written to
conform with their pronunciation, as 'cleere' & 'neere'. The most
obvious distinction between the written language of [del]the[/del] the age
age of Elizabeth and our own is the modernization, at the latter
period, of words having a short vowel sound, and the change of
ie to y at the end of words. The progress of the change is easily
marked; the uneducated preceding the educated in the general
march of improvement, and effecting what the learned despaired
of accomplishment. Thus the Folio of 1623, filled as it is with
the grossest instances of misspelling on the part of its ill educated
compositors, is more modern in orthography than more correctly prin-
ted works of that day. The variable orthography everywhere exhibited,
though seemingly subject to no restraint, was strictly confined within
certain and easily discoverable limits. It did not transgress the
well settled laws of orthoepy. Although 'seize' might be spelt
'ceaze', (by the untaught) [ins]and[/ins] 'construe' 'conster', 'meat' could never be spelled
