Words like 'daunt', then, in which the orthography [ins]au[/ins] was preserved, were pronounced with the broad sound of the diphthong, and similarly formed words in which the [u]u[/u] was suppressed received generally a like enunciation. A quotation from Mulcaster* will illustrate this practice. "<u>Glance</u>, <u>france</u>, <u>chance</u> where, a, before the, n, soundeth like a diphthong, tho, u, be not written." The rule laid down in the Elementarie is observed in the phonotypy of Gil. Still a decided minority held to the present pronunciation of many of these words, at least in those cases where [u]u[/u] was not retained in the written speech. This innovation is referred to by Mulcaster & Gil; and in the following dialogue from Coote:
"<u>Robert</u>. What spelleth b, r, a, n, c, h
<u>John</u>. <u>Branch</u>
<u>Robert</u>. Nay but you should put in (u)
<u>John</u>. That skilleth not, for both wayes be [del]common[/del] [ins]usuall[/ins] [ins]†[/ins]"

The vowelish and dipthongal pronunciation of 'daunt' waged constant war for a long series of years, and it was not till towards the middle of the last century, that the older pronunciation became obsolete. Still later the sound we give to 'dance' branched off from that of 'daunt', and now the prevalent cockneyism makes the sound of 'dance' the same as that of 'damsel'; in all which stages one tendency is manifest: 1. 'dawn', 2. 'daunt', 3. 'dance', 4. 'damsel'. The fourth sound is likely eventually to prevail.
