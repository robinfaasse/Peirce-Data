Of the Consonants

There is [ins]so[/ins] little doubt that all the [del]single[/del] consonants, with perhaps the exception of the letter R, had in most cases the same powers as at present, [del]that it will be unnecessary to adduce authorities in support of the general statement.[/del] Some little question has arisen, however, with respect to the pronunciation of J and G, which a few quotations will suffice to set at rest.

The occurrence [ins]in old authors[/ins] of the various forms "jerk" and "yerk" [del]has[/del] [ins]^[/ins] occasioned the assumption that [u]J[/u] was in Shakespeare's day invariably pronounced, as in some parts of the continent of Europe, with the force of the letter [u]Y[/u]. But Coote informs us that even "yerk" was pronounced "jerk"; and the theory appears to meet with no support from the old grammarians.
