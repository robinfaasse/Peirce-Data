that of the German and French [u]a[/u]. Here follow a few citations with
dates and authors prefixed.

1611. Cotgrave's French Dictionary. Ed. consulted 1650.
"A in the English tongue, and in no other, hath ten differing
sounds, the one open and cleere, as [u]Balaam[/u], the other pressing,
and as it were halfe mouth'd and mincingly, as [u]stale ale[/u]: In
French 'tis alwaies pronounced as the first, cleer and ouvert, as
[u]l'Amour fait la rage, mais l'argent le mariage[/u]." This descrip-
tion is very unsatisfactory in many particulars, but the clear and open
[u]a[/u] is probably intended to represent [u]a[/u] in 'ball', as otherwise there
would be no difference in [ins]the[/ins] sound of [u]a[/u] in 'Balaam' and 'ale'. This
[del]expl[/del] construction is strengthened by the fact that we are directed to
pronounce 'Baal' 'Bawl' by Strong, Coles, and Bailey.

1633. Ben Jonson. IX. 261. "[u]A[/u] with us, in most words, is pro-
nounced less than the French [u]a[/u]; as in [u]art[/u], [u]act[/u], [u]apple[/u], [u]ancient[/u],
but when it comes before [u]l[/u], in the end of a syllable, it obtaineth the
full French sound, and is uttered with the mouth and throat wide
opened, the tongue bent back from the teeth, as in [u]all[/u], [u]small[/u], [u]gall[/u], [del]fall[/del]
[u]fall[/u], [u]call[/u]."

1653. Wallis, (p. 6) after speaking of the sound of the broad [u]a[/u] in
'wall', proceeds: "Not only the Germans, but the French also and not a
few other nations for the most part pronounce their [u]a[/u] in the same
manner." He also discriminates between this sound and that of the
Italian [u]a[/u] which he compares to the English [u]a[/u] in 'can', and the
first letter of [u]entendement[/u].
