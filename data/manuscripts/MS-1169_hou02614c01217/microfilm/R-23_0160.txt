SOME, [u]a.[/u] In logic, the indefinite [u]some[/u], is [u]some in the sense of at least one, perhaps all[/u]; the
semidefinite [u]some[/u], is [u]some in the sense of a part, but not all[/u].

SORROW. Sorrow is uneasiness in the mind upon the thought of a good lost which might have been
enjoyed longer, or the sense of a present evil. Locke, [u]Essay concerning Humane Understanding[/u],
Book II., Chap. xx, 18.

SOUL, [u]n.[/u] A spirit that either is or has been connected with a body or material organization.

SOCHRETE, [u]n.[/u] That essence, whatever it may be, that makes Socrates different or diverse from
every other man.

SOCIOLOGY, [u]n.[/u] The science of the origin of society.

SUGGESTION, [u]n.[/u] [Late L. [u]suggestio[/u], and [u]suggestus[/u], common in Tertullian, from [u]suggero[/u], to prompt,
remind, suggest, classical.]
1. The act or operation of exciting an idea in the mind by the principles of association,
whether by a person or by an idea which brings up another idea. Frequent of temptation, as a prompt
prompting of the devil, in the sense of one idea exciting another, though common in Tertullian,
not frequent in modern writers until Berkeley, who brought the expression into vogue again, though
it had never entirely gone out.

SUPREME, [u]a.[/u] Add: Supreme genus, a genus which is not a species under another genus; otherwise cal-
led a chief or highest genus, or category or predicament. Supreme good, [u]summum bonum[/u], a good in
which there is no evil, something good in the highest possible degree, the perfectly good. The
supreme natural good is often said to be the continual progress toward greater perfections, beati-
tude. Supreme evil, evil in which no good is mixed, but this is an absurd notion. Supreme end,
the chief end, the last end in which the appetite or desire is satisfied.

SUBSTANCE, [u]n.[/u] [L. [u]substantia[/u], thing, substance, word first found in Quintilian, in common use in
Augustine's time, word invented by philosophers to translate Gr. [del]ὑπόστασις[/del] [ins]ὑποκείμενον[/ins], but it
soon came to be understood as the translation of [u]ousia[/u].]
1. That which in the reality of things corresponds to the subject of a sentence, that which
exists by itself, and sustains the accidents or qualities, relations, etc., a thing by itself,
an independent entity, the subject of motion, that which is permanent or perdurable and also
receives modifications; that which is not a mode of anything else.
Substance, is derived from [u]substo[/u], to stand under, for the subject which underlies the forms,
whether it be a body or another thing. Substance, as derived from [u]subsisto[/u], to make a stand, is
form which coming to a subject withstands it, that is, holds it under itself and other forms,
that is, makes it stand under itself and others, as the figure on a seal does the wax. Fr. from
[u]Art of Thinking[/u], [u]L'Art de Penser[/u], [u]Port Royal Logic[/u], [u]Part I, Chap. II[/u]. [About A.D. 1660]
A substance is a being subsisting of itself and subject to accidents. It subsists by itself
[gap] a subject, and it supports all substances.
