SORITES, n. [L. [u]sorites[/u], Gr. [u]soreites[/u], a heaper. Applied in classical times in sense 1. In sense 2, first used by Laurentius Valla, d. 1457]
1. A kind of sophism invented by Chrysippus in the third century before Christ, by which a person is led by gradual steps from maintaining what is manifestly true to admitting what is manifestly false. It is like this. One grain of sand cannot make a heap. Then if we add one grain to a grain, the one added grain cannot make that a heap, which was not a heap before. And so we proceed showing that each grain added cannot make a heap out of what was not a heap, until we find that a million grains of sand cannot make a heap.
2. A chain-syllogism, or argument having a number of premises and one conclusion, this argumentation being capable of being analyzed into a number of syllogisms, the of which the conclusion of each is a premise of the next. A sorites may be categorical or hypothetical, like a syllogism, and either variety may be progressive or regressive. The following examples will illustrate the latter distinction.

Progressive sorites.
If Harpagon be avaricious, he is intent on gain.
If intent on gain, he is discontented.
If discontented, he is unhappy.
Now Harpagon is avaricious.
He is, therefore, unhappy.

Regressive sorites.
If Harpagon be discontented, he is unhappy.
If intent on gain, he is discontented.
If avaricious, he is intent on gain.
Now Harpagon is avaricious.
Therefore, he is unhappy.
[Examples from Hamilton.]

SOPHISM, n. Properly, a false argumentation, fabricated and employed for the purpose of deceit. Sometime, made synonymous with fallacy, a false argumentation.
For the various kinds of sophism as to the figure of speech, of false universality, of false middle, of a non-cause as cause, of post hoc ergo propter hoc, of multiple question, of counter question, of a division, of composition, of division, of accent, of prosody, of accident, a dicto simpliciter ad dictum secundum quid, from suppression of the reason, of consequent, etc. see under FALLACY.

SOPHIST, n. Gr. [u]sophistes[/u], a wise man, a sophist. An ancient Greek teacher, who took pay, generally high, for teaching virtue, the management of a household or the government of a state, and all that belongs to wise action or speech, but who held that absolute truth is unattainable.

SOPHISTRY, n. The philosophy of the sophists.
