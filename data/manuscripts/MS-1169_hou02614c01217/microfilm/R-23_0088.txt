The rotation-area for an axis may be exhibited geometrically by a portion of the axis which is taken proportional to the area, and it is evident from the theory of projections that rotation-areas for different axes may be combined by the same laws with which forces applied to a point and rotations are combined, so that there is a corresponding parallelopiped of rotation-areas. There is, then, for every system, an axis of resultant rotation-area, with reference to which the rotation is a maximum, and the rotation-area for any other axis is the corresponding projection of the resultant rotation-area. The rotation-area vanishes for an axis which is perpendicular to the axis of resultant rotation-area. B. Peirce. Analytical Mechanics § 754.

RAMIFICATION. Add: Point of ramification, in the integral calculus, a point on the plane of imaginary quantity such that when the variable passes through it, the function to which the point refers has two of its values equal; the same as a critical point.

MATHEMATICAL WORDS IN S.

S, an operative symbol in quaternions, signifying the operation of taking the scalar part of a quaternion. It is also used in algebra for certain varieties of summation. s usually denotes space or the length of the arc of a curve. An [ins]s[/ins] above the line denotes a second of time. Below the line in enumerative geometry, it refers to a plane pencil of rays. $\Sigma$ signifies the sum of successive values of a function; the variable which is to take successive integral values in the terms to be added may be written below the line after the $\Sigma$, and the lower and upper limit of the summation may be written below and above the $\Sigma$. Thus, $a^x = \sum_n \frac{1}{n!} (\log a \cdot x)^n = 1 + \log a \cdot x + 1/2 \cdot (\log a \cdot x)^2 + \text{etc.}$ In the calculus of finite differences $\Sigma$ is used like a sign of indefinite integration, the lower limit being replaced by an arbitrary constant, while the upper is supposed to be 1 less than the value of the variable. Thus, $\Sigma Fx = F(x-1) + F(x-2) + \text{etc.}$ down to a constant value of the variable, and then an arbitrary constant is to be added to the series. $\sigma$ lower case, is used in the integral calculus to denote the area of a surface.
