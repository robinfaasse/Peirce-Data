3. A kind of self-consciousness resulting from an outward perception, whether directly or indirectly; [del]the external sense,[/del] the perception of a modification of consciousness; the faculty of distinguishing between a datum of sense and a product of reason; the consideration of the limitations of knowledge, ignorance and error, and of other unsatisfactory states as leading to knowledge of self; the discrimination between the subjective and objective aspects of feelings.

The latin word [u]reflexio[/u] was first used as a term of psychology by St. Thomas Aquinas [ins]in his De Principio Individuationis, which is generally recognized as genuine; but even if it were not his use of the term is established by contemporary testimony.[/ins] who seems to intend no optical metaphor, but to conceive that consciousness is turned back upon itself by the reaction of the object of outward perception. According to Aquinas, pure thought, in itself, can no nothing of singulars, or particular things; but in perception there is a peculiar sense of reaction or reciprocation which he calls reflection, and this first makes us aware of the existence of actual singulars and also of thought as being [del]xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx[/del] an action; and this, according to him is the first self-consciousness. Scotus accepted reflection, not as affording the first knowledge of singulars, but as but as a perception of what [del]xxx[/del] passes in the mind, and thus the original meaning of the term was modified. Walter Burleigh, who died [date]1337[/date], affords an illustration of this when he says, that the thing without is apprehended before the passion which is in the soul, because the thing without is apprehended directly and the passion of the soul only indirectly by reflection. A still further change of meaning had come about when Goclenius, in [date]1613[/date], defined reflection as "the inward action of the soul by which it recognizes both itself and its acts and ideas." Kant makes use of the term reflection to denote a mode of consciousness in which we distinguish between the relations of concepts, and the corresponding relations of the objects of the concepts. Thus, two concepts may be different, and yet it may be conceived that their objects are identical, or two concepts may be identical, and yet it may be conceived that their objects [say, two drops of water] are different, etc.

The faculty by which I place the comparison of representations in general by the side of the faculty to which they belong, and by which I determine whether they are compared by the with each other as belonging to the pure understanding or to sensuous intuition, I call transcendental reflection. Kant, Critic of the Pure Reason, tr. by Müller [p281]

In English literature, reflection often means a sad consideration of our own faults and limitations and of the vanity of human life, leading to self-knowledge and sobriety of conduct.
