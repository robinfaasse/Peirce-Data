[del]Reduction seems to have two principal genera of meanings, Diminution and Simplification. Reductio
was used in the middle ages in something like these senses, instead of deductio. Deductio in the
sense of diminution is classical, and in the sense of simplification is a translation of the Greek
apagoge, which although Lidell and Scott do not say so, was employed in a sense of this general kind
by Hippocrates of Chios, and this accounts for Aristotle's use of the word. I should arrange the
whole word, somewhat as follows:[/del]

REDUCTION [del]L.[/del] [ins]reductio[/ins], anciently used nearly in senses 1 and 2; and in the middle ages for
deductio, in senses 3 and 4. The last, 4, is a translation of the mathematical Greek, epagoge;
borrowed also by Aristotle from the mathematicians. Meaning 5 may be from deductio meaning expul-
sion, (9)]

1. Replacement; now only in surgery, the operation of restoring a dislocated or fractured
bone to its proper place.

2. The conversion of material into another form; as, reduction to powder. Especially, in
chemistry deoxydation (a sense extended from the reduction of a metal to a regulus.)

3. Diminution; as, the reduction of expenses, the reduction of the National debt. The accu-
rate copying of a drawing on a smaller scale; the copy so made.

4. [del]Siplification[/del] [ins]Simplification[/ins] in certain definite ways. (a) The bringing of a problem to depend on a prob-
lem already solved (b) The transformation of an algebraic expression into another of a simpler kind
(c) The lowering of the values of the numerator and denominator [ins]of a fraction[/ins], or of the antecedent and conse-
quent of a ratio, by dividing both by the same quantity. (d) The conversion of a quantity expres-
sed in terms of one denomination so as as to express it in terms of another denomination. Reduction
ascending, conversion to terms of larger units. Conversion descending, conversion to terms of
smaller units. (e) The proof of the conclusion of an indirect syllogism from its premises by means
of a direct syllogism and immediate inferences. This is said to be a reduction to the mode of dire
direct syllogism employed. Ostensive reduction, such a proof starting with the premises of the
indirect syllogism and proceeding to the conclusion. Apagogic reduction, the proof of the validity
of the indirect mod by means of the reductio ad absurdum. Short reduction, that which involves onl
only one immediate inference. Long reduction, that which involves two immediate inferences. Re-
duction is also used in logic for abduction, which see. (g) En astron., The correction of observed
quantities for instrumental errors, as well as for refraction, parallax, etc. so as to bring out
their cosmical significance. Also, a similar process applied to observations in other physical
sciences.

5. Conquest. As the reduction of a fortress.

[u]CATHA[/u]. [Ar.] The rule of six quantities. See RULE.

RULE, n. Add: Rule of intersection, rule of six quantities, the proposition that if a spherical
triangle be cut by a transversal great circle, the product of [del]shords[/del] [ins]chords[/ins] the chords of the dou-
bles of those segments which do not cut one another is equal to the product of the chords of the
doubles of the other three segments. This rule was discovered by Menelaus, about A.D. 100. Rule
of four quantities, a theorem of spherical trigonometry, so called in the middle ages. Rule of
Nichomachus [named for the celebrated Greek arithmetician, who flourished about A.D. 100] and who
is said to have been the author of this rule.] a rule for finding the square of a small number,
as follows: subtract the number from 10 and to the square of the difference add 10 times the num-
ber diminished by the difference. Thus, to find the square of 9, subtract 9 from 10, which gives 1
as the difference, the square of which is 1, and adding to this 10 times the excess of the origi-
nal number, 9, over the difference, 1, which excess is 8, we have 81 as the answer.
