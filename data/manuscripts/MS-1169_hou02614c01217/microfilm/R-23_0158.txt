[u]STOICHEIOLOGY[/u]. A division of almost any science which treats of the nature of the different kinds that science deals with, but not of the manner in which they are associated with one another; doctrine of elements.

In its stoicheiology, or doctrine of elements, logic considers the conditions of possible thought. The conditions of mere thinking are given in certain elementary requisites; and that part of logic which analyzes and considers these, may be called the stoicheiology, or doctrine of elements. ------ Logical stoicheiology, or the doctrine conversant about the elementary requisites of mere thought. Hamilton, [u]Lect. on Logic[/u], XXIV and IV.

[u]SPONTANEITY[/u]. The following definition, which should be given first, is not given:

1. That character of any action of any subject by virtue of which it takes place without being caused by anything distinguishable from the subject itself. Thus, if a vessel containing a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen were to be kept under unchanging physical conditions, and were to undergo no gradual change in itself, and if, after many years of quiescence it were suddenly to explode, that action would be said to be the character of spontaneity.

Spontaneity of certain cognitive faculties. In the philosophy of Kant, is the self-activity of those faculties, which are not determined to act by anything in the impressions on which they act. But the conception is not made very clear by Kant.
