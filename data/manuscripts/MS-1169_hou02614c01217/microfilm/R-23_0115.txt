Imagine several different forces which act in different directions so as to hold in equi-
librium a point, a line, a surface, or a solid. Imagine also that we impress upon the system of
these forces a small movement, either parallel to itself in any direction whatever, or around
any fixed point whatever, It will be easy to see that by this movement each of these forces will
advance or retreat in its own direction, unless some or more of them have their tendencies perpen-
dicular to the little movement, in which case this force or these forces will neither advance nor
retreat. For these advances or retreats, which are what I call virtual velocities, are nothing
but the increments or decrements of the several lines of tendency brought about by the little
movement; and these augmentations or diminutions will be found by erecting a perpendicular at th
the extremity of each line of force, so that it shall cut off from the same line of force in its
neighboring situation in the little movement a little part which will be the measure of the
virtual velocity of that force. John Bernoulli, tr. from the French. In the Nouvelle mechanique
of Varignon, vol. 2 section 9.
