SIGN, n. Nos. 1, 2, 7, should be brought under one number, thus:
1. Anything which serves to manifest, stand for, or call up the idea of another, in
the mind of the person perceiving it.

Antecedent sign, the sign of something about to come to pass. Arbitrary sign, one which is
constituted a sign by a convention or institution. Contingent sign, one which affords an uncer-
tain indication of its object. Consequent sign, one which signifies a thing already come to pass.
Conjunct sign, one which is contemporaneous with the state of things it signifies. Divine sign,
one instituted by God, as the rainbow, or a sacrament. Formal sign, one which signifies or re-
presents its object by virtue of its likeness to the same, an icon; such are pictures and diagrams
of all kinds; footprints are usually but wrongly said to be formal signs. Human signs, conven-
tional signs instituted by men. Instituted signs, the same as arbitrary signs, which see. Manifes-
tive sign, see Smiglecius. Material signs, one which represents its object by virtue of a real
relation or physical connection with it, an index; such are natural signs, weather-cocks and the
like; also relative and other pronouns, the letters of a geometrical diagram, etc. Natural sign,
one which represents its object owing to its own characters or relations, and not owing to con-
vention or institution. Necessary sign, one which is a cert in indication of the thing signified.

SIMPLE, a. Add: Simple acception, in logic, the acceptation of a universal term as signifying a
general nature abstracted from singulars, as when we say "Animal is the genus of man." Simple
affection, a character which belongs to [del]objects[/del] [ins]subjects[/ins] singly, as opposed [ins]a[/ins] to relation. Simple appre-
hension, [phrase found in Scotus, and common at all times in English logic] the faculty by which
we conceive a logical term, as distinguished from a judgment or inference. Simple comparison,
the faculty of judgment by which we compare the subject and predicate of a proposition. Simple
concept, one in which no plurality of attributes can be distinguished, which cannot be defined,
and of which nothing can be predicated. Simple conclusion, or simple consequence, an inference
drawn from a single premise; also, a conclusion from a single premise which is valid by virtue
of the meaning of the terms used, as Socrates is a man, therefore Socrates is an animal. Simple
convenience, the agreement of one thing with another in the same third, namely either a similari-
ty, identity, or equality. Simple conversion, in formal logic, the t inference of from a propo-
sition of another having the same subject and predicate, only transposed, and preserving the
quantity and quality of the original proposition. Two kinds of propositions only can be simply
converted, according to the traditional logic, namely the universal negative, "No S is P" into
"No P is S", and the particular affirmative "Some S is P" into "Some P is S." Simple ens, [a]
that which is neither composite nor componible, which is true of God alone; [b] the object of a
simple concept; [c] that which is not composed of different things, especially not of matter and
form, but is either pure matter or pure form; [d] that which is not composed of different kinds
of matter, as an element. Simple enumeration, the most naive method of colligating examples upon
which to base an induction.

Popular notions are usually founded on induction by simple enumeration; in science it car-
ries us but very little way. J. S. Mill, Logic, Book III, Chap. iii, §3.

Simple enunciation, one which cannot be resolved into several enunciations. Simple interpretation,
one of which no part signifies anything separately. Simple mode, one which is but a variation of a
single idea. Simple necessity, that of a proposition whose denial would imply a contradiction,
logical necessity. Simple part, one which has itself no parts of the same kind. Simple power,
that of first matter, pure power. Simple probation, one which involves a single inferential step,
one which cannot be analyzed into a succession of inferences. Simple proposition, the same as a
simple enunciation, which see. Simple question, the question whether a thing is and or what it is.
