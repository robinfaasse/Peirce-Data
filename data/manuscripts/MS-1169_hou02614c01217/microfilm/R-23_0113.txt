VARIABLE, n. For 2, put:
2. In math., a quantity which is indeterminate and is considered with reference to its different possible values. Originally, a quantity [del]continuous[/del] capable of values continuously connected in one dimension; so that it could be conceived as running through them all in the course of time; and this meaning still remains. But we now speak of the position of a point as variable in [del]two[/del] [ins]or three[/ins] dimensions, and we also speak of the arguments of functions in the calculus of finite differences, where there is no approach to continuity, as variables. The difference between an indeterminate constant and a variable, is frequently a mere difference of designation; but constants, though indeterminate, are not usually considered with reference to the different values which they may take. Independent variable, in the calculus, the variable with reference to which the differentiations are performed, the variable to which the differentiations refer; also, the variable which is [del]one[/del] considered first. [ins]or as the parameter for the others.[/ins] In any problem which may be proposed, it is a mere matter of convenience what variable shall be taken as the independent one, but after the equation is constructed, the matter is in many cases determinate. In partial differential equations, equations of surfaces, etc., there are two or more independent variables. Dependent variable, any variable not the independent one.

VARIATION, n. Add: to variation of the moon (which is usually called the variation, simply, and I do not remember having seen the expression variation of the moon) consisting in an acceleration in longitude from the quadratures to the syzygies, and a retardation from the syzygies to the quadratures. It was discovered by Tycho Brahe, b. 1546-d. 1601.
Add: 8. In the calculus, an infinitesimal increment of a function due to changes in the values of the constants, and affecting it, therefore, in different amounts for different values of the variables. Method, or calculus of variations, a branch of the differential calculus, established by the Bernoullis, Euler, and Lagrange, the object of which is to solve certain problems called problems of isoperimetry in which so one curve, surface, etc., is compared with another in regard to certain conditions. For example, the earliest problem of the calculus of variations was that of the brachistochrone, given two points A and B, to find the curve along which a particle will fall the quickest from A to B. A variation is denoted by a lower case Greek delta.
Variation of parameters, a change in an equation by which some of its constants are made functions of the variables; the use of [del]this[/del] [ins]a litigation[/ins] device [ins]to the solution[/ins] for solving differential equations is called the method of the variation of parameters.
9. In algebra, (a) the following of a + sign after a - sign or vice versa, in a row of signs; (b), a linear arrangement of some or all of a given set of objects. Thus, there are 15 variations of the letters A, B, C, as follows: A - B - C - AB - BA - BC - CB - CA - AC - ABC - BCA - CAB - CBA - BAC - ACB.

VELOCITY, n. It seems probable enough that meaning number 1 does exist, for that is the original meaning in French, where it is used for the movements of animals. But certainly none of the phrases cited in the text belong to this meaning, nor can I find any instance of such a meaning in any old author. I do not feel sure that it ever was good English to speak of a body as being in motion but without velocity. Under 2, omit the last sentence about the units. It is true that the second is quite generally taken as the unit of time in measurement of velocity, but there is no uniformity in the standard of length taken, and in theoretical works the centimetre would probably prevail.
Angular velocity, rate of rotation round an axis, measured by the angular displacement in any short or infinitesimal interval of time divided by that time.
