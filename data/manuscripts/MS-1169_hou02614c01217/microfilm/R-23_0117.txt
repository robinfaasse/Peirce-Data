9. This passage shows that a virtual velocity was not conceived by the author of the phrase as a

statement that in the case of a simple machine, or mechanical power, the velocities of the force
and the load are inversely as their weights. In generalizing this, Bernoulli seems to have used
the word virtual in sense number 1, above, so that virtual velocity is what corresponds to
the velocity in the older statement of the principle. The matter has not however, been so under-
stood, and virtual in this phrase has been interpreted in two ways, first as meaning possible,
and second as meaning infinitesimal. Virtual displacement, a modern expression invented because
a virtual velocity was understood to mean a velocity, an infinitesimal arbitrary displacement
not supposed to really take place, but imagined for the sake of showing that there is no tendency
to the production of it.

VIS, n. For the definition put:

Force; a term of dynamics, but without definite meaning, and embodying vague ideas of the
XVIIth century. Vis inertia, (a) in mechanics, the same as inertia, which see; (b) whence, moral
indisposition to commit oneself to an energetic line of action. Vis viva, a term invented by
Leibniz, in older writers the [del]quantity of[/del] mass into the square of the velocity, or the measure
of the mass multiplied by the square of that of the velocity, but recent writers frequently
use the term to denote one half of the above quantity. The principle of vis viva, the princi-
ple that when only positional forces are considered any changes in the vis viva of a system depen
depend only on the initial and final situations of the particles. Vis mortua, equilibrated force
or pressure.
