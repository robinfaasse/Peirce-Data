3. The L. reflexio was first used as a psychological term, as it would appear, by St. Thomas Aquinas, in the XIIIth century. He derived a good hint from St. Augustine, and the term antilepsis, or reciprocation or reaction, was in use in Greek, in somewhat the same sense. [del]It is found in Alexander Aphrodisiensis and Themistius,[/del] but more in this sense in Plotinus. Thomas denies that self-consciousness is immediate, but holds that in the rational cognition of an outward object the soul becomes conscious of its own activity, [del]and this consciousness of an action and reaction is termed by him reflexio. Without it, we could neither know our selves nor the actual existence of outward things.[/del] Scotus and others maintained another intuitive knowledge of singulars; and thus reflexio came to mean the [del]direct[/del] knowledge of modifications of consciousness [del]as such.[/del] Walter Burleigh, d. 1337, says the thing without is apprehended before the passion which is in the mind, because the thing without is apprehended directly and the passion of the soul indirectly by reflection. Goclenius, 1613, defines reflection as the inward action of the soul by which it recognizes both itself and its acts and ideas. The importance of the word in modern philosophy is due to Locke, who says:

The other fountain from which experience furnisheth the understanding with ideas is the perception of the operations of our own minds within us, as it is employed about the ideas it has got; which operations when the soul comes to reflect on and consider, do furnish the understanding with another set of ideas, which could not be had from things without; and such are perception, thinking, doubting, believing, reasoning, knowing, willing, and all the different actings of our own minds; which we being conscious of, and observing in ourselves, do from these receive into our understandings as distinct ideas, as we do from bodies affecting our senses. This source of idea every man has wholly in himself: and though it be not sense, as having nothing to do with external objects, yet it is very like it, and might properly enough be called internal sense. But as I call the other sensation, so I call this reflection, the ideas it affords being such only as the mind gets by reflecting on its own operations within itself. By reflection, then, I would be understood to mean that notice which the mind takes of its own operations, and the manner of them, by reason of which there come to be ideas of these operations in the understanding. Locke, Essay concerning Humane Understanding, Bk. II Chap i § 4.

[After the sentence about Burleigh insert: Ramus, in his dissertation on reflection, defines it as the successive direction of the attention to several partial perceptions.]

Reid endeavored to revive the Ramist use of the word, for which he is condemned by Hamilton. Kant, in his use of the term, returns to something like the Thomist view, for he makes it a mode of consciousness by which we are made aware whether knowledge is sensuous or not.

The act by which I place the comparison of representations in general by the side of the faculty to which they belong, and by which I determine whether they are compared with each other as belonging to the pure understanding or to sensuous intuition, I call transcendental reflection. Kant, Critic of the Pure Reason, tr. by Müller [p. 231].

Mr. Shadworth Hodgson, in his philosophy of Reflection, 1878, means by reflection [del]that[/del] one of three fundamental modes of consciousness, in which the objective and subjective aspects of what is present are discriminated without being necessarily separated into person and thing.

The particular [ins]reflection[/ins] [del]that[/del] states of consciousness are things, or that the subject is its objects, constitutes the reflective mode of consciousness. Perception is the rudimentary function in reflection as well as in primary consciousness; and reflective conception is a derivative from it. Hodgson, Philosophy of Reflection, B i 128.
