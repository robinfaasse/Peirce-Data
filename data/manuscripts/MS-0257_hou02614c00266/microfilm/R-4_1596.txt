[u]Theorem[/u]. If a surface is filled by two films which occupy no surface in common, they occupy a line in common.

[u]Demonstration[/u]. Let $\theta$ (thee'ta) be a surface occupied by the films $\gamma$ (gam'a) and $\delta$ (del'ta), which have no common surface. Then, I say, there is a line, $b$, common to the two films.

[fig]Circle divided by a line into regions gamma and delta[/fig] For let the two films move together, so as continually to altogether leave their surfaces. Then, by Definition, in the course of the motion they occupy solid spaces forming one solid space $\Theta$. The two solid spaces they occupy have, by the last Theorem, a surface in common. This surface is occupied [ins]according to Definition[/ins] in the course of time, by a filament. At the first instant, this filament, by Definition, occupies a line. Call this line, $b$. Then, $\gamma$ and $\delta$ must have this line in common. Q.E.D.

[u]Theorem[/u]. If a line is filled by two filaments which have no line in common, they have a point in common.

[u]Demonstration[/u]. Let $t$ be a line occupied by the filaments $c$ and $d$, having no line common to them.

[fig]Line t with segments c and d meeting at point p[/fig] Let the combined filaments move [del]without[/del] [del]breaking away from one another[/del] Then in the course of time they fill a surface and have a line in common. Hence, at the first instant, they must have a point in common.
