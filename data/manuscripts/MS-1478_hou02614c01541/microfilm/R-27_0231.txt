The manufactures of the United States must probably today
exceed those of Great Britain, Germany, and France put together,
even after deducting one fourth from ours for the illusory effect of the
protective tariff. Our population is about one fourth of theirs, and
manufacturing is not the chief industry of this country. This
extraordinary and proud superiority is due, in a general way,
to American systematization; but among more definite facts,
it is due chiefly to the absolute supremacy of American
machinery, which none other approaches in excellence other-
wise than by copying its merits. This supremacy is trace-
able, above all, to Eli Perkins having early worked out such
a system as to render the parts of machines interchangeable.
A symptom of this is seen in the fact that throughout the conti-
nent of Europe there are today but three systems of bolts
and screws, one of them, metrical, of recent introduction
and [del]altogether[/del] [ins]highly[/ins] injurious to those countries, helping to keep
their manufactures down, the second, that of Whitworth, an
inch system, but one which, on the whole, they would do
better to abandon, the third, by far the most scientific and
practical, the American system, also, of course, based
upon the inch. There can be but little doubt, on the part of
