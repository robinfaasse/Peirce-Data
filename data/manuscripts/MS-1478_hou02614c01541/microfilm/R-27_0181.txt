In discussions of the metric system the two parties indulge pretty equally in sophistry, perversion of truth, and childish nonsense. To blame them equally would be unjust; for such talk springs either from passion or from ignorance. As to passion, there is some color of excuse for a man's losing his temper when he thinks he sees his business [ins]en[/ins]jeopardied and the business of many of his fellows for the sake of a pretty idea without practical importance; while there is no such excuse for temper on the part of an advocate of the system. Many of those advocates are teachers; and one of the greatest [del]crimes[/del] sins a teacher can commit is to allow stupidity to make him angry. So as to ignorance, [del]the opponent of[/del] [ins]he who opposes[/ins] a change because he thinks he sees clearly a conclusive objection to it must be excused if he looks no further into the facts. Any other rule of conduct would fritter away all our time. But it is incumbent upon every man who proposes a great social change[del]s[/del] fully to inform himself of all its probable workings before he assumes the grave responsibility of actively urging its adoption. The volume under review illustrates a good deal of futility on both sides at which we shall glance presently.
