The greatest mischief is produced in politics, as everywhere else, by those who are always doubting things that have been settled long ago & who think they have a right to do so, without having objections which a both new & likely to prove solid. The following narration will be suf-ficient to show the danger of this procedure.

John Smith doubted that 60 times 16 was 960. ([fig]Multiplication of 16 by 60[/fig] [ins]$\frac{\begin{array}{c}16 \\ 60\end{array}}{960}$[/ins]) He supported his position by an experiment. On the [u]8[ins]th[/ins][/u] of [date]October 1868[/date], he calls together the members of the press and invites to the meeting the candidate for mayor whom he advocates, and then and there he opposes the Scholastic method with the Baconian method & he maintains his position to the satisfaction of his hearers — by an EXPERIMENT. The experiment, stripped of details, consists, 1[ins]st[/ins] in finding that 60 drops of water make a teaspoonful, 2[ins]nd[/ins] that 16 teaspoonfuls make a wine glassful, 3[ins]rd[/ins] that less than 960 drops fill the wineglass. This experiment having been made very rigidly & found correct — and anyone can repeat it for himself — a mathematician is asked for the proof that 60 times 16 is 960.
