[u]An Outline Sketch of the Synechistic Philosophy.[/u]

[u]The[/u] word [u]synechistic[/u], it is fitting to explain, is formed quite regularly from the Greek [u]συνεχής[/u], and naturally means ‘usually making continuous,’ which describes the main characteristic of that method of theorizing which Mr. C. S. Peirce entitles the [u]Synechistic Philosophy[/u]. The contrary to [u]synechistic[/u] will [del]naturally[/del] [ins]conveniently[/ins] be called [u]diechistic[/u]

Rational dissent from this philosophy can only come from disallowing the validity of the inferences upon which it rests. Precisely this is in dispute; so that a systematic discussion of the principles of logical inference must precede any attempt to set forth the proofs of the philosophy. But all logic rests upon deductive logic as its basis. With that therefore the argument must open.

Ordinary logic merely asks whether propositions are true or false, without recognizing any third grade of excellence. It thus ranks them on a scale of two. It deals with a scheme of quantity having only two values, like an arithmetical congruence of modulus 2. In this simple algebraical system, but one operation will be required, which will combine two propositions to make a third, whose value, as true or false, will depend on the values of those other two.
