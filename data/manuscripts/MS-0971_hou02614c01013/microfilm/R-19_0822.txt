Kants' refutation of idealism in the second
edition of the Critic of the Pure Reason has been
often held to be inconsistent with his main
position or even to be knowingly sophistical.
It appears to me to be one of the numerous passages
in that work which betray an elaborated and
vigorous analysis, marred in the exposition by
the attempt to state the argument more abstract-
ly and demonstratively than the thought would
warrant.

In "Note 1," Kant says that his argument
[del]turns the[/del] beats idealism at its own game. How
is that? The idealist says that all that we know
immediately, that is, otherwise than inferentially,
is what is present in the mind; and things out-
of the mind are not so present. The whole idealist
position turns upon this conception of the present.
