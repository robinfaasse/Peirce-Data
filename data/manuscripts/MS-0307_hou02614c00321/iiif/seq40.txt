It has often been noticed that the spontaneous
emissions of radioactive bodies consist of three distinct
types of radiation. The properties of these
are easily shown in the following table.

[fig]figure[/fig]

The <math>{\displaystyle \alpha }</math> rays are helium atoms with two positive
charges. They are emitted with high velocity. They
have great ionising power but are easily absorbed.
The <math>{\displaystyle \beta }</math> rays are negative electrons, emitted with velocities
up to that of light. They have less ionising power
than the alpha rays but are more penetrating.
The <math>{\displaystyle \gamma }</math> rays are electromagnetic waves of very short wave
length. They have very little ionising power but are 
very penetrating. 
The properties of radioactive bodies are independent
of their physical state, temperature, or pressure.
Radioactivity is an atomic phenomenon.
The radioactivity of a substance is measured by the
rate of decay of the substance. Each radioactive 
substance has a characteristic half-value period
which is the time taken for half the atoms of
the substance to disintegrate. 
[del]In addition[/del]
The [del]disintegration[/del] transformation theory states that
radioactive atoms are unstable. They disintegrate 
with the emission of [del]a[/del] particles, forming new
radioactive atoms with different properties. This 
process continues until a stable atom is reached. 
For example, radium disintegrates to form radon
(radium emanation), which in turn disintegrates to form 
radium A. The series continues until radium G
(lead) is reached. 
Radioactive substances are found in nature in three
series, the uranium series, the thorium series, and the 
actinium series.
