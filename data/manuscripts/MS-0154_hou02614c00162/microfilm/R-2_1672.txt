[pn]PCM[/pn] [u]On the Problem of Coloring a Map.[/u]

A map is an assemblage of regions, each of which is a definite surface consisting of a single piece, and no two of which have any superficial part in common.

If we ignore the distinction between different regions and consider them all as making up one surface, this latter may be called the [u]subject surface[/u] of the map.

The subject surface may consist of a multitude of [del]distinct[/del] separate pieces. Each of these pieces may have topical singularities, which are lines and points on the surface, from which the possibilities of motion are different from those of motion from ordinary lines and points on a surface. Line-singularities are either [u]cilia[/u], [del]that is, lines[/del] connected with the rest of the piece only at isolated points, or [u]limits[/u], that is, lines beyond which the surface does not continuously spread, or [del]lines of splitting[/del] [ins]splits[/ins], that is, lines from which different sheets diverge. Point-singularities are either extremities of cilia[del]s[/del], or [u]branch-points[/u] of cilia, or [u]binding-points[/u], that is, points where cilia join superficial parts, or [u]split-nodes[/u], that is points where splits [ins]are met[/ins] [del]meet[/del] by other splits or by limits, or [u]split-terminals[/u], that is, points on a split where certain sheets cease to be [ins]immediately[/ins] connected with other sheets, or [u]lacks[/u], that is, [del]point[/del] isolated points where different sheets are connected.
