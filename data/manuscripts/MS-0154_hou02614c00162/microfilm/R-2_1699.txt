[u]On the Problem of Coloring a Map.[/u]

[u]Definitions[/u]. An ordinary surface is a surface on which any two self-returning lines whatever that cross one another at one point cross one another at some other point.

A map is a figure covering a given surface and consisting of three kinds of parts, viz: 1[sup]st[/sup], [u]regions[/u], or superficial parts, each in one piece, no two having a superficial part in common, [del]and[/del] [ins]together[/ins] including [ins]and finite in number[/ins] every superficial part of the surface covered; 2[sup]nd[/sup], [u]boundaries[/u], or linear parts, each in one piece, no two having a linear part in common, each [del]one[/del] [ins]either[/ins] belonging to a single pair of [ins]different[/ins] regions, being [del]at[/del] every part of it at the mutual limit of those regions, and continuing as far as those two regions have a continuous mutual limit, or [del]else[/del] belonging to a single region [del]everywhere[/del] [ins]limited[/ins] along this line [del]limited[/del] by the limit of the surface covered [ins]by this map, the boundary continuing as far as [del]this is true[/del] [ins]the region continues to be so limited[/ins][/ins]; and 3[sup]rd[/sup], [u]corners[/u], or punctual parts, each a single point, where three or more regions mutually limit one another, or where two regions are together limited by the limit of the surface covered.
