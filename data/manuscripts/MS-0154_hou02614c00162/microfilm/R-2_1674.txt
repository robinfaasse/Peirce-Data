[marginalia]Map[/marginalia] [u]On the Problem of Coloring a Map.[/u]

A map is, for the purpose of this problem, nothing but an assemblage of regions, each of which is a surface consisting of a single piece, and no two of which have any superficial part in common, these regions making up together a surface which may be called the subject surface of the map.

A point may be said to [u]belong to[/u] a line, if it lies either upon or at an extremity of the latter. A point may be said to [u]belong to[/u] a surface, if it [del]either[/del] lies [del]either[/del] within the latter or belongs to its limiting edge. A line may be said to [u]belong to[/u] a surface, if every point that belongs to the former belongs also to the latter.

By a [u]boundary[/u], I mean a line which either, 1[ins]st[/ins], lies wholly on a limiting edge of the subject surface and which stretches as far as the same region [del]on[/del] has a continuous line [del]belong[/del] lying on that edge, or, 2[ins]nd[/ins], which belongs to at least two regions, every linear part of which belongs to the same set of regions and to no others, and which stretches as far as all those regions have a continuous line belonging to them and to no other region.
