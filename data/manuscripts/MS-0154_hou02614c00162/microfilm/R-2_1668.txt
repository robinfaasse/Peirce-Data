[pn]P 21[/pn]

[u]On the Problem of Coloring a Map.[/u]

§1. [u]Definitions[/u]. A map is a figure covering a given surface and consisting of three kinds of parts, viz.: —
[u]1[sup]st[/sup][/u], [u]regions[/u], or superficial parts, each in one piece, no two having a superficial part in common, and together including every superficial part of the surface covered;
[u]2[sup]nd[/sup][/u], [u]boundaries[/u], or linear parts, no two having a linear part in common, together including every linear part of [del]the[/del] [ins]every[/ins] limit of the surface covered (if it has any) together with every linear part of every mutual limit between two regions, each boundary either belonging to a single pair of different regions, being at every part of it as the mutual limit of these regions, and continuing as far as those [ins]two[/ins] regions have a continuous mutual limit, or else belonging [del]to[/del] [ins]to[/ins] a single region that is limited everywhere along this line by the limit of the surface covered, the boundary continuing as far as the region continues to be so limited; and [u]3[sup]rd[/sup][/u], [u]corners[/u], or punctual parts, each a single point where either three or more regions mutually limit one another, or where two regions are together limited by the limit of the surface covered.

Two regions are said to [u]confine[/u] to one another if they have a common boundary.
