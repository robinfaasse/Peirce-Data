Given certain conditions, [ins]$\aleph$,[/ins] (not necessarily altogether general), if any description, [u]$d$[/u], be such that, whatever description [u]$c$[/u] may be, there is a logical possibility that there should be a single object, $X$, and a description of ordered pair, $\mathcal{G}$, such that if $B$ be any individual object subject to the conditions, [ins]$\aleph$,[/ins] and to which [u]$d$[/u] and [u]$c$[/u] both apply, then the description, $\mathcal{G}$, applies to the ordered pair $(B:X)$, while if $B'$ be any object subject to the conditions $\aleph$ [del]but[/del] [ins]to[/ins] which [u]$d$[/u] and [u]$c$[/u] do not both apply, then the description, $\mathcal{G}$, does not apply to the ordered pair $(B':X)$, in that case, and only in that case, the object, — [u]ens rationis[/u], ([u]terminus[/u] for the Okhamist [u]formalitas[/u] for the Scotist) — whose being consists in this possibility conjoined to the applicability, or non-applicability of [u]$d$[/u] to definite objects conforming to $\aleph$, this object being considered as an individual identical with everything whose being consists in the same possibility and applicability, and also as capable of entering as a member into ordered pairs, is called [u]the collection of all the $d$'s which conform to $\aleph$[/u]. [del]Any[/del] [ins]An[/ins] individual object to which the description $d$ applies and which conforms to the conditions $\aleph$ is said to be a [u]unit of the collection[/u].

It may be objected to this definition that it is circular, inasmuch as it involves the idea of an ordered pair, which is a collection of two objects modified by distinguishing one of them, as first, from the other, as second. But I do not think that this objection is just, since we may adopt the following definitions. An ordered pair is an [u]ens rationis[/u] whose being depends upon an individual [del]object[/del], termed its [u]relate[/u], conforming to existential conditions while an individual, termed its [u]correlate[/u], conforms to existential conditions, possibly different from the others. A general description of an ordered pair is an [u]ens rationis[/u] whose being depends upon the possibility of enunciating [ins]any definite[/ins] proposition [del]mentioning[/del] [ins]about[/ins] "a relate" and "a correlate". Such a description is applicable to a given ordered pair, provided the proposition upon which it depends [del]is true[/del] when the relate and correlate of that pair are substituted respectively [del]for[/del] [ins]for[/ins] "relate" and "correlate" in that proposition.
