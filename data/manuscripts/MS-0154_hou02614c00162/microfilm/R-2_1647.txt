The main idea of the following group of definitions is adopted from Cantor:

If there is a possible relation in which every $A$ stands to some $B$ to which no other $A$ so stands, then the collection of all the $As$ is said to be (at least) as small as the collection of all the $Bs$, and the latter to be (at least) as great as the former. I omit the unnecessary words "at least."

If one collection is, at once, as small as and as great as another, it is said [del]to be[/del] [ins][u]equal[/u][/ins] to that other.

If one collection is [del]as small as but not as great as[/del] another, it is said [del]to be[/del] [ins][u]smaller than[/u][/ins] that other, which is said [del]to be[/del] [ins][u]greater than[/u][/ins] it.

In the Monist for (Vol. [ins]p.[/ins] ), I showed that of any two collections [ins]each[/ins] [del]whatever[/del] [del]either[/del] is either as small as or as great as the other; and I have [del]been[/del] told that Cantor, in a paper I have not read, had already proved the same proposition.

Evidently, the relation of being as small as is a transitive one. That is, if $A$ is as small as $B$, and $B$ is as small as $C$, then $A$ is as small as $C$. The same is consequently true of the relations of being equal to and being smaller than. Further, if $A$ is smaller than $B$, and $B$ is equal to $C$, $A$ is smaller than $C$; and if $A$ is greater than $B$, and $B$ is [del]greater[/del] equal to $C$, $A$ is equal to $C$.
