<u>Theorem IV.</u> [[del]Census Theorem.[/del]] The census-number [del]derived from[/del] [ins]of a whole place[/ins]
is equal to [del]that[/del] [ins]the sum[/ins] of those of the [ins]homogeneous[/ins] parts of that place.

<u>Illustrations.</u> 1st An ordinary closed surface has 2 for its census-
number. Hence, if it is divided into pieces [ins](called faces)[/ins] having edges, which
edges have extremities [ins](called vertices)[/ins] and no other singularities, then the sum
of the number of [del]surfaces[/del] [ins]faces[/ins] and vertices is 2 more than the
number of edges. [del]A[/del] Thus, four parts of such a surface
can all touch one another. For then the number of edges will
be $4 \times (4-1) \div 2$ and the number of vertices $4 \times (4-1) \div 3 = 4$. Now,
$4 + 4 = 6 + 2$. 2nd A [del]perissad[/del] surface has 1 for its census
number. Hence if it is divided as above, the sum of the faces
and vertices is one more than the number of edges. Hence,
6 parts of such a surface can all touch one another. For
$6 \times (6-1) \div 2 = 15$ and $6 \times (6-1) \div 3 = 10$ and $6 + 10 = 15 + 1$.
3rd A ring-surface has 0 for its census number. Hence if it is
divided in the same way, the sum of the numbers of faces and
vertices equals the number of edges. Hence, seven parts of
such a surface can all touch one another. For $7 \times (7-1) \div 2 = 21$
and $7 \times (7-1) \div 3 = 14$, and $7 + 14 = 21$.
