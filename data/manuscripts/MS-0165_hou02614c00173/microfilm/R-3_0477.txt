Königsberg: [date]1771[/date], pp. 49 ff.)[/del] It may occur to the reflective teacher that the doctrine taught in the text would lead to the conclusion that infinitesimals, in the strict and literal sense, are perfectly intelligible, contrary to the teaching of [del]all[/del] [ins]the great[/ins] body of modern text-books on the calculus.

It seems that mathematical opinion is in a [ins]state of[/ins] transition regarding infinitesimals. The researches of Weierstrass and others upon the theory of functions have led to many [del]fundamental[/del] changes of doctrine upon the fundamentals of the calculus. Especially, the works of Georg Cantor have produced a great impression upon mathematicians of late years. Cantor [ins]not only[/ins] shows that we can [del]not only[/del] reason mathematically concerning infinite quantities, but he has even gone so far as to construct a calculus of them. Though the leading theorems of Cantor are fully accepted, it does not seem to have occurred to anybody to apply his ideas to infinitesimals. The truth is, the question between the method of limits and the method of infinitesimals is of little interest to mathematicians, and is hardly noticed except in the introductions to [del]elementary[/del] treatises on the calculus. Supposing, for the moment, the current doctrine, that it is necessary to commence the calculus in the roundabout way now used, to be mistaken, the only way in which it is likely to be rectified is, by some elementary writer's teaching the contrary. The revolution, if there is to be one, must begin in an elementary work. I think the time is hardly ripe for that; and therefore I am careful in the text to say nothing which is not now fully recognized and accepted by mathematicians.
