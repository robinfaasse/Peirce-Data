[u]Theorem 50[/u]. Every plane and ray contain a common point.

[u]Demonstration[/u]. Let [math](\alpha)[/math] be any plane, and [math]\{b\}[/math] any ray. Then I say there is a point [math][\alpha b][/math] which is contained both in [math](\alpha)[/math] and in [math]\{b\}[/math]. For let [math](\gamma)[/math] and [math](\delta)[/math] be any two different planes containing [math]\{b\}[/math]. Then, by Corollary 99, there is a point [math][\alpha \gamma \delta][/math] which is contained in each of the planes [math](\alpha)[/math], [math](\gamma)[/math], [math](\delta)[/math]. And by Corollary 96, this point is contained in [math]\{b\}[/math] the common ray of the planes [math](\gamma)[/math], [math](\delta)[/math]. Thus, there is a point contained at once in [math](\alpha)[/math] and in [math]\{b\}[/math]. Q.E.D.

[u]Illustration 50[/u]. If we imagine one point to be luminous and another to be opaque, and the atmosphere is a little misty so as to show the shadow of the latter due to the former, then that shadow is [ins]assumed to be[/ins] the path the light would take, if the opaque point were not there. Of course, this quite leaves out of account diffraction which always comes in when we observe such phenomena with any precision; but this is only mentioned here to illustrate how we are not dealing with the real world but only with an idea. The shadow is a straight line. If it were continued round space to the luminous point and thence to the opaque point whence it takes its origin, it would be a complete ray. By
