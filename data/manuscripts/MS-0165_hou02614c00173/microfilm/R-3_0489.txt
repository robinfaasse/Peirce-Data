Section 5. Topical Classes of Surfaces

[del]Art. 35.[/del] [ins]Scholium 42.[/ins] Surfaces have other [del]features[/del] [ins]peculiar differences[/ins] even more important than their singularities. These are due to the features now to be described.

Definition [del]36.[/del] [ins]37. 118.[/ins] A [u]fornix[/u] is a [del]portion[/del] [ins]feature[/ins] of a surface such that it is possible to puncture the surface, and to cut it from the [del]puncture[/del] puncture back to the puncture, so as to produce two edges, without cutting the surface into two pieces.

[u]Illustrations.[/u] [del]51.[/del] Fornix is the Latin for a vault, possibly connected with [u]forare[/u], to bore. [ins]The plural [u]fornices[/u], is accented on the first syllable like [u]calices[/u] and [u]vofices[/u].[/ins] [del]In[/del] [ins]A[/ins] fornix in the geometrical sense is a bridge with a tunnel under it. Thus, the [ins]surface of the[/ins] handle of a flatiron is a fornix. [del]Any[/del] [ins]The surface of any[/ins] ring has a fornix, though there is no reason for [del]calling any[/del] [ins]identifying any[/ins] particular part as [del]the[/del] an attachment to the rest. The inner and outer surfaces of a tobacco-pipe form together a surface with a fornix. A cullender has as many fornices as there are holes in it. The surface of a button has as many fornices as there are holes through the button. The surface of a four-legged table with that of the floor make up a surface of [u]three fornices[/u]; for it may be cut through in three places without severing it into two parts. The surface of a ring is called a [u]torus[/u] and surfaces with fornices are called [u]fornicate[/u] or [u]toroidal[/u].

[u]Scholium 43.[/u] Lines have no features corresponding to fornices, except so far as the returning of a line into itself presents something analogous.
