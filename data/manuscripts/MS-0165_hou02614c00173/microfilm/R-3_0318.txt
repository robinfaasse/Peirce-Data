[ins]Art 29.[/ins] Notation 7. The sign of multiplication is a [ins]decussate, or St. Andrew's[/ins] cross, [u]x[/u]; that is to say, [u]a[/u] x [u]b[/u] means the product of [u]a[/u] and [u]b[/u]. A dot can be used instead of the cross; or the sign may be entirely omitted and the factors simply written one after another. In this case, the parenthesis about them is always omitted. Thus [u]ab[/u] + [u]c[/u] means ([u]a[/u] x [u]b[/u]) + [u]c[/u], never [u]a[/u] x ([u]b[/u] + [u]c[/u]). In reading, omit all mention of multiplication where the sign is omitted, read . "dot" and x "times". But [u]a[/u] ([u]b[/u] + [u]c[/u]) is generally read "[u]a[/u] times [u]b[/u] plus [u]c[/u]," or "[u]a[/u] into [u]b[/u] + [u]c[/u]"

Notation 8. The long sign of division is [u]÷[/u], the dividend preceding, the divisor following the sign. But the fractional form has at all times been the most usual mode of writing a quotient. There is a horizontal line called a virgula, or virga, above which is written the dividend, called the numerator, and below the divisor called the denominator. [del]In[/del] A modification of the fraction consists in writing the numerator and denominator on the same line [del]the former preceding[/del] [ins]between them[/ins] [del]the latter[/del] with an oblique line [u]/[/u], [del]like[/del] [ins]like[/ins] a bar sinister, but called a solidus. The colon, or sign of ratio, [u]:[/u], is also used by many mathematicians as a sign of division, the antecedent being arbitrarily called the dividend, the consequent, the divisor. The proportion

2 : 6 : 10 :: 3 : 9 : 15

means that one number may multiply each of the numbers 2, 6, 10 and another each of the numbers 3, 9, 15 so as to make the first three products respectively equal to the last three. This has suggested that (:) be [ins]made[/ins] a sign of division. [del]to which there is no great objection. But a ratio is not a quotient.[/del] Some purists object to regarding a ratio as [del]exactly[/del] the same as a quotient; but the objection seems unmathematical and futile.
