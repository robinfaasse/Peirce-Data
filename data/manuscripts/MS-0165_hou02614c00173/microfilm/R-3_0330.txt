ALGEBRA OF WHOLE NUMBERS.

<u>Art. 34. Scholium 12.</u> We now proceed to prove the elementary
algebraic truths about whole numbers.

These propositions are mostly so obvious, that the student will ask,
what is the use of proving such things? [ins]this question is a very natural[/ins] [del]there are two or three reasons.[/del]
one; but the pupil's good sense will show him that he cannot at
the outset of a mental discipline fully appreciate or comprehend
any attempt to explain the nature of the service it is to render him.

The principal reason for giving [ins]the[/ins] proofs which follow about numbers
is this. Mathematics is one of the most useful of studies, if successful-
ly prosecuted; but it is so difficult that, without a careful system of
teaching, there is danger the student may entirely fail to gain any
[del]mastery[/del] [ins]command[/ins] of it. It presents difficulties of several different kinds.

Now, one of the axioms of teaching and learning is to divide diffi-
culties, and conquer them one by one. One of the main difficulties
with elementary mathematics is to get the idea of a mathematical de-
monstration. We have had a bit of practice in mathematical thought,
already. But the formal and artificial logic of mathematical demon-
strations has not yet been well illustrated. It is far better to learn
the character of [del]math[/del] demonstration in a branch which presents no other
difficulties; so that, when we go on to other subjects having other
kinds of difficulties, the scholar's mind may be free to deal with
these by themselves.

Besides, these proofs and others in different branches of the
science have a real value, apart from their use in convincing
us of the truth of the propositions. Namely, they show how the different
properties of the subject-matter [ins]in this case, the system of num-[/ins]
bers, — are related to one another, and how the whole plan of study-
ing them hangs together. But this cannot be made very clear before-
hand.

Finally, however certain things may appear, yet if we take them
for granted without perfect proof, it is probable fully as certain
that a percentage of them will not really be true. Looking into
the proof of things that appear certain in advance is insuring ourselves
against a [del]deep[/del] risk. It is like insuring a house against fire. The pro-
bability of its being burned within a year is excessively slight; but then
the [del]conseq[/del] inconvenience would be very serious if it should burn.
