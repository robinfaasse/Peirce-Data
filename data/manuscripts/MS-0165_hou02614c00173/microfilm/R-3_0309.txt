It may be added that algebra was formerly called
[u]Cossic[/u], in English, or the [u]Rule of Cos[/u]; and the first algebra
published in England was called "The Whetstone of Wit",
because the author supposed that the word [u]cos[/u] was the
Latin word [del]of that[/del] so spelled, which means a whetstone.
But, in fact, [u]cos[/u] was derived from the Italian, [u]cosa[/u], [del]thing[/del]
the thing you want to find, the unknown quantity whose
value is sought. It is the Latin [u]caussa[/u], a thing aimed at, a cause.

[del]Gloss[/del] [ins]18[/ins]. The word number is used with propriety in
various senses; but it will contribute to clearness to restrict
it here, as much as possible, to the following meaning:

[u]Definition 20[/u]. A number is a sign, belonging to a
sequence of such signs, used to show the relative places of
different objects in one sequence, and to compare the
places of objects in corresponding sequences.

[u]Illustration 21[/u]. The [del]words[/del] vocables
Eeny meny mony mi
Pasca lona bona stry,
used by children in counting out, are a kind of num-
bers, though used for a very restricted purpose. In fact
they are derived from the [del]nam[/del] [ins]Romany[/ins] numerals. They well
illustrate the fact that numbers have no general signification
but are a mere apparatus for exhibiting relations.
