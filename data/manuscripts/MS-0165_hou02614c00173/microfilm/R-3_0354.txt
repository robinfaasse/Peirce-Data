Having thus conducted the pupil through all the dry and dusty part of the doctrine of whole numbers, it is distressing to have to turn from the path, just as it is about to open into one of the most delightful regions of pure thought. But that is the condition which [del]the ideas of the[/del] [ins]circumstances[/ins] [del]prevalent ideas[/del] impose upon the teacher of elementary mathematics.

It is indispensible in mathematics, when we find ourselves hampered by exceptions, owing to the narrow and arbitrary character of our hypotheses, to enlarge our hypotheses [ins]so as to[/ins] get rid of their angularity.

If our numbers are only to serve for counting collections, there can certainly be no number less than [u]zero[/u]. And thus we have the irregularity that some numbers can be subtracted from others, while some cannot. Operations of such partial applicability give rise to irregularities and complexities, without end. They are to be avoided. Considering a simple sparse sequence, of which the system of whole numbers from 0 up is a particular case, there is no reason why this must have a first object.

Nor is the counting of collections the only use to which the whole numbers are ordinarily put. We also use them, for indicating places in entirely unlimited sequences.
