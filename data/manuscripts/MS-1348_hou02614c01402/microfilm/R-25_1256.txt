The electrolysis of a solution of sodium
chloride gives primarily sodium at the cathode
and chlorine at the anode. The liberated sodium
at once decomposes the water, setting free hydrogen
and forming sodium hydrate. The conductivity
of the hydrate is 2.2 that of the chloride and it
is very rapidly diffused through the liquor.
The action of the chlorine is complicated. A part
of it acts upon the water according to the for-
mula

$$2\text{ Cl}_2 + 2\text{ H}_2\text{O} = 4\text{ HCl} + \text{O}_2.$$

But this action, in the absence of coloring-
matter or other reducing agent to take up
the oxygen, is very slow, especially in a dim
light; for water is extremely inert to direct
action. My analyses show that only 7 per
cent of the chlorine is thus converted into
hydrochloric acid. Nearly all the rest of the
chlorine acts upon the sodium hydrate,
which is very rapidly diffused, to make
