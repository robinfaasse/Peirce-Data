Let $p$ be the molecular proportion of $\text{NaCl}$
in the solution; so that that of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ is $1-p$.
The $\text{NaCl}$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ are continually undergoing
double decomposition at a slow rate propor-
tional to the product of their concentrations.
If $c$ is a [ins]certain[/ins] small constant, the rapidity of
this action is $c p (1-p)$. In this way $\text{HCl}$
and $\text{NaOH}$ are formed in equivalent pro-
portions, say $x$. But these rapidly undergo
double decomposition, at a rate proportional
to the product of their concentrations; so that
if $C$ is a certain large constant, the rapidity
of this action will be $C x^2$. When equili-
brium is reached, these two rapidities are
equal, and $x = \sqrt{\frac{c}{C} p (1-p)}$. Or, if we wish
to be so precise as to take account of the
loss of $\text{NaCl}$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ in forming $\text{HCl}$ and
$\text{NaOH}$, the equation will be,
$$C x^2 = c (p-x) (1-p-x);$$
and the approximate solution of this is
$$x = \sqrt{\frac{c}{C-c} p (1-p)} - \frac{1}{2} \frac{c}{C-c}.$$
