The electrolysis of a solution of sodium
chloride gives primarily sodium at the cathode
and chlorine at the anode. The sodium at
once decomposes the water, liberating hydrogen
and forming sodium hydrate. The [ins]molecular[/ins] conductivity
of the hydrate is 2.2 that of the chloride, and
it is rapidly diffused through the liquor. The
action of the chlorine is complicated. It is
at first dissolved in the water, and if the
liquor from the anode is drawn off it can
be distinctly smelled. But before its color
becomes perceptible, the hydrate reaches
it, and sodium hypochlorite and chloride
are formed in equivalent proportions, thus:
$\text{Cl}_2 + 2\text{NaOH} = \text{NaOCl} + \text{NaCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$.
A small and variable amount of chlorate
is also formed according to the formula
$3\text{Cl}_2 + 6\text{NaOH} = \text{NaO}_3\text{Cl} + 5\text{NaCl} + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}$.
[del]At the same[/del] Mean time, the chlorine is
slowly decomposing the water in which it is
dissolved, according to the formula
$2\text{Cl}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} = 4\text{HCl} + \text{O}_2$.
