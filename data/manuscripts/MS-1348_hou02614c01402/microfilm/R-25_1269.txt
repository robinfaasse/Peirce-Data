Of [u]insoluble lime salts[/u], the only one of
importance is the carbonate. Therefore, we may
add to the water, after this has been neutralized
by lime water, the right quantity of a soluble,
normal carbonate, — say carbonate of am-
monia, of potassa, or of soda. Probably, the
last would be the cheapest.

Carbonate of soda and sulphate of lime
would make
Carbonate of lime and sulphate of soda.
The carbonate of lime would [del]g[/del] soon settle as
a precipitate. The sulphate of soda is a purging
salt; but in most cases the quantity would
be insignificant.

Of [u]insoluble sulphates[/u], the most important are
the sulphate of baryta and the sulphate of
lead. One grain of the former requires 15 gallons
of water for its solution. Therefore, if a soluble
salt of baryta is added, in solution, in the right
proportion, say the chloride (which can be obtained
at a very low price)
Chloride of barium and sulphate of lime
would make
Chloride of calcium and sulphate of baryta.
The chloride of calcium is a deliquescent salt, entirely
unobjectionable. The sulphate of baryta is deposited.
