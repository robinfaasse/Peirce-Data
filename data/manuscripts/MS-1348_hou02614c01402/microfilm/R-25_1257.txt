The electrolysis of a solution of chloride of sodium gives primarily sodium at the cathode and chlorine at the anode. The liberated sodium at once decomposes the water, liberating hydrogen and forming sodium hydrate. The hydrate conducts about 2.2 as well as the chloride. The action of the chlorine is complicated. In the first place, it acts upon the water, according to the formula
[u]2Cl[del]l[/del][ins]₂[/ins] + 2H₂O = [del]4[/del] HCl + O₂[/u].
But this action, in the absence of a reducing agent, is very slow, especially in a dim light; for water is exceedingly inert to direct action upon it as water. Consequently, only about 7 per cent of the chlorine is destroyed in that way.[ins]*[/ins] In the second place, the chlorine acts upon the sodium hydrate. It may be objected that the sodium hydrate is not at the anode but at the cathode.
