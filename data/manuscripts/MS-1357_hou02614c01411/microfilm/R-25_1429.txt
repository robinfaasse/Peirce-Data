[del]X[/del] We are in possession of no other way of solving a problem with plural solutions than by proceeding backwards from a solution assumed to a datum, or from a parameter or parameters to both solution and datum. At least, this is true of the algebraic part of the solution. Indeed, this is what the method of analysis consists in, which is not to be regarded as coextensive with algebra[ins]*[/ins]. I do not know that there is no other possible way of solving such problems; probably not; but none has been developed. As for the arithmetical part of the calculation, this (barring exceptional cases) must proceed substantially by approximation. But it is not necessary that anything like the rule of false should be used.[ins]†[/ins] Probably the method of approximation is never necessary where there is but one solution[ins]‡[/ins]; but it is in many cases preferable.

In funicular problems, such as that of the suspension bridge, there will be a difficulty in assuming a solution, since it may not conform to terminal conditions[ins]||[/ins]. This difficulty may be overcome in, at least, two ways. We may, in the first place, exclude all absolute constraints from the mathematical hypothesis, substituting high elasticities, as in nature. Although this proceedure complicates the problem very much, yet if the problem is very complicated at best, the advantages of this mode of treatment may very well outweigh the disadvantages.

* Note A † Note B ‡ Note C || Among terminal conditions I include those of the lengths of the spans etc.
