That this rule is only a special case of the polynomial Theorem will
readily be admitted, because it results from Taylor's theorem, which
is but an application of the binomial theorem, which is only another
form of expression of the polynomial theorem. But in order to show
just how the rule results directly from the polynomial theorem, it
is necessary to consider the "admirations", that is, the numbers 2!,
3!, 4!, etc. We may define [u]n[/u]! as [u]n[/u]([u]n[/u]-1)! This definition
leaves 1! an arbitrary constant; but no inconvenience will
result from that. Only 1! is understood to be finite. Since, then,
by the definition ([u]n[/u]-1)! = [u]n[/u]! / [u]n[/u], it follows that 0! is 1! / 1, i.e.
equals 1! Also (-1)! = 0! / 0, that is, is infinite; and all the
admirations of finite negative numbers are infinite. Their ratios,
however, may be obtained from the definition which gives [u]n[/u]! / ([u]n[/u]-1)! = [u]n[/u].
That is, (-1)! / (-2)! = -1, (-2)! / (-3)! = -2, whence (-1)! / (-3)! = (-1)(-2) = 2, etc.
