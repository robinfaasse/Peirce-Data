[u]On the Simplest Branch of Mathematics, Dyadics.[/u]

By C. S. Peirce.

The purposes of this memoir are, [ins]1[u]st[/u],[/ins] [del]to develope[/del] [ins]lay the foundations of[/ins] the simplest possible theory of pure mathematics, [del]and to[/del] [ins]furnish[/ins] a system of diagrams for the study of it, and to deduce some of its main propositions; 2[u]nd[/u], to apply this theory to deductive logic, and to draw certain corollaries, not strictly mathematical but logically necessary; 3[u]rd[/u], to use these principles to clear up certain doubts in the theory of [del]Multitude[/del] [ins]“Multitude[/ins], or “Cardinal Numbers”; 4[u]th[/u], to present some reflections [del]of[/del] resulting from these studies concerning the proper method of investigating topical geometry.

[u]First Section. Hypotheses as to Definitions and as to Physical Necessities.[/u]

1. [u]Let us suppose that there is something called a “sheet.”[/u]

2. [u]Let us suppose that there is but one “sheet.”[/u]

3. [u]Let us suppose that the sheet is an individual of a class of objects, called “areas.”[/u]

4. [u]Let us suppose that there is a class of [ins]possible[/ins] objects called “points” and a relation, called “being upon”; and let us suppose that, given any “area” whatever, it is always physically possible that there should be a “point” “upon” it, other than any “point” already “upon” it.[/u]
