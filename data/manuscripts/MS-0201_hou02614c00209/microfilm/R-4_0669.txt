[pn]111[/pn] [u]Long division becomes singularly simple in secundal arithmetic.[/u] All that guessing at the successive figures of the divisor that used so to perplex us as school-boys, when our guesses, on being tried, would often turn out to be too small or, still worse, too great, — all of which, it is true, clean vanishes when we have once dressed a table of multiples of the divisor, — is yet, in [del]the[/del] secundal arithmetic avoided without recourse to any table, inasmuch as no other multiple than the divisor itself is ever called for. [del][[/del] For an example, see below. [del]][/del] In case the entire circulating secundal should be desired, — and although the necessity of the fact is obvious, it may be worth while to call attention to impotence of the nine significant figures of decimals to reduce circulating fractions to any simpler forms, on the average, than those of the secundal system, — the division may be very simply performed by this following rule: First [del]treat[/del] [ins]treat[/ins] unity as the multiplicand, afterward multiplying the quotient by the desired multiplicand.
