[u]The Boolean Calculus[/u].

The Boolean calculus falls between two stools: mathematicians do not care for it, because it is too easy; nor logicians, because it is too hard. Both parties are wrong. Oh ye mathematicians! Consider Schubert's denumerative geometry. It is nothing but an application of the Boolean calculus; and if it is very easy, the problems it solves were not so, before this way of solving them were found out. They seemed, indeed, to be beyond human powers. But ye do not interest yourselves in Schubert's calculus, nor in any form of the logical calculus. Why? It is, perhaps, [del]for[/del] because it does not seem to open vistas of wonderful generalizations. That, I believe, is simply because of the unraised powers of us who have began to cultivate it. Give it a shove, [u]apply it to problems of continuity[/u], and you will begin to find it has a future.

Oh ye logicians! This thing seems strange to you, and ye are not lovers of the strange nor of the novel. You are apt to think a man a simpleton who advances any abstract proposition without a secret intention of [del]showing off[/del] [ins]shooting off[/ins]
