[u]Two Fallacies[/u].

Practically speaking, all formal fallacies involve confusion between [u]some[/u] and [u]all[/u]. But it is difficult to find illustrations of this sort of thing which can really exercise the reasoning powers of any but the veriest dolts, unless they are run over very rapidly. An excellent exercise is to make a [ins]brief[/ins] list of examples, similar to those in Whately's Logic, and read them to the class at the rate of one every 20 seconds, the ordinal number of each being called out. Each member of the class has paper ruled in four columns for Correct, Undistributed Middle, Illicit Process of the Minor, [del]for the time is insufficient for more[/del] [ins]which[/ins] Process of the Major, and he sets down the ordinal numbers of as many as he can in their proper column. The exercise should not continue over ten minutes without repose [del]for they time thirty will have been read. to practice[/del] [ins]in this time thirty will have been read. To practice[/ins] is not only improving, but affords a good measure of a certain kind of mental alertness.
