[u]universal[/u],

* 4. In logic (a) one of the five predicables, genus, species, difference, property, accident, which name the different logical varieties of predicables.

(b) A general term or predicate. In logic, the word universal has entirely lost its etymological signification. The universe is a unitary collection, logically an individual, and the existent is that which is in dynamical relation, [ins]or association by contiguity,[/ins] [del]with[/del] a certain single series of experiences; it is thus not universal in the logical sense, [ins]namely[/ins] [del]not[/del] characterized by certain relations of reason, namely not connected with experience by associations of resemblance merely. But though [del]the[/del] [ins]a[/ins] universal is in its universality not contracted to actual existence, it does not follow that things real have [del]so[/del] in their real existence no universal predicates. This is, however, the doctrine of nominalism, which admits that real things may have dynamical relations, but denies that they have [del]any[/del] real resemblances or any intelligible characters whatever.
