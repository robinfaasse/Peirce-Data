[u]Scholium 1.[/u]
§1. [del]Whole Numbers.[/del] The properties of whole numbers are due entirely to the use of them in counting.

[u]Definition 1.[/u]
The process of counting, typically described, is as follows: We have a lot of things to be counted; we touch with the fore-finger, or otherwise indicate (Latin, [u]indicare[/u], whence [u]index[/u], [ins]not to be confounded with [u]indicĕre[/u], to proclaim[/ins]), one of them, and pronounce the word, [u]One[/u]; we indicate another and say, [u]Two[/u]; we indicate still a different one, and say, [u]Three[/u]; and so we go on as long as [ins]any[/ins] [del]one[/del] remains unindicated; if things of the [del]lot[/del] [ins]lot would[/ins] always remain, the count cannot be completed [ins](and the collection is said to be inenumerable)[/ins]; but if after [del]a[/del] [ins]a[/ins] while no more remain, the last numeral word pronounced marks the character of the lot in respect to multitude.

Thus, the numerals are words without signification, in the sense in which we say that "sewing-machine," "scholar," "book," [del]laws[/del] signify something. They have just as much and no more meaning than,
Eny meny mony me
Pasca lena bona stry.
