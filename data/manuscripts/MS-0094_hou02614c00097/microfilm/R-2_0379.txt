[del]60.[/del] [ins]Lemma.[/ins] Two different triangles cannot be formed on a given line $AB$ (fig. 33), of which the sides, $AD$ and $DB$, are respectively equal to $CA$ and $CB$, and terminate at the same extremities of $AB$.

[u]Proof.[/u] For, first, the vertex $D$ of one triangle cannot fall within the other triangle $ACB$, as in fig. 19, because, by § [del]40[/del] [ins]196[/ins], $AD + DB$ must in this case be less than $AC + CB$.

Secondly. If $D$ falls without $ACB$, as in fig. 33, the triangles $ACD$ and $BCD$ are isosceles, since $AC$ is equal to $AD$ and $BC$ is equal to $BD$.

Hence [del]$\angle ACD = \angle ADC$,[/del]
and [del]$\angle BCD = \angle BDC$;[/del]

but this is impossible; for of the first members of these equations
$\angle ACD > \angle BCD$
while of the second members
$\angle ADC < \angle BDC$.

[del]61.[/del] [ins]Theorem.[/ins] When two triangles are equilateral [ins]with respect to each other,[/ins] they must be equal, and must also be equiangular with respect to each other.

[u]Proof.[/u] Let $ABC$ and $DEF$ (fig. 31) be the triangles, whose sides $AB$, $BC$, and $AC$ are respectively equal to $DE$, $EF$, and $DF$.

If $DE$ is placed upon $AB$, the point $F$ must by the preceding proposition fall upon $C$, and the triangles must coincide.
