§117.
[u]Scholium[/u]. When we look upon any scene, a ray from each visible point is the path of light to the eye. Let a plane of glass be set up between, and let the point where each ray to the eye cuts the plane of the glass be marked upon the latter. Then, as far as the shapes of things are concerned, the picture so produced, seen from just that relative point of view, will look just like the natural scene, viewed with one eye, and disregarding focal accommodation of the eye [ins]and diffraction, etc.[/ins] The art of placing the points of a picture so that they will appear to the spectator as if the rays from the natural objects pierced the picture as a glass window is called [u]Linear Perspective[/u].

This art is not only useful to the artist, but is still more indispensible to a person who may wish to interpret [del]a perspective picture or[/del] a scene before him and judge how the different objects he sees are really situated. But what most concerns us, as students of geometry, is, that it is through the study of perspective, and the problems it suggests, that we can most easily attain an understanding of space, and that we gain a philosophical comprehension of the relations of its different properties.
