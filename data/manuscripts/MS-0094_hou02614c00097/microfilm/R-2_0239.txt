§123 [u]Theorem [del]19[/del][/u]. The delineation of a ray cuts the original ray.
[del]Demonstration[/del]
[u]Proof[/u]. The [del]original[/del] delineation lies in the plane of the station point and the original ray. But two rays in one plane have a point in common. ([del]Theorem 1, corollary[/del]).

§124 [u]Theorem 20[/u]. The points at which the delineations of three rays [ins]which intersect in three points[/ins] [del]each other of these three points[/del] respectively cut their originals lie on one ray (are collinear.)
[del]Demonstration[/del]
[u]Proof[/u]. The three points lie in one plane; and the three original rays lie in that plane. Their delineations lie in the plane of delineation. Hence, the intersections of the original rays with their delineations lie both in the plane of the three points and in the plane of delineation. But two planes have only a ray in common. Hence, these three intersections lie in the ray [del]of[/del] in which these two planes intersect.

§125 [u]Scholium[/u] In order to draw a straight line, the student provides himself with a straight edge. And looks along the edge to [del]make[/del] sure it coincides with a ray of light.

In order to draw a line which meets a given line at the firmament, we make use of a ruler along which slides one side of a triangle, [del]another[/del] side being a straight edge.

[fig]Drawing of a ruler and a sliding triangle[/fig] Keeping the ruler fixed we slide the triangle into a new position for every new line, and the lines so drawn [del]will[/del] may be assumed to meet at the firmament, for when con- [del]tinued to a very great distance they will appear to meet there[/del].
