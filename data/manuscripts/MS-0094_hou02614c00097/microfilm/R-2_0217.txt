[u]§113. Scholium.[/u]

the congruency.

[u]Definition 153[/u]. The [u]class[/u] of a congruency is the number of its rays that lie in any fixed plane having no special relation to the congruency.

[del]Definition 154. The order-class of a congruency is the sum of its order and its class.[/del]

If the student has a good mathematical imagination he can see that [ins]since[/ins] each ray [del]being[/del] [ins]is[/ins] able to move [ins]in[/ins] [del]two[/del] [ins]independent[/ins] ways, the congruency is enveloped by a surface; and moreover in these two ways of motion there are only two points on the ray which are restricted to the surface; so that every ray [ins]of the congruency[/ins] touches the surface in two places. If the student can see this, it will give him a clearer idea of a congruency; but if he cannot, it will not prevent his understanding the present demonstration. This is merely an interjected scholium.

[del]Considering[/del] [ins]In[/ins] an echinus, or the doubly continuous [ins]multitude of[/ins] rays [ins]through one point[/ins], as a congruency, its order is [u]one[/u], [del]its class zero, its order-class[/del] [ins]for through its centre just one ray can be drawn to any other fixed point (Cor. 1 (5)).[/ins] Its class is [u]zero[/u], for there is no ray [del]common to[/del] [ins]containing[/ins] a fixed point and [ins]contained in a fixed[/ins] plane (unless the plane passes through the point); for if there were, since there are [del]two[/del] [ins]a multitude[/ins] of planes containing each ray, there would be [del]two[/del] [ins]a multitude of[/ins] planes containing a fixed point and a fixed ray not containing that point, contrary to Cor 1 (6). Hence, the [del]order-class[/del] [ins]of[/ins] the echinus is [u]one[/u]. (The surface which [del]its[/del] [ins]rays[/ins] twice touch degenerates to a single point [del]which counts for two[/del])
