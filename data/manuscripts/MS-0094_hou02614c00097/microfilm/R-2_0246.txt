The art of linear perspective is not merely useful in drawing, for which purpose a perfect theoretical knowledge of it is not indispensible to an artist of keenly accurate perceptions and [del]a[/del] vivid imagination. It is also valuable in enabling us accurately to interpret a perspective picture or a scene before us, and from the appearance to understand how the different objects are really situated. But what renders it most important to the student of geometry is that in it lies the best key to the science of space. [u]Scholium[/u]. Two great faults [ins]of perspective[/ins] are ordinarily committed by artists. The first is that of exaggeration, The treatises on the subject inculcate this. A perspective picture is drawn to be looked at with one eye (for binocular perspective is impossible without a stereoscope) from a particular stand-point. If the picture is otherwise viewed, its perspective is falsified. Now, artists have the habit of constructing their linear perspective as if the picture were to be viewed from a distance of a foot or less, while the whole treatment is such that it requires to be seen from a distance five times as great.
