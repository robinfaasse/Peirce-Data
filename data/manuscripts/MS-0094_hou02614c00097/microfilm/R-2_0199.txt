§102. Scholium. A spacious curve skims at any point along a plane. Of the book of planes hinged around its tangent ray at that point, there is one which it leaves, as it were, reluctantly. Take a point on the curve and two other points on the curve on opposite sides of the first. These three points are contained in one plane. Take the two points last mentioned successively nearer and nearer the first and [del]the plane through them[/del] [ins]there is an endless succession of planes through them. As[/ins] they approach the first [del]having that[/del] [ins]point for their limit,[/ins] these planes approach a limiting plane. This plane considered as moving along the curve, perpetually turning round the tangent ray is called the [u]osculating plane[/u]. It envelopes a surface which is filled up with the tangent rays, [ins]and[/ins] each a fibre, in moving successively [del]from[/del] into occupation of these, turns perpetually about an axis in its point of contact. Such a surface is called a [u]developable[/u]; in some respects developables differ radically from other surfaces. The curve is nothing but a line in that surface, where every section of the surface has a cusp. It is called the [u]cuspidal edge[/u], or [u]edge of regression[/u], of the surface.

[del]One[/del] [ins]Two[/ins] of the most [del]marked[/del] [ins]significant[/ins] features of a spacious curve are the number of its [u]apparent nodes[/u], that is, the number of nodes in central projection of it, and the number of its [u]apparent bitangents[/u], that is the number of rays which twice touch such a projection.
