<u>Fundamental Figures.</u>

§97. The Point, the Ray, and the Plane are used to generate most of the figures of geometry by the motions and otherwise; and shapes are described with reference to the [ins]numbers of[/ins] points, rays, and planes which are in stated relations to them. Hence, they are called the <u>chief elements</u> of space.

[marginalia]Definition 139.[/marginalia] Every [ins]genus of[/ins] figure has a constant-number, which is the number of independent ways in which any one figure of the genus has to be varied in order to get all the figures of the genus.

Thus, the constant-number of the point is 3, because, starting with one point, it may be moved all along a line, which line may be moved all over a surface, and this surface all through space to reach all [del]possible[/del] the points.

In like manner, the constant-number of the plane is 3, because, starting with any one, this may be rotated round any line in it, this line may be turned in a plane round any point on it, and the plane of turning may rotate round the original line. Or, we can make the calculation as follows: A plane passes through any three points. Now, 3 is the constant-number of one point, and therefore 9 is the constant-number of three points. But 2 is the constant-number of [del]points in the[/del] [ins]a definite[/ins] plane, and therefore 6 is the constant-number of 3 points in a definite plane. [del]Sub[/del]tracting 6 from 9, we get 3 for the constant number of planes in space.
