§68. The cases in which some of the known quantities are
infinite are sometimes important in geometry. [del]The[/del] [ins]Any[/ins] equation
in which such a quantity occurs may conveniently be divided
by that quantity. A finite quantity divided by an infinite
quantity is zero. Thus, if [u]a[/u] is infinite, but [u]b[/u] and [u]m[/u]
finite, we divide the two quantities equated in the first equation
by [u]a[/u], and in place of
[u]a[/u] [u]x[/u] + [u]b[/u] [u]y[/u] = [u]m[/u]
we get
[del][u]x[/u] = 0.[/del] [u]x[/u] + 0.[u]y[/u] = 0.
One infinite quantity divided by another gives a quotient
which cannot be ascertained [del]by[/del] from the values of the nu-
merator and denominator. If two infinite quantities occur,
we must generally know what their ratio is, or we cannot
determine the values of [u]x[/u] and [u]y[/u].
