Chapter VII
Branches of Geometry.

§23. [ins]Definition 73.[/ins] From the Constitution of Space naturally follows a division of geometry into three [del]or[/del] [ins]n[/ins] branches, viz: —

1. Topology, which traces out the consequences of the intrinsic characters of space, its being continuous, uniform, homaloidal, and tridimensional, but which recognizes no dominant system of homaloids (does not, for instance, care whether lines are curved or straight, nor whether surfaces are flat or rounded) and [del]nor[/del] does [del]it[/del] [ins]not[/ins] [del]concern itself[/del] consider measurements.

2. Graphics, or Projective Geometry, which is the study of geometry by means of the dominant system of [ins]optical[/ins] homaloids, i.e. Rays and Planes. The questions which it investigates are: how many intersections and tangents there are, and the like.

3. Metrics, which is the study of geometry by means of lengths and [del]and[/del] angles.

These three branches are [ins]distinguished[/ins] [del]distinguished as follows[/del] [ins]in a striking manner[/ins] by the kinds of [del]bodies[/del] with which they deal.

Topology considers fibres and laminas which can [ins]perfectly[/ins] be bent, limp and extensible, mere glairs, or rather they are fluid.

Graphics considers fibres and laminas which if homaloidal remain so, and if [del]Rays or Planes[/del] straight or flat remain so, but which are freely expanded & contracted so far as this can be done without violating the optical condition (i.e. that straight fibres remain straight).

Metrics considers fibres and laminae which are rigid and when moved preserved all the angles and distances of their parts unchanged.
