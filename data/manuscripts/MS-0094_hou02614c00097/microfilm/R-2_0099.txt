§ 4. Two or more surfaces placed one upon another are generally regarded as [ins]separate[/ins] preserving their identity ([del]though this[/del] [ins]apprehending the matter[/ins] [del]is a mere way of regar[/del] [ins]looking at it[/ins]) even though they coincide in part, or wholly. The connectivity of the whole is then to be reckoned like that of any other group of surfaces. Consider a star-shaped surface. Imagine this [fig]A five-pointed star with lines radiating from the center[/fig] to be generated by the motion of a line which turns about a point in the middle where one extremity [del]is[/del] remains fixed, while the line lengthens or shortens so as to keep the other extremity on the boundary. It is plain that the interior part will be entirely covered before the whole boundary has been described, and will, when the whole boundary is described, be covered two or more times.

[fig]A five-pointed star with lines radiating from the center[/fig] Let us determine the connectivity of a five-spoked star. The central part is twice covered. Let the [ins]projecting[/ins] spokes shrink down to nothing and [del]there are[/del] [ins]the result will be[/ins] two disks in contact and [del]with[/del] [ins]having[/ins] a cross-connection. We cut from the border down and back again, and cutting through two surfaces get [del]two[/del] [ins]four[/ins] simply connected surfaces. If we join these together twice we undo the effect of the two cross-cuts and so find the connectivity is [del]zero[/del] [ins]3[/ins], or the same as if there were no cross-connection. Several other ways will suggest themselves for showing that cross-connections do not affect the connectivity.*

[marginalia]* The rules here given differ slightly from those often given in the theory of functions, where the effect of the first puncture is insignificant. But in the theory of polyhedra it cannot be overlooked.[/marginalia]
