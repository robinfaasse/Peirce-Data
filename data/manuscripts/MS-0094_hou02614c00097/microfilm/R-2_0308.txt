[u]§153 Theorem 30[/u]. The metron can move so that all the particles in any one ray remain fixed, while all the laminae containing that ray move round it.

[u]Demonstration[/u]. By the definition of the metron, any one particle remaining fixed [ins]in a point, and[/ins] [del]and any straight[/del] the fibre in that ray remaining in it, [del]the[/del] any flat lamina contained in it can be moved into any plane containing it. Now there is a simply-continuous multitude of such planes; and therefore motion [ins]being[/ins] by a simply-continuous multitude of displacements in the simply-continuous multitude of instants of time, the lamina can continuously move through all those planes. [del][This is not saying that it can complete the round in any finite or infinite time.][/del] [ins]or any definite part of it[/ins] But [del]one[/del] [ins]the fibre[/ins] being fixed and one particle [ins]being[/ins] in it fixed, by the definition of the metron all the particles in the fibre, and therefore in the ray are fixed. Thus, with all the particles in the ray fixed, all the flat laminae containing it can move round it.

[u]Definition 101[/u]. The motion such as is described in the above theorem is called [u]rotation[/u], and the [del]fixed[/del] [ins]ray[/ins] occupied by the fixed [del]fibre is called[/del] the [u]axis of rotation[/u]. The amount of [del]motion[/del] [ins]displacement[/ins] required to restore a lamina to its original position is called a [u]circuit[/u], [ins]or turn[/ins] and is denoted by a letter c with the lower part continued under the upper part, 𝖼.
