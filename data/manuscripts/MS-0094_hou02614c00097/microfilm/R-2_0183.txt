[u]Chapter 2. The Fundamental Propositions of Graphics.[/u]

[u]§91 Scholium[/u]. Euclid defines a ray, or straight line, as "a line which lies evenly with respect to its points."

Prof. B. Peirce in the original edition of the present work defined it a line having the same direction throughout.

Legendre defined it as the line of shortest distance between two points.

The two last definitions are objectionable as introducing metrical conceptions. The last hides an important proposition.

A plane is ordinarily defined as such a surface that a ray through any two points of it lies wholly within the surface. This also hides a proposition.

We first set forth explicitly, the eight propositions involved in homaloidal surfaces and lines existing in tridimensional space, and then define the plane and ray by their relations to the familiarly assumed properties of light.

[u]Definition 136[/u]. Points, planes, and rays are called the [u]chief elements[/u], or [u]dominant homaloids[/u], of space.
