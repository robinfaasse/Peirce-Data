[u]§128 Scholium[/u]. Suppose a spectator to stand upon a boundless
plane, — in geometrical strictness. Then, according to the idea of
space adopted in graphics every ray [del]from[/del] through his eye will
cut that plane. But graphics is non-committal as to the distances
at which these rays cut. The question whether there be any parts
of the plane at an infinite distance is beyond its purview. Perhaps,
for aught graphics can say, a dog running off [ins]along[/ins] [del]in[/del] a ray would after
a number of days reappear from behind, having completed the
circuit of space. Perhaps, on the contrary, the observer standing on
the plane would see a circle lower than his eye which was at an
infinite distance so that time would not be long enough to carry light
any further. Perhaps, as is [del]general[/del] [ins]generally[/ins] assumed, all parts of the plane
below his eye are at a finite distance, but the ray on a level with
his eye would strike the "horizon" or limit between earth and sky.
In this case, the parts of [del]the plane[/del] space at an infinite distance
[del]are[/del] form the limit of planes, and it is perfectly proper [del]and[/del] [ins]and true[/ins] to
say they are [del]a plane[/del] at an infinite distance. Now this may not be
quite true. We shall discuss this somewhat under Metrics: but it is
certainly excessively close to the truth. It would be quite nonsensical
to draw a prospective picture on any other supposition.
