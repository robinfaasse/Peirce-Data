Let us go back to our pail of water with the lump of
ice adhering to the side of the pail. There is an ice-water-wood
line, which we will suppose to be a curved line, the
ice not reaching to the bottom of the pail. This line is
bounded by the two points, A and B,
where ice, water, wood, and air con-
centre. The water-air surface we
will assume to be plane, that is,
to be a portion of a plane. Of
course, in stricter truth a level
surface is not plane, for if water
were poured in up to that level, the
surface would enclose the whole earth, except such highlands
as might protrude. But as the whole thing is merely a
creation of the imagination we will imagine the air-water
surface to be plane. Now let the water be slowly drawn
off by a siphon; and what will happen to those two points,
A and B? They will move down, of course, and will soon
begin to approach one another. As the water [ins]level[/ins] is lowered,
they will approach with greater and greater speed. For
