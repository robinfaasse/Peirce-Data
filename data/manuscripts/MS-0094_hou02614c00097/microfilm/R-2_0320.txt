[u]§157. Theorem 34[/u]. Corresponding to every ray [ins]{$r$}[/ins] in space is another ray [ins]{$s$}[/ins] called its [del]absolute[/del] polar conjugate, every point of one being distant half a circuit from every point of the other, and every plane through one being at right angles to every plane through the other.

[u]Demonstration[/u]. Upon any ray take any two points [u]M[/u] and [u][N][/u]. If the ray {$MN$} is axis of rotation, there is a plane through each point of it whose lamina does not leave the plane during the rotation. Let those planes, through [u]M[/u] and [u][N][/u] be $(\mu)$ and $(\nu)$ respectively. The planes $(\nu)$ have a ray [ins]{$\mu\nu$}[/ins] in common. Every plane [ins]$(\alpha)$[/ins] through the first is perpendicular to $(\mu)$ and to $\nu$) Such a plane cuts $\{\mu\nu\}$ in a point, [ins]say [u]L[/u] = [$\mu\nu\alpha$][/ins] which by the first corollary of the last theorem is distant $\frac{1}{2}D$ from [u]M[/u] and from [u][N][/u]. If a different point [u][N'][/u] is taken on the ray {$MN$}, the intersection [u][L][/u] will not be altered, because the ray {$ML$} will remain the same, [fig]triangle with vertices L, N, M[/fig] the distance $ML$ will be the same.

If we change the plane $(\alpha)$ to $(\beta)$, we shall get another point $[\mu\nu\beta]$, always in the ray $\{\mu\nu\}$, which will still [del]be distant[/del] $\frac{1}{2}D$ from every point $[N']$ of the first.

Every plane through $\{\mu\nu\}$ is perpendicular to both $(\alpha)$ and $(\beta)$ [ins]and to every other plane through $\{\mu\nu\}$[/ins] that [del]and[/del] $(\mu)$ and $(\nu)$ are.
