In regard to Leonardo Fibonacci's
problem about the [del]7[/del] [ins]7[/ins] old women
with 7 mules 7 sacks etc., there
is a nursery riddle in English.
As I was going to St. Ives
I met a man with 7 wives,
Each wife had 7 sacks,
Each sack had 7 cats,
Each cat had 7 kits.
Kits, cats, sacks, & wives,
How many were going to St. Ives?
Now Ahmes names powers, malt,
rats, cats, etc. and sums powers of
7. But we have another Nursery
Rhyme
This is the house that Jack built,
This the malt that lay in the
house that Jack built.
This is the rat that eat the
malt that lay in the house that
Jack built.
This is the cat that [del]eat[/del] [ins]killed[/ins] the
rat that eat the malt that lay
in the house that Jack built
etc etc.

[marginalia]
Now the Hebrew version of this begins
"A kid, a kid, that my father bought with 7 shekels!"
This brings in 7 again. I believe, then, that our "House that
Jack built" refers to the Egyptian names of the numerical
powers. But why was the sum of powers of 7 more
interesting than those of any other base? Perhaps
because there were 7 planets and in this way the
number of the stars was to be found out.
