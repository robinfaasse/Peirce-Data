I suppose you know the true origin of the word. But in case you
should not, I will state it. Euclid's Elements, — which, by the way,
a person who actually wrote a book about it erroneously entitled it
"Elements of Geometry," — contains 132 definitions, 414 theorems, [del]51[/del]
51 problems, 23 or 24 genuine corollaries, 17 lemmas, 2 remarks,
5 postulates, and 9 axioms. His numerous editors added a host of
corollaries that Euclid did not think it needful to state, so obvious
were they; but these editors in their vain-glory at adding these
[del]profound[/del] precious gems, these spoils of their conquest of mathematics
and of their victories over Euclid, — who, in fact, was a strong mathe-
matician and a great logician, — got the habit of putting on the
margin opposite such an insertion [del][fig]small sketch[/fig][/del] a little wreath of victory,
a corolla, — just as in many editions of the "Quaestiones scripti Oxo-
niensis" of Duns Scotus additions are marked by [fig]asterisk symbol[/fig] in the margin.

I ever read (excepting Whately's Logick, which I devoured at the age
of 12 or 13) was Schiller's Aesthetische Briefe, where he discourses
at large, for hundreds of pages, about the Spiel-trieb. So that impressed
upon me very deeply Schiller's conception of Play. (By the way, it
was a momentary aberration of memory which made me just now
