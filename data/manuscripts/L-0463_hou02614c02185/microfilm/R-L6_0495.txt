In reference to their immediate objects, signs
are either signs of qualities, signs of existents,
or signs of general laws. It seems clear that
a qualisign cannot be a sign of a general law.

In regard to their signified interpretants signs
are either Rhemes, Dicents, or Arguments. The
definitions of these, as you know, are much disputed,
[del]and[/del] [ins]Explanations, therefore, and not mere formal definitions[/ins]
are required. The triad, Rheme, Dicent, Argument,
is a substitute for the usual triad, Term, Proposition, or
Judgment, Argument or "Schluss"; or "Simple Appre-
hension," "Judgment, and Reasoning." In the first place,
[del]the usual triad only contemplates symbols alone.[/del] [ins]I wish to distinguish these from symbols alone.[/ins]
But I wish to divide signs in general. My rheme
[del]is[/del] [ins]includes[/ins] not only [del]a[/del] [ins]terms or class-names, but any[/ins]
word [ins]or phrase[/ins] which does not assert any object or fact
which does not produce the idea of a proposition
and any diagram or picture (without a legend)
which does not produce the idea of a proposition
I particularly object to the view which erects the com-
mon noun into an essential part of speech. It is
only in the Aryan languages (and the Basque) that this
part of speech is fully developed, and it is quite unne-
cessary. My general algebra of logic has nothing corre-
sponding to a common noun. In the Shemitic languages
such nouns are represented as verbal formations
even when no such verbs exist. Therefore, in place
of class-names, in this general division, I put words
and phrases not amounting to propositions, or any
signs which are not meant to be in themselves to assert
anything. Thus, a pointing finger is a rheme; so is
a geometrical figure. Indeed every icon is a rheme.
