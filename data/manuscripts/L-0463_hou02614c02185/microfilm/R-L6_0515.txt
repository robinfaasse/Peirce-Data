My dear Lady Welby:

A hot spell having reduced me to a state of imbecillity & incapacity for any exertion, I spent yesterday in reading the Theætetus; and whether it was that utter relaxation of my entire psyche, it made an impression of novelty upon me, such that I almost doubt whether I ever read it before in the original; and since I want to jot down my impressions about it & also to thank you for your kind interest in my interests which I have been so ungrateful as to leave unthanked so long, & since I am still in so stupid a state that I don't feel equal to any more vivacious epistle, I am going to sketch what I found to-be-liveship in this dialogue. It is as lifelike as Plato's dialogues generally are. This young Harrow school-boy of remarkable brightness & real intellect is exhibited just as he is; and the first part of the dialogue is taken up with

He does not ask for an empirical conception of knowledge, because that would not accord with his notion of knowledge, the begs the whole question at the outset by saying that he wants the pure idea of perfect knowledge. He illustrates what he wants by giving a pure [u]a priori[/u] definition of clay, (this earth made up into a dough with moisture (γῆ ὑγρῷ φυραθεῖσα). So clay is a pure Idea. So then Theætetus who has been studying something equivalent to the X[sup]th[/sup] Book of Euclid (quite beyond the majority of [ins]your[/ins] Cambridge undergraduates) is able to derive from that [del]algebraic[/del] [ins]geometric[/ins] a notion of what Socrates's notion of a notion of knowledge ought to be. Along about here occurs one of those passages of Socratic irony. Truly it is remarkable that irony should produce upon us such profound veneration for thorough irony and Socrates more than any other variety, — consists in pursuing two contrary purposes at once, the impression produced by the Socratic irony is that of such thorough single-mindedness that those of us who are least apt to feel veneration (witness I) cannot resist it in the case of this man.
