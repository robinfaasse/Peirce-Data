14[/marginalia] As to "revolution" the classical Latin fully conforms to what you would make to be the sense of the word and which would be better expressed by circumvolution. But when you suggest that the French revolution should be called a "revolt", you seem not to be aware that "revolution" and "revolt" are from the very same verb, the original sense of which is [u]to roll back[/u]. Now one may roll back by rolling round or by a reversal of the rotation. One would say the latter meaning was, of the two, the better in accord with the formation. But anyway, it seems to me most desirable not to attempt what cannot be done. The evolution of meaning takes place by somebody giving a word a certain meaning, and if his phrase is sufficiently attractive to stick in the memory, others will take the word in that sense and it will become established; and after that has once happened, what facts, what historical facts justify for one instant the expectation that any rationalistic argument will ever dislodge that sense from attaching to that word? Oh, I know cases enough in which a foolish criticism has effected such a dislodgement; but it has not been by force of logic, but merely because the objection has been stated in such a form as to make a deep impression on people's memory. I would counsel postponing all expenditure of energy until there was ground for a reasonable expectation that something commensurate should come of the effort. For, mind you, what energy you put into one attempt, you render unavailable for some other beneficent action to which it might have been successfully applied.
