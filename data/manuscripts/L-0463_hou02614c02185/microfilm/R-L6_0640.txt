If you have ever had your attention called to this famous nine-point theorem, you have doubtless seen a much better & easier demonstration of it than the one I am about to give, since it is only a corollary from Pascal's hexagram. But my [del]sole[/del] purpose being to illustrate the elementary handling of the barycentric notation I prefer the one I give because it does not soar into conic sections.

[del]Instead[/del] And by the way, as algebra I believe is one of your [u]bêtes noires[/u], let me say that [del]there[/del] it wouldn't pay at all to dig through the algebraic work. I give it, because this letter may come to be read by somebody who would be better satisfied to see it; but if it is not easily grasped in a moment, it would only distract the attention. Suffice for it, for most readers, to be assured that it is here.

I now restate the theorem. [del]In a plane[/del] (this is not immediately extensible to 3 dimensions as the 10-point theorem is. The latter is easiest proved by first showing that it holds when the three rays first drawn are not in a plane) let there be two black rays $(A'B'C')$ and $(A''B''C'')$ and let them meet in $[P]$ which is not shown. On the former, mark [del]three[/del] any [ins]three [del]different[/del][/ins] points $[A']$, $[B']$, $[C']$; and on the latter any three all different $[A'']$, $[B'']$, $[C'']$. Draw the [del]three[/del] [ins]six[/ins] rays $(A'B'')$, $(B'C'')$, $(C'A'')$, $(A''B')$, $(B''C')$, $(C''A')$, and mark the three points $[(A'B'')(A''B')]$, $[(B'C'')(B''C')]$, $[(C'A'')(C''A')]$. Then these three [del]rays[/del] [ins]points[/ins] will lie on one ray. Let us express the conditions of the proposition barycentrally in terms of $[P]$, $[B']$, $[B'']$ taking $a'$, $a''$, $c'$, $c''$ so that $[A'] = \bar{a}' P + a' B'$, $[A''] = \bar{a}'' P + a'' B''$
$[C'] = \bar{c}' P + c' B'$, $[C''] = \bar{c}'' P + c'' B''$
