began to discern that he was becoming the laughing-stock of all who
had studied his subject, and then he only held his peace, but did not
explicitly acknowledge the proof. Here that proof is. It [del]relates[/del] [ins]relates[/ins] to those
systems of algebra which have a number of [u]linearly independent units[/u];
that is to say, if $i_1, i_2, i_3$ etc are a set of such no one of them
as $i_1$ can be expressed as a polynomial in the others $i_1 = c_2 i_2 + c_3 i_3 + \text{etc}$
where $c_2, c_3,$ etc are numbers. If there is one such set there are an
infinite number each of such a nature that every other quantity of
the system can be expressed in the form $c_1 i_1 + c_2 i_2 + c_3 i_3 + \text{etc}$. The
whole character of the system is expressed in its multiplication-
table. As a general rule $i_1 \times i_2$ is not equal to $i_2 \times i_1$ but the multipli-
cation is [u]associative[/u] that is to say if $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ are any quantities of the
system and if $\alpha \beta = \mu$ $\beta \gamma = \nu$ then $\alpha \nu = \mu \gamma$ or $(\alpha \beta) \gamma = \alpha (\beta \gamma)$. The
multiplication-table may be expressed in a general way by writing
$i_m i_n$ where $m$ and $n$ may be any two numbers and $i_m$ is the
$m^{\text{th}}$ of the set of independent units while $i_n$ is the $n\text{th}$ unit of the set.
$i_m \cdot i_n = [m,n,1] i_1 + [m,n,2] i_2 + [m,n,3] i_3 + \text{etc} = \sum_p [m,n,p] i_p$
