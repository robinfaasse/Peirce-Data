Their notion are that existence is a mode of motion, that one cannot cross the same brook twice, that man is the measure of things, that everything is relative. So Socrates asks the young fellow what perfect knowledge, [foreign]scientia[/foreign], consists in, — and the rule imposed upon the victim is that he must answer every question as well as he can. So he says that feel-knowledge is perception by the senses, — far too profound an idea for him to have grown in the garden of his own mind, but doubtless got it out of the book of Protagoras. In fact, he admits pretty much that, and there is no pretension about the fellow, but certainly extraordinary intelligence. But the argument that strikes him are about such as might have convinced a Harrow school-boy of forty years ago of the falsity of the philosophy of Mill or Bain. If the individual man is the measure of things, there can be no false opinions. Now not only some man but all men agree that everybody (except themselves) do entertain false opinions in great profusion & therefore it must be so & the doctrine falls to the ground. Of course, the true Socratic method of ascertaining what the conception of perfect knowledge was, — and the valid method, if the conception of the Greeks of that age be meant, — [del]was to[/del] would have been to examine the sciences which they had successfully developed & the list would have reduced itself chiefly to a single science, geometry (though of course it was a century & a half before Euclid, so that even that science was in an [ins]almost[/ins] infantile condition. It is interesting to note that this is the very method that poor little Theaetetus at once starts when the question is put to him, mentioning Geometry & Grammar & Music & Drawing & Cordwainery (does anybody know nowadays what a cordwainer is?)
