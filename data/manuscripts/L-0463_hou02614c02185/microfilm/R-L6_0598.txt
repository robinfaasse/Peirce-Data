It seems to me that one of the first [ins]useful[/ins] steps toward a science of
semeiotic (σημειωτική), or the cenoscopic science of signs, must
be the accurate definition, or logical analysis, of the concepts of
the science. I define a [u]Sign[/u] as anything which [del]is[/del] so determined
by an [u]Object[/u] and [ins]on the other hand[/ins] so determines an idea in a
person's mind, that this latter determination, which I term the
[u]Interpretant[/u] of the sign, is thereby mediately determined by that
Object. A sign, therefore, has a triadic relation to its [u]Object[/u] and
to its [u]Interpretant[/u]. But it is necessary to distinguish the
[u]Immediate Object[/u], or the Object as the Sign represents it, from
the [u]Dynamical Object[/u], or really efficient but not immediately
present Object. It is likewise [del]requisite[/del] to distinguish the
[u]Immediate Interpretant[/u], i.e. the Interpretant represented or signi-
fied in the Sign, from the [u]Dynamic Interpretant[/u] or effect actually
produced on the mind by the Sign, and both of these from the [u]Normal[/u]
[u]Interpretant[/u], or effect that would be produced on the mind by the Sign
after sufficient development of thought. On these considerations
I have a recognition of ten respects in which Signs may be divided.
I do not say that these divisions are enough. But since every one
of them turns out to be a trichotomy, it follows that in order to decide
what classes of signs result from them, I have $3^{10}$, or 59049, dif-
ficult questions to carefully consider; and therefore I will not
undertake to carry my systematical division of signs any further
but will leave that for future explorers.
