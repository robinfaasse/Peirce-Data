To gain an understanding of the real character
of Napoleon, it is requisite to remember, first of
all, that he was a Corsican. Though he said him-
self, "I am not so Corsican as people think," there
is little room to doubt that that element really
was predominant in him. What sort of a man,
then, was the Corsican of Napoleon's youth? We
can answer this from what is recorded of Paoli and
a few other individuals, from the impressions of
travellers, from the known manners and customs
of the island, and from analogy with the Greeks,
whom the Corsicans considerably resemble.
They were, in the first place, a rude, savage, and
bloodthirsty people. Their desires were of the simplest;
they never knew what it was to be undecided as to
what they wanted; and what they wanted they pur-
sued by the directest path, not knowing what a
scruple was, — except as a threat of divine ven-
geance; and naturally disposed to [del]gain[/del] [ins]seek[/ins] their ends
