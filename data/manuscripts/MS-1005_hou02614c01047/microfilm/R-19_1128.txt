[u]Of the Difference Between Analytical and Synthetical Judgements.[/u]

In all judgements, wherein the [del]proportion[/del] [ins]relation[/ins] of a subject to a predicate is thought (if I [del]here[/del] consider only affirmative[ins]s[/ins], the application to negatives is [del]easy[/del] [ins]afterwards easy[/ins]) this relation is possible in two ways. Either the predicate B belongs to the subject A as something which is contained in this conception A (in a concealed way) or B lies wholly out of the conception A, although it stands in connection with it. In the first case I call the judgment analytic, in the second synthetic. Analytical judgements (affirmatives) are, [del]accordingly[/del], those in which the connection of the predicate with the subject is thought by identity; but those in which this connection is thought without identity, shall be called synthetic judgements. We might call the first also [del]elucidation[/del] judgements of elucidation, the others judgements of enlargement, since the former by the predicate add nothing to the conception of the subject, but only separate this by dissection into its [del]divis[/del] constituent conceptions, which are already thought therein (although confusedly); the latter on the contrary add to the conception of the subject a predicate which was not thought therein at all, and which could [del]have[/del] [ins]not[/ins] been produced from it by no dissection. For instance, if I say: "All bodies are extended", this is an analytic judgement.
