"[u]The discourse was beautifully, elegantly, and forcibly delivered[/u]."
[u]This is better than[/u]
The discourse was beautifully, elegantly [ins]and[/ins] forcibly delivered.
Since the latter suggests two items [u]beautifully and elegantly[/u] and
forcibly. It is hard, however, to judge of such a ridiculous
sentence, where the "beautifully" is quite [u]de trop[/u].

"[u]And, feeling all along the garden wall,[/u]
[u]Lest he should swoon, and tumble, and be found,[/u]
[u]Crept to the gate, and opened it, and closed.[/u]"
The comma after "And" I hold to be inexcusable and
positively bad. The others can be excused as indicating the
way Tennyson meant it to be read. Otherwise, I think the
following would be better:
[u]And feeling all along the garden-wall,[/u]
[u]Lest he should swoon and tumble, and be found,[/u]
[u]Crept to the gate, and opened it and closed.[/u]
The reason of the comma after "gate" is that the "and"
might lead the reader to begin to construct the sentence
on the idea that something else to which he crept was
to be mentioned. After 'tumble' a comma is called for
because what he feared was that he should immediately
swoon and tumble and subsequently be found. Comma
after "garden-wall" because what follows is explanatory.
