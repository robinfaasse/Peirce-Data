"Exhaustive"! Some people ask whether this or that work is quite exhaustive.
The answer for all, No, it is not. Possibly an exhaustive dictionary might
be drawn up for Volapük, supposing that to be now a dead language: yet we
doubt it. Chemists and microscopists designate certain tests, reagents,
stains, etc., by their devisers' names; and of these terms Mr. Alfred I. Cohn
has now compiled a dictionary entitled 'Tests and Reagents' (John Wiley and Sons,
[date]1903[/date]). It contains some three thousand titles. Opening the volume at random,
we come upon 'Mayer', and there find twenty-three entries, but not Victor Mayer's
test for ammonia. This leads us to turn to 'Nessler's Reagent'. Two formulas
for its preparation, differing insignificantly, are given; but no word of
the special precaution needful alike in preparing and in applying this
reagent, [del]of[/del] [ins]the[/ins] neglect or slighting of which, however obvious its importance
to the accomplished chemist, is probably to be attributed the singularly
large number of formulas for it which Mr. Cohn tells us have been given.
In place of that information, better have said "Beware of ammonia in your water
and on the walls of your vessels." On the whole, our examination of the
book assures us that in spite of numerous imperfections incident to all
human works, especially dictionaries and most especially first editions, it
may be recommended as useful to those for whom it is intended, as it
certainly [del]cautions to those for whom it is not intended[/del] [ins]will prove to those for whom it is not intended[/ins]. The author's
previous work on indicators is well known and approved.
