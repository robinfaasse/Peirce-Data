may now be added that the assertion that any sort of thing
does not exist is of that nature. In point of fact, every
general description amounts to the statement that some
sort of thing does not exist. To say that all crows are
black is the same as to say that non-black crows do not exist.
Propositions are thus of two essentially different kinds;
those which assert that one thing follows from another,
being thus equivalent to hypothetical propositions, and
these deny the existence of something, and secondly, those
which deny that one thing follows from another, and these
affirm the existence of something. A compound proposition
may be partly of one kind and partly of the other. Thus,
if I say "all the mammals of Australia (except the dingo)
carry their young in pouches," I affirm that there are mammals
in Australia, and I deny that there are any placental mammals
there. The latter fact may be stated in the hypothetical
form, "If any mammal is Australian, it carries its young
in a pouch." So to say that every man is mortal implies
that being mortal follows from being man. Besides these
cases, there are others where a proposition belongs prin-
cipally and mainly to one of the two classes, and yet implies
also a proposition of the other class. Take this example:
"Every ghost (if there be such a thing) is the shade of
some man."

Some men would not be terrified by any ghost.
