For the process of putting a theory to the test of experiment
is frightfully expensive in money, time, thought, and
every way. Of course, the right theory will have to be put
to the test anyway, and therefore if one has solid reason
to think one can put one's finger on the right theory, there
will be great economy in doing so. But in general one
ought to be well on one's guard against such trusting to
preconceived ideas. Often the fact that a theory is almost
certain to be wrong is a reason for selecting it, because it
will probably speedily be disposed of. That is good sound
policy. But the best rule is to try a hypothesis which just
bisects the probabilities. Abduction is the finest part
of scientific research. It is here that genius is wanted. One
must now narrow one's view to a single surprising experience,
One must combine different surprises that seem to be allied.
A strange human birth must be taken into consideration
with animal and vegetable abnormal growths. One must
especially note cases in which the form of the surprise is
similar. One must consider right & left handed sugar in
connection with vines that twist to the right and left, and so
on. What one has is a sum total of life-energy, money,
time, and peculiar gifts. Now the question is how to lay all
that out most economically. One must remember that
the more one already knows of a given subject the more it
will cost to make a given addition to that knowledge and
