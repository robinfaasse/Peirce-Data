I then on the other hand will just trace out the history
of thought in a very few words since the Dark Ages.
To call the Dark Ages the ages of faith is to entertain a
low opinion of faith or else not to understand the
Dark Ages. In those ages, everything was unsettled; every
thing had to be incessantly fought for. People had no clear
notion that truth was not to be altered. There was a belief
they meant to fight for. If a man wanted to prove another
a murderer he thought the way to do it was to fight him.
Fight settled questions & the truth meant the settlement
of a question. The first light upon this state of things came
along with the first books of Aristotle which became known
in Western Europe. They came along very gradually. For
two centuries they were just school boys never dreaming of
doubting what their master Aristotle, — the "prince of
philosophers", — told them. But that had to be squared
with the traditions of the church. Now such an idea as
that of the fixity of Truth, when one has not got it, is by
no means an easy idea to develope. Of course about
little everyday affairs, — where is my knife? — and the
like they had the idea. But questions about God, and
virtues, and essences [ins]and angels[/ins] and all those abstract things we
utterly disconnected from ideas of common things. That is one
of the great characteristics of early thought. Celestial bodies, stars
&c, moved in circles naturally, terrestrial bodies in straight lines.
They never dreamed of putting high & low into one category. From
1210 to 1230 or thereabouts until say 1450 — or 1500
the higher minds gradually acquired independence, but
