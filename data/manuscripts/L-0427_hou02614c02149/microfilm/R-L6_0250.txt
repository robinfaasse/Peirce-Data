I go on to note that Kant did not hold this doctrine at the same time that he held that physical things (meaning the reference, of course) were immediately given, or indeed (delightful paradox!) known to exist on the testimony of our self-consciousness (ed. Hartenstein, Vol. III, p. 599).

All the while (as Descartes, Locke, Berkeley, Hume, Kant agree in holding) nothing is directly given but our immediate experience — that of which we exist. But this ex-

[del]perience is limited to past, but immediately up to a point. We recognize that what we immediately perceive is not the real past but only an image of it, & if we forget this, we find I think that what enables this image to be of the past, but projected, is, in a large part at least, a certain motor attitude. Now, in perception too, we act as if the objects perceived were permanent, had other sides, existed independently of us (i.e., of our immediate experience), & so give to them an extra-mental projection analogous to the temporal projection such as worry. In all cases, then, that is given is simply immediate experience & our motor attitudes give to it a projection beyond us, an "objective reference." Experiences become objects of consciousness solely by getting this objective reference.[/del]
