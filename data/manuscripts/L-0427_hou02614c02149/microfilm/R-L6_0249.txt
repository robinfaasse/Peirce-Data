Besides the perceptions, memories, etc., of which we become aware as objects, other states, that are immediately experienced but have no objective reference as such, pleasure & pain, desire & will. The subject, then, is simply the total datum of psychology, or, as James puts it, the "passing thought."

Now, it is extremely important to note that the subject, just because it is the vehicle of knowledge, cannot be aware (in the cognitive sense) of itself. It can only feel or experience its own existence. Hence we have, in our own immediate being, an untranscendable which thought cannot reach. Thought's own reach is only indirectly & representatively, in later reflection.

This is the foundation-stone of contemplation, as, indeed, but inimi-cal to itself—no, not itself, experience. But, in that case, we have no empirical ground for asserting its existence. It is impossible to see how it could have existed at the first moment, or could subsequently have become known.

The only way, in my opinion, is to distinguish between consciousness (knowledge) of experience, & to hold that we become conscious of (know) the object by experiencing the subject. The subject, in short, is immediate experience, the matrix of doctrines, in which the knowledge of objects is developed by evolutionary processes. James, in his article on "The Function of Cognition," has given an analysis of knowledge that harmonizes well with this view.
