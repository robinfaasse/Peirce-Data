things are as they do in their number being three. Instead of laughing at
this, let us see what vague agreement there may be. The first kind of
meaning seems to be an unanalyzed but ensemble of feeling
arising from familiarity with the symbol. The second grade of
meaning is formalistic. In the case of a proper name, it points
out where the object may be discerned. In other cases, hard dyadic
relations bring the matter to simple elements which we are sup-
posed to know in some direct manner. With most writers, this "meaning"
seems to be a definition which hits as close to the bull's-eye as possible
while still striking quite clear of it. The third kind of meaning is always prag-
matic, — what the purport clearly comes to. The ancient and [del]modern[/del]
philosophy was not ripe for pragmatism; but the greatest modern
philosophers, Spinoza, Berkeley, Kant, have always tended toward
pragmatic definitions. To get the concept, the symbol of intellectual
value, you must pass the sponge over all coloring to begin with. Then
you must cast aside the indicative elements. To say that the
two verbs monstrare and indicare are, if preserved, in any
steady way, the distinction of two kinds of showing would be untenable.
But there are the two roots [greek]δείκνυμι[/greek] and monstrare; and there
are two radically different kinds of showing. One exhibits; the other
sets you on the track of experiences. This last experiential busi-
