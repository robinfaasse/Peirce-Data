Now then, that done. How are we to express the intellectual meaning
of the concept, if somebody doubts whether it really has any meaning?
The answer which follows plainly from my logical system is that you
must describe the habit [ins]of behaviour[/ins] that the symbol might serve to express. It
must be something concrete, in a certain sense. It must at the same
time be general. A desire, or a habit, a rule of behaviour, alone
meets the requirements.

For example, you tell me (p. 294) that the immediate [del][[/del] let me say
direct [del]][/del] perception of objects existing independently of the mind
at a distance from the body is inconsistent with the fact that they
call forth perceptions only by acting on the body. But what do you
mean by "existing"? If you do not accurately define it, you
cannot be sure you mean anything definite; and I, for one,
should most seriously doubt its corresponding to any concept at all.
What the pragmatist means by "existing" is perfectly clear. To exist is to
react against other existents. Effort and resistance to effort are
supposed to be conceptually (not as to the feeling that perhaps ac-
companies them) closely similar to the behaviour of physical
things that are [ins](in comparison to one another)[/ins] accelerated to or from one another. "The perception
of objects existing independently of the mind" means then that
the percept cannot be dismissed like a reverie by a direct effort,
that it can only be got rid of [del]by[/del] indirectly by interposing some
veil or removing the object. Also that other people see nearly
the same thing, that a photographic camera will help to show
that it is "independent of the mind" etc. That I maintain to be
the only [ins]intellectual[/ins] meaning that can be attached to the phrase. Now
