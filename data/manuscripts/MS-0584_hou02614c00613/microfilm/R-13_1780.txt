For one thing we perceive that Erigena attaches a vast importance
to words. In consequence of this he seems to suppose that non-exis-
tences are as real as existences. He begins his work de divisione
[del]naturae[/del] He begins by dividing all things into those which are and
these which are not. In another place he declares that no philoso-
pher rightly denies that possibles and impossibles are to be reckoned
among the number of things. And such expressions are [ins]in fact[/ins] constant-
ly met with in his works. [del]Thus he says of God[/del] He does not seem
to see that as the ancient philosopher said "Being only is
and nothing is altogether not." Thus he says that [ins]the name[/ins] Nothing signi-
fies the ineffable, incomprehensible and inaccessible brightness
of the Divine nature which is unknown to every [ins]understanding[/ins] [del]intellect of[/del]
man or [del]of[/del] angel, which "dum per se ipsam cogitatur" neither
[del]is[/del] nor was nor will be. And he describes creation as the
production out of the negations of things which are and which
are not, the affirmations of all things which are and which
are not. Again he says "Darkness is not nothing but some-
thing; otherwise the Scripture would not say "and God called
the light day, and the darkness he called night." Thus you
perceive he has the idea that the [del]immediate[/del] [ins]immaterial,[/ins] object of a name
is something.
