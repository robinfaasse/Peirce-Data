<u>Experiment 2</u>. Put a piece of iron in dilute sulphuric acid. Although the iron is very heavy it will be transfused through the sulphuric acid and color the whole green. If you dip a piece of zinc into the surface of the solution, the iron will be deposited on the zinc, so that a heavy piece of iron may be lifted from the bottom to the top of a liquid, in this way.

Chemistry is the science which deals with these phenomena. Chemistry, therefore, appears to be a branch of physics.

Now while Natural Philosophy, occupies itself with forces, Natural History, on the other hand, is the study of natural things. And chemistry, since it studies substances, all compounded of the same elements as are those found in nature, is a branch of natural history. Thus, like almost all sciences, it belongs to both divisions of natural science, but it divides its attention more equally, than perhaps any between the two.

But what, after all, is the difference between matter and force, since we only know matter by its affecting our senses, that is by forces?

The answer is that force is that which acts; matter, that which is acted on. To obtain a criterion, then, by which we distinguish between matter and force, we must find some force which acts upon everything which is acted on, at all. Such a force is gravity; and everything which has weight, we call matter; everything else, force.

"The action of force upon matter is motion."* "Force itself, we only know of or believe in, as will." All the forces of nature, then, are motions. So that we shall not be surprised to hear that they are altered, [del]that[/del] vary in intensity, and counteract each other in various circumstances. But they are never destroyed; by the law of cause and effect, every motion will produce its effect which will itself be a motion and will produce its effect, and so on forever. These are the two general laws of the natural forces.

As for matter: 1[ins]st[/ins] since every effect is a motion, matter can neither be created nor destroyed, 2[ins]nd[/ins]; its properties, or the forces which act in, it, may change. These are the two general laws of matter. It will be seen that they are the same as those of force.

Having now explained what matter is, the next question is "What constitutes a difference in kind of matter?" The answer is very simple: a greater or less susceptibility to the action of the various forces.
