But "what is the meaning of this thing?" is not the question [del]any[/del] science puts to nature. "What makes the rain-bow? What makes it [ins]go or[/ins] stand up?" are the kind of inquiries belonging to Mechanics, Optics, Acoustics, and all the branches of Physics. They are questions of force. Force is that in every change which causes that the change unless stopped shall keep itself up & expend itself on its own continuance. [del]For[/del] [ins]For it[/ins] must be wherever matter is known, since matter is known by the affections of our senses which must be effects of force. The Science of Kinds of matter must then treat questions of force — it is therefore a branch of Physics. For instance, in the case of salts it is obvious that there must be a certain force which makes the spirit of sea salt & mineral alkali transform themselves to salt. Now if 35 parts of spirit of sea salt neutralize 40 of Mineral Alkali & 50 of Vegetable Alkali and if it requires 50 parts of Oil of Vitriol to neutralize 40 of the former, it follows that it contains just force enough to neutralize 50 of the latter. And so we find it & this same amount of force is in every salt.

Thus it appears that the Science of the [u]Kinds of Matter[/u] through treating of [u]matter[/u] belongs to Natural Philosophy; through treating of [u]Kinds[/u] belongs to Natural History, and thus unlike the other sciences treats alike of [u]form[/u] and [u]force[/u], — of the [u]worth[/u] & the [u]expense[/u] of the spiritual manifestation of nature. Indeed, as that very expression shows, the form and the force are but the same thing from different points of view. (One is the Spirit seen by its [u]Influential[/u] relations the other by its [u]Causal[/u] relations.) The peculiarity of Chemistry is that it embraces both views in one.
