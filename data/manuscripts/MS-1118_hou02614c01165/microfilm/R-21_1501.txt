Nevertheless, this limitation of genius to art has met with universal disapproval among those later authors who have written upon the subject. Jürgen Bona Meyer[ins]*[/ins] expressly declares that there is such a thing as scientific genius; and so do other authors who have not reconciled themselves to this limitation of the common modes of language, and since Kant and Schopenhauer people continue to speak of savans of genius, as they did before.

The question whether any definite psychological conception can be attached to the word genius can therefore only be decided by making a psychological analysis of the poets, composers, painters, virtuosi, actors, men of learning, statesmen [del][ins]Has any statesman ever been considered a genius, except in spite of his statesmanship?[/ins][/del] and generals who have been recognized as geniuses, and then deciding by comparison whether there be any common characteristic of them all, any inward affinity which justifies us in applying one term to all these minds.

Famous poets who have known how to observe [del]themselves[/del] conditions have often said that their works were made as in a dream unconsciously to themselves, that instead of being voluntarily created they have, as it were, flown to them. Goethe says: "There is a sense in which it is true that poets and all real artists must be born not made. Namely, there must be an inward productive power, to bring the images which linger in the organs, the imagination, the memory freely, without purpose or will to life; they must unfold themselves, grow, extend, [del]accumulate[/del] in order to become instead of fugitive diagrams really present pictures."
