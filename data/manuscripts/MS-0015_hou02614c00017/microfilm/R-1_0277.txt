[u]2[ins]nd[/ins][/u], What is [u]quantity[/u]? That is to say, granted that the system of whole numbers is a system of quantity, that the system of rational numbers is another, the system of real numbers is another, the system of imaginaries is another, and the system of quaternions is another, it is required to form a distinct and unitary conception applicable to those systems, so limited as to be of the greatest possible utility in the [del]account[/del] [ins]theory[/ins] of cognition, bringing into view those features of the above systems which give them their importance for science, and connecting itself directly with an explanation of that importance. In connection with this question, I also consider [del]what the chief systems of quantity are.[/del]

[u]3[ins]d[/ins][/u], What are multitude and collectional quantity? This [del]question[/del] [ins]problem[/ins] is found to divide itself into several parts, of which the chief are, What is the [ins]natural[/ins] place of predications concerning the multitudes of collections in the logical classification of propositions? Is it true that of any two possible multitudes not equal one is less than the other? That is, [del]matter[/del] [ins]is[/ins] [del]one[/del] of two collections always capable of being in one-to-one correspondence with the other? What are all the grades of multitudes, and how are they distinguished? Is there any maximum possible multitude? Are the grades of collectional quantity the same as those of multitude?

[u]4[ins]th[/ins][/u], What is [u]continuity[/u]? That is to say, if we develope the natural common-sense idea of time and space into a logically exact conception, in what does the unbroken flow of time and the capacity of space for admitting unbroken motion consist, as so conceived? That answered, I very briefly consider what the evidence is that this natural common-sense idea of time and space is true to nature. I also inquire how far it can concern the mathematician.
