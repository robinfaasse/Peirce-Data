In order to suggest the place which mathematics would seem to take in the system of the sciences, according to the above analysis, I venture to propose the following scheme of classification of all the sciences, modified from that [del]of[/del] [ins]of[/ins] Auguste Comte, and like his proceeding from the more abstract to the more concrete. Each science (except mathematics) rests upon fundamental principles drawn from the truths discovered by the science immediately preceding it in the list, while borrowing data and suggestions from the discoveries of those which follow it.

1. [u]Mathematics[/u], which observes only the creations of the mathematician himself. It borrows suggestions from all other sciences, [ins]from philosophy[/ins] [del]from[/del] Mathematical Logic, [ins]from psychics[/ins] Mathematical Economics, from physics Mathematical Optics, [del]etc.[/del] Metrics, etc.

2. [u]Philosophy[/u], which makes no special observations, but uses facts [del]commonly[/del] [ins]in order to[/ins] [del]known[/del]. To be exact, it must rest on mathematical principles. It divides into [u]Logic[/u], which studies the world of thought, and [u]Metaphysics[/u], which studies the world of being; and the latter must rest upon the principles of the former.

3. [u]The science of time[/u] and [u]the science of space[/u] are the most abstract of the special sciences. They must be based upon metaphysical principles, [ins]and geometry largely upon the science of time,[/ins] though the one draws [del]its[/del] data from psychology, and the other from astronomy and chemistry.

The science of time is psychical and that of space physical; and from this point on Psychics and Physics are widely separated, and influence one another little.
