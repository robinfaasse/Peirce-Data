Three countries only, so far as we know, approached the scientific stage of civilization in high antiquity, — Babylonia, Egypt, and northern China. All three were flooded yearly by the rivers that traversed them, while at other seasons they required irrigation. To carry on such irrigation a strong government is needed, and the natural course of events developes it. Besides, the man who had stored grain against a famine would of length find himself in a position to enslave the others. A king ruling over a population which during a considerable part of the year was reduced to idleness, so far as agriculture was concerned, would be obliged to feed his people during that season; and by way of keeping up order and discipline he would have to find some work for them to do. Under such circumstances great operations of engineering would be [del]inevitable[/del] [ins]predestinate[/ins]. Now, engineering affords a training in exactitude and definiteness of conception, and is, in so far, a propedeutic [ins]as[/ins] to science.

We see in our day a certain proportion of superior minds led from engineering to eminence in different branches of pure science, as mathematics, physics, astronomy, physiology, anatomy, etc.; and of those who continue to be engineers many acquire distinctly scientific bents. Along with these there are others in whose souls we remark an adoration of force joined to a contempt of all science not plainly leading to practical results. The man who appreciably advances science is of a different temper.
