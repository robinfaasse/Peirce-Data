4. [u]The form and kind of the apparatus[/u]. Mr. Chanute very sensibly advocates a curved aeroplane; but in the [u]total lack of a dynamical theory[/u], he overlooks the importance of fixing to the lower side of the aeroplane (or rather aeroplanes) a heavy mass.

5. [u]The extent of the sustaining surfaces[/u]. Mathematics forbids very large wings, and therefore there must be a great number of them, which can be tilted independently.

7. [u]The maintenance of the equilibrium[/u]. Mr. Chanute repeatedly remarks, as if with a sigh, that birds preserve their equilibrium by instinct; and the notion seems to be that our being unable to endow a machine with instinct puts us at a great disadvantage. Permit us to say, however, that it is not by instinct that birds preserve their equilibrium. They preserve their equilibrium just as we do ours in walking, by automatic coordination. Nor is there any difficulty in principle about "endowing" a machine with an automatic adjustment? We cannot think a matter like that will be allowed to block the progress of civilization [del]long[/del], when the procession once reaches it.
