Mr. Chanute says: "It seems to the writer quite certain
that flying machines can never carry even light and
valuable freights at anything like the present rates of water
or land transportation." [u]Quite certain[/u] and [u]never[/u] are
strong expressions from a writer whose special function
is to weigh his words. To us it seems that a man of
science is out of his depth when he sets up an
[u]Unverifiable Hypothesis[/u], whether as quite certain or
even [del]only[/del] [ins]as[/ins] probable: now a prediction that something will
never happen is ipso facto unverifiable. We, in turn, will
hazard a prediction; it is that within three years Mr.
Chanute's prediction will cease to seem quite certain even
to that eminent engineer, himself; but be it understood
that we consider the whole question of flight as quite
in the air, for the present.

Let us add one word about the dress of this book.
It is cheap and plain, but legible and agreeable. The press-work
is not of modern perfection, and consequently the print has not the
anaemic look of other recent books. The ink is positively black, and
the letters are not composed of spider-lines. They are a sort of Elzevir type
that puts on no [del]mode[/del] [ins]fashionable[/ins] "frills." Nor is the page so leaded that the
wrist tires with holding up all the blank paper between the lines.
The paper itself is cheap, a little speckly, and of a true écru appear-
ance. There is a refreshing modesty and good taste about the
whole effect. In short, it is pleasing.
