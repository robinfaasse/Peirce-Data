There are several examples of them in the Encyclopaedia Britannica (9th Ed.), and the first chapter of his treatise on Elliptic Functions is a masterpiece in this respect. His lectures, too, were [ins]as[/ins] crystal clear as anything could be, — a rare gift among strong mathematicians. He was appointed Sadlerian Professor in the University of Cambridge in 1863, and died in the harness.

Cayley confined himself strictly to mathematics in his writings, but he was assuredly a Platonist of the most pronounced type. For him geometrical figures, and even the substance of pure algebraical expressions were far more truly real than anything in everyday life, whether of wood, of gold, or of flesh and blood. Perhaps the greatest of his discoveries was that there is in space a certain individual Something, circular in shape, which though it lies in a plane not only at an infinite distance but also in that unseen, inward region of space of which our universe is but the rind, is yet in intimate relation with everything we see, and cuts at two points even the smallest circle which can be drawn around any point.
