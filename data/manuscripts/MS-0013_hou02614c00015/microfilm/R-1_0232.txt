[u]Art. 3[/u]. Logic discriminates between true and false propositions. That is
the final end of logic; and this is the reason why a philosophical
[del]development of[/del] logic must begin with the proposition, not the
term. From a mathematical point of view, we may say that being true
and being false are the two values one of which every proposition has.
In probable logic there is a continuous line of values from certain truth
to certain falsity. In the logic of continua, a proposition may be true, or
the truth may lie at the limit of what the proposition admits, or the
proposition may be quite away from the truth. But in the syllogistic
logic with which we are alone concerned at the outset, the system of
logical values has but two grades and is thus the most rudimentary of
all systems of quantity.

A term may conform to the reality or not; that is, it may signify
[del]some[/del] a kind [del]of[/del] which there are examples in the universe of discourse
or it may not. It is therefore not accurate to define a proposition as that
which is either true or false. [del]But[/del] A proposition should be defined as [del]that[/del]
[ins]the truth[/ins]
which professes to be true, or assigns a logical value to itself. [del]Whether[/del]
[del]is defined as that logical value which a proposition assigns to itself.[/del] Whether
or not there really is such value, [del]or another[/del] [ins]whether there is any truth is[/ins] a question, not of definitions,
but of fact.

The first postulate of the mathematics of logic is that every proposition
[del]really[/del] has a logical value; namely, there are propositions, called [u]facts[/u], which
really have the value that every proposition assigns to itself. Calling [ins]any[/ins] [del]the[/del]
fact, [u]x[/u], [u]x[/u] has the value it says it has, that is [u]x[/u] is [u]x[/u]. This is the princi-
ple of identity.
