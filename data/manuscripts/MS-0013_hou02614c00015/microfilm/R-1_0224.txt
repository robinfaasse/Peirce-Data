[u]Art. 3[/u]. Any world, or product of experience, may be studied in three ways, according as the mind gives prominence to its firstness, to its secondness, or to its thirdness.

It may, in the first place, be scrutinized [del]with[/del] [ins]in[/ins] respect to its qualities, regardless of anything more than [del]what[/del] [ins]how[/ins] each object appears. That is the way in which the Artist, who relatively to [del]other[/del] non-artists is always an impressionist, looks upon an experiential presentment. His conversation shows how he values each object for itself, how he delights in the exceptional and in the original, and how his distrusts, and even dislikes, mental arrangements in sets that are not to be seen in nature.

The world may, in the second place, be examined with reference to the actions and reactions of different objects upon one another. This is the way the Practical Man studies the world. How that man's mind runs upon pairs will be manifest as soon as he talks. His divisions are almost all dichotomies: every transaction is either honest or dishonest; those he deals with are either friends or enemies; doctrines are either commendable or reprehensible.

Finally the world may be studied in order to discover how things are brought about, what the laws of the phenomena are, what idea governs the cosmos. This is the way in which the Philosopher, or man of intellect, is interested in the world. He loves to group objects in threes, and the nature of his pursuit leads him often to do so without remarking it.
