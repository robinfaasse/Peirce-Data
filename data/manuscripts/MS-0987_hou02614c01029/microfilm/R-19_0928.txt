[date]1906[/date] [u]Note to 944 B Laws[/u]

I do not think Jowett writes as much better than Plato as he thinks he does. The English here seems to make sense owing to its being such a "free translation." But a literal translation with his understanding of the words would seem to be nonsensical.

First it is said a man may lose his arms κατὰ θάλατταν which in the original meaning of the words, to which Plato usually inclines in general, means "down to the bottom of the sea". But he connects it with the next clause which, literally is, "or by lying in places where a great flood of water had unexpectedly lain in wait for them." Plato has in mind a phenomenon that occurs in Greece, where a cloud passes over a mountain and as fast as it passes over is bodily and instantaneously converted into water, and makes a flood that washes everything before it. Nothing like that can happen at sea. Nor is there any grammatical way, as one who looks closely will see, of connecting the "at sea" (J's translation) with the cloud burst or flood. There is no "stormy" in the original. On the contrary, were it such a place the thing could not happen quite ἀξαίφνης to a prudent man, this word meaning "suddenly and unexpectedly." "By way of extenuation" is utterly wrong; ἐπᾴδειν refers to talk by way of consolation or soothing. To "justify a misfortune" is bad English; it is "to excuse an easily misrepresented mishap." In the next sentence "must" should be "ought to" or "it is expedient." "To divide from" is not English. He should have put "sever" τέμνειν ἀπό usually means and here means to cut off from. By his "divide from" he probably means to distinguish between. I wont say that τέμνειν ἀπό might not mean that; but it cannot do so here,
