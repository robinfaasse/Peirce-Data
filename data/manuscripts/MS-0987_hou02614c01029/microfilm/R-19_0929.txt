that means [u]the contrary[/u]. But the two cases are not contraries; nor is it

the reproachful epithets the implication of guilt, and so reduce them
to exclamations at the undesirable quality of the event. So ἐναντίου
cannot here mean contrary, but is to be taken in its original sense of
an experience or occurrence, what strikes the [del]senses[/del] senses. So I
should translate the whole sentence awkwardly but literally
τέμειν δή such an act of cutting χρέων is called for κατὰ δύναμιν
as far as we can possibly can, to the bottom of our power τὸ μεῖζον καὶ
τὸ δυσχερέστερον κακόν a severing of the greater and more mortifying

take the most favorable view one can. Still worse is the next sentence
He makes it, "And a distinction may be drawn in the use of terms of
reproach." But this "and" is nonsense; for this only repeats what has
just been said according to him. First τινὰ can only be translated
by "a" when the article is emphatic; [ins]but[/ins] it comes nearer to "some". But substitute
"some" and how ridiculous this sentence becomes, when according to Jowett
the distinction has just been precisely defined! But τομὴν must mean
what τέμειν means; therefore not 'some distinction' but 'some sub-
traction', some pruning down. οὖν means what it commonly means, "at any
rate"; and σχεδόν I suppose means mostly. Therefore the whole awkwardly
but literally is "At any rate in reproachful language the application
of these terms is mostly susceptible of some subtraction". That is,
most reproaches are much more laden with faultfinding than they
need be. This makes consecutive sense, which his translation
does not.

This is a pretty fair sample of Jowett's whole work.
Throughout his loose paraphrase allows him to read into
Plato what a strict rendering would show that he could
not possibly have meant.
