§ 2. A valental [del]rule[/del] [ins]mode[/ins] of graph formation is one [del]in[/del] [ins]according to[/ins] which each spot has, by its intrinsic quality, a determinate number of places at each [del]of which[/del] bonds can be joined to it. These may be called the [u]hands[/u] of the spot. In general, the different hands of a spot will be distinguishable; so that, for example, Figs. 1 and 2 will have different diagrammatic significations. Yet it is perfectly possible that this distinction should not exist in the particular matter which the graphs represent.

[fig]Two diagrams labeled Fig. 1 and Fig. 2 showing spots with hands[/fig]
Fig. 1. Fig. 2.

We may either suppose that a graph can be composed of two graphs [u]plus[/u] the bonds which in the whole unite the two parts, or that the whole is composed of the two graphs [u]minus[/u] the connecting bonds which belonged to each part. One or other of these views must be adopted, unless we suppose that some hands hold the ends of bonds of which the other ends are loose while other hands are empty, — a distinction which can hardly mean anything, and will be excluded. In the former case, a graph consists of a cluster of spots together with bonds of which both ends are held by hands of these spots. The [u]hands of the graph[/u] will be those hands of the cluster of spots which are empty. A whole graph will consist of graphs together with some bonds connecting some hands of those graphs; and two hands of the same partial graph may in the whole graph hold the two ends of a bond. There is nothing to forbid two hands of the same spot holding two ends of a bond. A partial graph may contain all the spots of the whole graph, nothing being added but bonds.

On the other view, none of the hands of the cluster of spots in a graph are empty
