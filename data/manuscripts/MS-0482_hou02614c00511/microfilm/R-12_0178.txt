The fact must not be overlooked that the node [ins]here[/ins] joining the three bonds is [del]for[/del] in the strictest sense a relative meaning "— and — and — are not [ins]and the same[/ins] [del]one[/del] individual." This may be called the triad of diversity. So Fig. 26 means "[del]Anything[/del] [ins]Somebody[/ins] is either virtuous or is identical with an ugly man and with a wise man."

No fourth relative of non-unity is required; for Fig. 27, for example, expresses that nobody is at once ugly, [del]extravagant[/del] [ins]virtuous, wise[/ins] and extravagant. Nor can any of the three relatives of non-unity be suitably expressed by means of the other two. It is true that Fig. 28 very nearly expresses that nobody is wise making an expression of the monad of nullity in terms of three triads of diversity. But what it precisely expresses is not that nobody is wise, but that nobody is wise if anybody else exists. Fig. 29 precisely expresses [del]"— is not —"[/del] but then it only does so as an encircled graph which will not answer the purpose.

Many other important relatives are formed by combinations of the three fundamental arithmetical relatives; but the one which it is the most imperative to notice is that shown in [del]of[/del] Fig. 30, which may be called the dyad of nullity. Inserted between two monads, as in Fig. 31, it produces the assertion that either no man is wise or no man is virtuous. Connecting to monads by means of triads of diversity, as in Fig. 32, it means that either the one or the other is true.
