[u]On Logical Graphs.[/u]

§1. A [u]graph[/u], in its most general sense, would be a diagram composed, first, of [u]spots[/u], or parts treated as points, though they have distinguishable sides and are [del]distinguished[/del] [ins]differentiated[/ins] by [del]colors or other simple distinguishing qualities[/del] [ins]simple visible distinguishing qualities called their colors[/ins]; second, of [u]bonds[/u], or parts treated as lines, though they too may have sides and colors; third, of [u]faces[/u], or parts treated as surfaces; and so on to higher dimensions. A graph of the lowest dimensionality has no faces nor higher parts. A [u]simple-bonded graph[/u] is a graph of lowest dimensionality in which every bond is a simple line with two ends, one of which, at least, is attached to a spot, while the other may either be so attached or may be a [u]loose end[/u].

The [u]Listing numbers[/u] of a graph may be defined as follows. Let a particle have space without the colored parts of the graph to expand in M-1 dimensions, so as to form an unlimited (but not generally infinite) M-dimensional continuum (like a ring or a closed surface), which, once formed, can by no motion within those colored parts of the graph without rupture collapse. Then, the number of such continua each of which occupies some point into which none of the others can be gradually moved within the graph without rupture is the M[sup]th[/sup] Listing number. The first Listing number, which may be called the [u]chorisis[/u], is the number of separate pieces. The second is the [u]cyclosis[/u], or number of independent rings.
