<u>Chapter I</u>

<u>Fundamental Notions.</u>

Logic must begin with analyzing the meanings of certain words which we shall take up in due order.

The term <u>ens</u> [ins]([del]plur[/del] [ins]plur:[/ins] entia)[/ins] was employed by the schoolmen to denote whatever can be mentioned, whether it exist or not. The corresponding abstract noun is <u>entitas</u> in Latin, <u>entity</u> in English. But in this case as in many others in our language the abstract form is frequently used with a concrete signification. The schoolmen all say that <u>entia</u> are [del]either[/del] <u>entia realia</u> or <u>entia rationis</u>, but they frequently use <u>ens</u> for short when they mean <u>ens realis</u>. This use is almost universal with us, as when we sneer at those writers for holding abstractions to be "entities." I propose, however, to restore the word to its original signification, of whatever can be [ins]among things,[/ins] named or mentioned [ins]or to which we can direct our thoughts[/ins].

We apply the word <u>is</u> to entities in general, as when we say that, a griffin <u>is</u> a fabulous animal, a foursided triangle is an absurdity, elevation <u>is</u> the distance from the ground, nothing <u>is</u> that which does not exist.

<u>Ens</u> is so general a term that it includes its own negative. For non-ens since it is named is also an ens.
