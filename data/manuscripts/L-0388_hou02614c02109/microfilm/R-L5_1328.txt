Upon small streams, storage reservoirs may be utilized, if the topograph-
ical conditions are favorable, and a portion of the storm flow retained
and used in the dry season, thus raising the regulated volume of flow
above the minimum. But where very large powers are required, streams
of large flow and with falls of considerable height are essential, and
upon such streams, the construction and maintainance of storage reservoirs
of sufficient capacity to be of value, will be expensive undertakings.
An instance in this direction is the case of the Genessee River, at and
above the City of Rochester. The river has three falls within the
boundaries of the City, aggregating 263 feet in height. The total annual
flow of the river at Rochester is 37,000,000,000 cubic feet, and if this
flow were distributed evenly throughout the year, more than 23,000 horse
powers could be continuously utilized. But, in fact, the flow has great
variation. It is as high at times as 3,000 cubic feet per second and
at other times as low as 60 cubic feet per second. The number of turbines
actually in use requires more than this lower volume and consequently
there are periods when there is not enough water to run them all. Then
the mills must be run by supplementary steam power, or a considerable
part of their machinery must lie idle. Examinations were made some years
since under the direction of the writer, and it was found practicable
to construct a great storage reservoir on the river about 30 miles above
the city, but the dam necessary to provide storage for only a portion
of the annual flow would be over 100 feet high, and would cost nearly
$2,000,000.00.
