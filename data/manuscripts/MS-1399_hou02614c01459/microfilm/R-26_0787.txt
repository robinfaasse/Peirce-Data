[u]Great Astronomers[/u]. By Sir Robert Ball,

Macmillan: [date]1895[/date].

Sir Robert Ball is a brilliant mathematician and a respectable astronomer. He begins this book, of course, with an account of the astronomer, astrologer, and geographer Ptolemy. Now, [del]to[/del] many things of human interest [ins]about Ptolemy[/ins] can be gleaned [del]book[/del] from his writings. Whether or not Sir Robert Ball is aware of this, his book affords us no means of knowing. But he has evidently [del]glanced[/del] looked over the Almagest, and therefore he cannot but be aware that Ptolemy was as [del]mathematician[/del] [ins]a pure geometer[/ins] upon the borders of greatness, and as an applier of geometry to physical problems must be ranked among the men who are more than eminent, — who are really unexpectedly great. Yet there is not one word to show this in Sir Robert Ball's sketch. Why? Because he is not writing to please himself, — which is a phrase for writing what he knows to be the truth of God and of Wisdom, — but is writing to making his book acceptable to a very large number of readers.
