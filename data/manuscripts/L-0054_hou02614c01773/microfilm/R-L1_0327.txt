Your definition, given in the same paragraph, of
"breadth" coincides perfectly with the usual meaning
of "extension"; and if that of "depth" varies from
"intension", it is only because the latter is very
vaguely used by most writers on Logic, — even
by Hamilton.

I prefer to consider the position of
a term as subject or predicate to be a mere
accident of language, and therefore incapable of
affecting its significance in thought. This seems
to me demonstrable, at least in the case of
every judgment which admits of simple conversion.
Yet you appear to make the significance of a
term entirely dependant on its fulfilling the [del]re[/del]
function either of a subject or predicate; and

this view is fundamental to the doctrine both
of your 2d and your 3d paper. J. S. Mill
agrees with you, in so far as he regards a
predicate as always connotative in meaning.
But it seems plain to me that, at least in the
subsumption of every syllogism, predication is
[ins]merely[/ins] [del]the[/del] inclusion of a subject in a class, [del]the[/del] [ins]it[/ins]
of the predicate not being thought of except as
justifying this classification. I assert that S is M,
only in order to put S into that class of which
I have just said that all M is P.

You must take these crude and
ill-jointed thoughts, not as criticisms or fault-
finding, but only as expressions of the interest
and attention with which I have read your
