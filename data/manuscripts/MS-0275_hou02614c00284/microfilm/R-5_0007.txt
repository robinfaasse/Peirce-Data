I. In selecting a suitable Notation for the problem involved, it is necessary first of all to ascertain whether the entire aggregate of units constitutes an enumerable mass, or not.

Assuming, as we have always hitherto done, that the total number of immediate neighbors of any given unit [del]are[/del] [ins]is[/ins] finite, it will be found that the entire aggregate of all the units is always an enumerable mass. For either (a) the total number of units in the manifold is finite, or (b) the total number of those units is infinite.

In the case of alternative (a) it is obvious that the entire aggregate of the units is an enumerable mass. In the case of alternative (b) it is not so obvious, but may be proved by the following consideration. Taking any arbitrarily selected unit as a starting point, we may affix the numeral "1" to this. By hypothesis this unit has a finite number of immediate neighbors, say n. The numerals 2, 3, 4, etc. to n + 1, may be attached to these in any arbitrary order. Units which can be reached from the initial unit by traversing not more than one intermediate unit, may be called "neighbors at one remove". By a corresponding nomenclature, which will not need further explanation, we may speak of "neighbors at two removes", "neighbors at three removes", and so on. Let us suppose that our initial unit has n neighbors at one remove, then we can attach to these neighbors at one remove the numerals n + 2, n + 3, n + 4, etc., to [del]n + 1[/del] [ins]n + n + 1[/ins]. Similarly we can attach numerals, given in any arbitrarily selected order to the entirety of neighbors at two removes which the original unit possesses. And inasmuch as every unit of a manifold must be a neighbor at some finite number of removes from the initial unit, the entire mass is an enumerable mass.
