It is easy enough to find superficial explanations of the fact that
[del]while[/del] the [ins]most[/ins] enlightened logicians of the twelfth century are nomina-
lists, everybody in the thirteenth century has definitively come to believe
in realism. We may say that in the [del]12th[/del] century learning was not or-
ganized, and when it came to be organized in close connection with the
chapter of the cathedral, realism was seen to be more consonant with
theology. Abelard it must be remembered was, in his palmy days, not
a priest, at all. But of course every scholastic Doctor was a priest.
Or again we may say that the Metaphysics and de Anima of Aristotle
had penetrated men's minds with the idea of substantial forms. Now
a form is [u]natus aptus praedicari de pluribus[/u]. But such explanations,
however true they may be, are not satisfactory. The same logicality
which resulted in this era of Scholasticism determined it to
be realistic.

The age had passed by an inevitable "logic of events" from a
conception of truth as each man's castle to a conception of
truth as the property of the entire congregation of the faithful
in its organized church. It was a public thing and of its essence
general. Now when truth is regarded as general, reality must
be looked upon in the same light. Nominalism did not come
until Ockham quarrelled with the Pope toward the middle of the
fourteenth century, about the time when gothic architecture
was losing its catholic character and becoming flamboyant
in France, perpendicular in England.
