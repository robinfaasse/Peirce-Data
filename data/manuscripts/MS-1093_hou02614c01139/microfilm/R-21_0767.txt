We thus see that when an investigation is commenced, after the initial expenses are once paid, at little cost we improve our knowledge; and that improvement is especially valuable; but, as the investigation goes on, additions to our knowledge cost more and more and at the same time are of less and less [del]worth[/del]. Thus, when Chemistry sprang into being, Dr. Wollaston with a few test tubes and phials [del]all contained[/del] on a tea-tray, was able to make new discoveries of the greatest moment. Now, a thousand chemists, with the most elaborate appliances, are not able to reach results which are comparable, in interest, with those early ones. All the sciences exhibit the same phenomenon; and so does the course of life. At first, we learn very easily, and the interest of experience is very great; but [del]experience[/del] [ins]it[/ins] becomes harder and harder and [del]less and less worth while[/del], until we are glad to have done [del]with it[/del] with life.
