for that only amounts to saying that his method of settling belief is not ours. He does not
propose to himself to be rational, and, indeed, will often talk with scorn of man's weak and
illusive reason. So let him think as he pleases.

[u]§5.[/u] But this method of fixing belief, which may be called the method of tenacity, will be unable
to hold its ground in practice. The social impulse is against it. The man who adopts it will
find that other men think differently from him, and it will be apt to occur to him, in some
saner moment, that their opinions are quite as good as his own, and this will shake his confi-
dence in his belief. This conception, that another man's thought or sentiment may be equiva-
lent to one's own, is a distinctly new step, and a highly important one. It arises from an
impulse too strong in man to be suppressed, without danger of destroying the human species.
