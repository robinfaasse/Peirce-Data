Every answer to a question that has any meaning is
a decision as to how we would act under imagined
[del]conditions[/del] circumstances, or how the world would be
expected to reach upon our senses. Thus, suppose I am
told that if two straight lines in one plane are cut by a
third making the sum of the internal angles on one side
less than two right angles, then those lines if sufficiently
produced will meet on the side on which the said sum is
less than two right angles. This means to me that if I had
two lines drawn on a plane and wished to find where they
would meet, I could draw a third line cutting them, and
ascertaining on which side the sum of the two [ins]internal[/ins] angles was
less than two right angles, [del]and[/del] should lengthen the lines on
that side. In like manner, [ins]all[/ins] doubt is a state of hesitancy
about an imagined state of things.

[del]§2.[/del] Doubt is an uneasy condition from which we struggle
to free ourselves. In this, it is like [del]any other stimulus[/del]. It is
true that just as men may, for the sake of the pleasures of
the table, like to be hungry and take means to make
themselves so, although hunger always involves a desire to
fill the stomach, so for the sake of the pleasures of inquiry
men may like to seek out doubts. [del]But[/del] [ins]Yet[/ins] for all that, doubt,
essentially involves a struggle to escape it.
