The three figures of [u]a priori[/u] inference are as follows: —

$$\begin{array}{ccc}
\text{All } A \text{ is } B & \text{All } A \text{ is } B & X \text{ is } B \\
X \text{ is } A & X \text{ is not } B & X \text{ is } A \\
\hline
\therefore X \text{ is } B & \therefore X \text{ is not } A & \therefore \text{Some } A \text{ is } B
\end{array}$$

The first figure consists in taking as a predicate the predicate of another
predicate. Every predicate is a symbol of its subject. So that this inference
depends on the fact that the symbol of the symbol is itself a symbol
of the same object. Now the most that a symbol of a symbol can con-
tain is the symbol itself; hence if all this can be predicated, any other
symbol of the symbol may be; but [del]unless[/del] [ins]the proposition[/ins] $A$ which is $A = A$ is the law
of identity. The second figure depends in the same way on the equi-
valent law of contradiction, there is no symbol whose symbol is not a
symbol. The third figure depends upon the law of excluded middle,
a symbol is a symbol, [ins]or not according[/ins] [del]just so far[/del] as it coincides with its object.

The principles of the three inductive inferences of the three major
premises of the above forms are, $1^{\text{st}}$ The symbol of an object has
the same predicates as its object $2^{\text{nd}}$ Nothing which is not the symbol of an
object has the same predicates as its object $3^{\text{rd}}$ A [ins]predicate[/ins] [del]symbol[/del] applies to an
object or not according to its predicability of the symbol of that object.
