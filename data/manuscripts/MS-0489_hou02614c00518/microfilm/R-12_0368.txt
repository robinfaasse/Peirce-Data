We thus see that
"It thunders"
is logically equivalent to
"Either it thunders or [circle] is true"

But by the definition of logical equivalents either follows from the other. Hence, if it is true that it either thunders or that [circle] is true, it must be true that it thunders. But this can only be necessary, supposing that if [del][circle][/del] is true, it is true that it thunders. Consequently, we must conclude that [u]if [circle] is true, it is true that it thunders[/u]

But what has thus [del]be[/del] [ins]been[/ins] proved in regard to the proposition "It thunders" is, by the same reasoning, proved in regard to any other proposition whatever. Hence we must conclude
[u]If [circle] is true, all propositions are true.[/u]

Hence, [circle] is absurd.

All absurd propositions are equivalent, for if any absurd proposition were true all propositions, including any other absurd proposition would be true. Hence we may interpret [circle] by any absurd proposition such as "all propositions are true", or "[del]who[/del] [ins]something[/ins] false is true" or "what I [del]aver[/del] [ins]assert[/ins] is not true."
