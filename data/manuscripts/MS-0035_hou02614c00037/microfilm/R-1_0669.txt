In a paper on the logic of number, published
in [del]1881[/del], I remarked that multitudes could be sepa-
rated into three classes, according to the applicability
to them of the Fermatian inference and the [del]syllogism[/del]
of transposed quantity. To understand the essential
character of the method of reasoning invented by
Fermat, let it be required to prove that every one
of a certain multitude of objects, say the S's, has a
certain character, Q. We must first find one or more
of the S's which evidently have that character. We
must then discover a relation among the S's having
the following properties. [del][To express this,[/del] I use the
expressions "A is I of B", "B is I'd by A", to mean
the same thing, namely that A stands in the relation to
B expressed by I.]

third is itself I of that third.
