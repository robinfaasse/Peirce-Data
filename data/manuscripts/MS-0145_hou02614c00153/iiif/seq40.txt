PART I. PRELIMINARY CONSIDERATIONS.

The study of Psychology, like that of all sciences,
starts with certain data and is carried on for the
purpose of reaching certain conclusions. By data we
mean those facts or phenomena about which our study 
deals. These facts are, in the first place, the objects of
observation and description. In the second place, these
facts must be classified or grouped together according
to their similarities and differences. In the third place,
it is the task of science to find the causes of its phe-
nomena. We should not be satisfied to describe a fact
accurately; we should also explain it. This can only be
done by showing its relations to other facts. These relations
are expressed by the so-called laws of science. A law
is a statement of a constant relation between certain
phenomena. When we say that we have found the law of
any phenomenon, we mean that we have discovered its
cause. The end of all science, then, is to discover the
laws of the facts with which it deals. This process of
explaining facts by finding their causes is also called
induction. The work of induction is not complete until
all the facts belonging to any science have been
organized into a system.
These preliminary considerations lead us to a general 
definition of Psychology. Psychology is the science 
which seeks to describe and explain the phenomena
of consciousness. By phenomena of consciousness we
mean those facts of which we are aware in our daily
life, such as our thoughts, our feelings, and our desires.
In the following chapters, we shall consider the
main classes of these facts and shall seek to discover
the laws which govern them.
