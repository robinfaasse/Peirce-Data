The balance
between the amount of water in a soil
and the salts it holds in solution is one of
the most vital relations to plant life. When
the proportion of salt is high it may even
reach values such that the soil becomes un-
inhabitable for most plants. Such soils are
common in the arid and semi arid regions
of our country and are called "alkali lands."
In these regions irrigation is practised and
with it we are constantly face to face
with the problem of the control of the salt
content of the soil. As the water evaporates
from the soil, the salts are left behind in a
more concentrated form, and unless drainage
is provided, the water table may rise and
at the same time the concentration of salt
may reach such a point that the soil is
no longer of use for agriculture. In these
cases we find it necessary to provide systems
of drainage and at the same time treat the
soil with more water so that the salts may
be leached away.
The control of the salt content is also of
great importance in the soil which we find in
temperate regions. Many of our agricultural
practices lead to a change in this regard. The
use of fertilizers increases the salt content.
The growth of plants on the other hand leads
to a depletion of the salt supply. This is
the reason for the use of fertilizers and the
importance of the rotation of crops.
The salts in the soil which are of the most
importance to plants are the compounds
of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium.
