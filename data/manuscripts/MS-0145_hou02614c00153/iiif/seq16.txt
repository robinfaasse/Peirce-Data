[del]The whole in this last sense is something beyond the[/del]
[del]parts of which it is the sum. It is that sum plus the fact[/del]
[del]of its collection in this particular way. We have[/del]
[del]seen that this is what the whole of a definition is. It is[/del]
[del]more than the set of its constituents, for these can be[/del]
[del]otherwise arranged. It is these constituents as so[/del]
[del]collected. This is something more than the constituents[/del]
[del]taken separately. And it is something more than[/del]
[del]their sum. For the sum would not disappear if the[/del]
[del]collection were changed: but this whole would disappear.[/del]
[del]But this whole itself cannot be defined because it has[/del]
[del]no parts. It is something simple. This can be seen from[/del]
[del]another point of view. For if the whole of a definition[/del]
[del]is the sum of its parts plus their unity, then let the[/del]
[del]parts be a b c and the unity u. Then the whole is[/del]
[del]a+b+c+u. But then we may go on to ask what the unity[/del]

[del]be a+b+c+u+u'. And so we may go on forever. To avoid[/del]
[del]this infinity, we must suppose that the unity is not a[/del]
[del]further constituent, but the whole itself. If the whole[/del]
[del]has no further constituent, then it is simple. And[/del]
[del]this simple whole is not the same as the sum of its[/del]
