[pn]31[/pn] [u]On the Theory of Collections and Multitudes[/u]
By C. S. Peirce

1. In a problem of pure mathematics there are certain explicit hypotheses; and [del]anything[/del] [ins]proposition[/ins] not conflicting with them is [u]possible[/u]. Two [ins]or more[/ins] possibilities are [u]simultaneously possible[/u], if, and only if, each is possible in every [del]possible[/del] [ins]possible[/ins] combination of realization of others. For [del]instance[/del] [ins]example[/ins], there may be room in a carriage [del]for[/del] for any one of a certain collection of persons; but it does not follow that there is room for all simultaneously. If, however, there is room for each one in every possible distribution of the others among entering and not entering, then there is room for all, simultaneously.

2. Every possibility has one, and only one, [del]negative[/del] [ins]negative[/ins], which is the negative of no other possibility; and this negative may be possible.

3. A subject or condition is said to have [u]definite being[/u], if, and only if, no possibility and its negative are simultaneously possible of that subject or under that condition. [del]Thus[/del] [ins]For example[/ins], it may be simultaneously possible that an apple should be red, and that an apple should not be red; hence 'that there is an apple' is a condition without 'definite being.'
