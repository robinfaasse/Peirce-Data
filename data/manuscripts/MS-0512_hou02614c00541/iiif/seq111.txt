The entire truth about characters is summed up in De Morgan’s
statement that, taking any collection of things whatever, there is a
quality common and peculiar to those things. These words convey
the idea; but our algebra affords us no means of satisfactorily
stating it. When we deal with an inenumerable universe (and
no man can sum up his entire experience) we cannot make
[del]every[/del] all the statements, nor every form of statement that is mathe-
matically possible. We can only make enumerable statements
about single things; and we can state [u]rules[/u] by means of a device
which reduces them to statements about single things. I say: "I
will leave it to you to choose any man you like, and then I assert
beforehand that that man is mortal." That virtually states that all
men are mortal. But when it comes to taking "any collection of
things whatever," — I can only say that supposing it to be a fact that
taking any adversary you like, that adversary will have selected
something to please himself, then I say there is a quality such that
[del]anything [ins]you may select now[/ins] was either[/del] [ins]and[/ins]
[del]everything[/del] selected by an adversary possesses that quality and
everything was not selected by an adversary and does not possess
that quality. But this introduces us to a universe of adversaries as
difficult to define as a quality.
