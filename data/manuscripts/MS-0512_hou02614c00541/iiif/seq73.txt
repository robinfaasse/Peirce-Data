The logician of the theological stamp is inclined to say, we have seen that
there is absolutely nothing in demonstrative reasoning but syllogism; why
then delay longer upon it? But the experimentalist must try how this is in
practice; and when he tries, he finds that however true that may or must be,
yet the whole gist of reasoning lies outside the syllogism. Nor is it merely
that new premises are used. No: so far, we have utterly failed to bring out
the essential nature of reasoning.

Come, then, to practical applications, —not to fabricated examples;
they can teach nothing except by being carelessly constructed. Come
to the living applications. MacColl was the first to offer a really prac-
tical application of Boolean algebra, when by means of it he showed
how to transpose [ins]multiple[/ins] the signs of definite integration when the limits of one
integration involve the variables of another. Schröder, too, has applied
the Algebra of Dyadic Relations to the solution of real problems of Logic,
such as this: "what must be the constitution of a relation in order that it may
be [u]transitive[/u], that is, in order that [u]x -< x[/u]?" He finds the necessary and
sufficient condition to be that x shall be so related to some relative that
[u]x = u ⋅ (u ≼ \overline{u})[/u]. But, for my part, while extolling the merit of all that
work, I prefer to take up mathematics, branch by branch, and inquire
into its logic. I do not for an instant fail in honor to these students nor to
their achievements; but I must gang my own gait.
