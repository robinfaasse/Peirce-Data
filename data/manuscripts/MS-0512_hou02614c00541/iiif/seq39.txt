XII.

I am a little disappointed with my graphs. My cumbrous
General Algebra, with all its faults, seems preferable. But I have
another algebraic system which is not without merits. It expresses
things simply. The difficulty with it is that it is so overburdened
with identical formulae. This algebra may be combined with
the general algebra to any extent. Its peculiarity is that it dis-
penses with the quantifiers and indices, if not wholly, yet for
the most part by introducing [u]two relative operations[/u]. There
are really eight of these operations; but it is not necessary
to have special signs for more than two of them in order to
accomplish the main purpose of this algebra, which I name
my [u]Algebra of Dyadic Relatives.[/u]

The two operations [del]for which special[/del] [ins]are used are[/ins] signs are used are as follows:

1[ins]st[/ins], [u]Relative Multiplication[/u], which I denote by writing the
relatives together with no intervening sign. Let l and b be
two dyadic relatives, say "— loves —" and "— benefits —". Then
lb is such a relative that

(lb)[ins]ij[/ins] = [del]Π[/del] [del]l[sub]ik[/sub] b[/del] Σ[sub]k[/sub] l[sub]ik[/sub] . b[sub]kj[/sub] .

In other words, lb means "— is a lover of [del]an[/del] [ins]some[/ins] existing benefactor of —".

2[ins]nd[/ins], [u]Relative Addition[/u], which I denote by writing the relatives
with the sign † (a dagger with a scorpion tail) [del]between[/del] between them.

Then (l † b)[sub]ij[/sub] = Π[sub]k[/sub] l[sub]ik[/sub] + b[sub]kj[/sub] .

In other words, l † b means "— is lover of whatever there may be
except benefactors of —" or "— is lover of all but benefactors of —".
