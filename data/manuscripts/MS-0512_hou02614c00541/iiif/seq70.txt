The way to use this algebra, or any algebra, to the best advantage
is with a free hand. Don't be bound down to any forms or methods.
Mix with it freely indices and quantifiers. Broaden the interpre-
tation and everything. Think always of the problem in hand. Do
not think of the algebra, but learn to think [u]in[/u] the algebra.

XIII.

Let us now see how all this apparatus is going to work in prac-
tice. I often feel disposed to define an intelligent man as one who
experiments. When an undeveloped mind comes to me, I often
give him a paragraph to read which contains a sentence. Let
the reader do so and so, that is, perform some mental experi-
ment. Then after a few minutes in which I am supposed
to be occupied with doing that, I [del]say[/del] [ins]ask[/ins] if he had read it. Yes.
Well, what was the result? It appears he never has performed
the experiment. Then I try to make him understand that that
is all the difference between the man that thinks and the man
that does not think. The difficulty with the theologians is largely
that they do not think. That is they do not perform mental experi-
ments. The reason is, they have no burning passion to learn; they
only burn to [u]teach[/u]. Now the man who wants to teach is convinced
he knows, and the man who is convinced he knows cannot learn.
Most of those who have written on logic have been thorough-bred
theologians. Two things are requisite to an experiment. First
the doing impulse that sets the idea into operation Second the
eyes to see clearly what the result is.
