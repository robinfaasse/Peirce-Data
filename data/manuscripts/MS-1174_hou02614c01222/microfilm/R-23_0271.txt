[u]Accident[/u]. The [del]term[/del] [ins]word[/ins] [u]accidens[/u], as a noun, occurs first in Quin-
tilian as the translation of [greek]συμβεβηκός[/greek]. This term in its strict
sense belongs to Aristotle, but was derived from the ordinary usage of
the Greek language.

1. Whatever is [u]in[/u] substance; that is, whatever cannot be supposed to
exist without supposing that something else (the substance) exists. In
this [del]the[/del] sense, it is opposed to [u]substance[/u]. This is a common sense in Aristotle
and in all later philosophers.
2. [del]Whatever[/del] [ins]or mark[/ins] [del]quality of an individual or individuals[/del] [ins]which[/ins] does not [ins]necessarily[/ins] belong to
[del]its subject.[/del] [del]the whole species.[/del] In this sense (opposed to [u]property[/u]) it is also used by Ar-
istotle and all subsequent writers.
"Accident is that which is present and absent without the de-
struction of its subject" Porphyry.
