<u>ABSTRAHENT.</u>

This term was often used in the 12th century (see the treatise
De intellectibus Cousin Frag. Philos. p 481.) Kant [del]has endeavored[/del]
suggested the revival of the term to denote pure concepts of
the understanding.

<u>Abstrahentium non est mendacium.</u> This is an awk-
ward way of saying that a quality which is abstracted from
is not denied. If abstraction is not denial, far less
is generalization. Yet the two seem to be confounded

by Hegel when he says that being and nothing are
the same because they are equally the absence of
all determination.

<u>ABSURD</u>

1. That which involves an error obvious to common sense.
2. That [del]which[/del] would involve an error, which would offer
no illusion or deception whatever; as, that to have a hun-
dred dollars is absolutely the same as to be without it.

<u>ABSURDITY.</u>

1. The absurd in either sense.
2. "An absurdity to a particular person is a proposition, true
or not, which conflicts with a proposition which has to that
person the force of an axiom." Chauvin.
3. The quality of the absurd.
