The first great division of science is according to its fundamental purpose; which is for the Theoretical Sciences, simply and solely knowledge of God's truth; for the Practical Sciences, the uses of life. Louis Agassiz [del]did[/del] called branches, those divisions of the animal kingdom in which he thought he saw different plans. I will, somewhat in imitation of him, call that first great division of science, according to purpose a division into two branches. [del]I shall[/del] [ins]Below, I shall[/ins] undertake only the classification of the theoretical sciences. [del]For the present[/del] [ins]sciences proper[/ins], I shall speak only of the first sub branch, the theoretical [ins]proper[/ins] [del]sciences[/del].
Of the theoretical sciences, in respect to their relation to observation, the [del]source[/del] [ins]mother[/ins] of all knowledge, we must distinguish three classes.* The first is Mathematics, which does not undertake to ascertain any facts whatsoever, but merely posits hypotheses, and traces out their consequences†
The second class is Philosophy, which deals with positive truth, or fact, but contents itself with such observations

[ured]* I do not, with Ventura and other catholics, admit any sciences resting on authority. Not but that I, like everybody else accept doctrines on authority; only that, according to my notion, is not science. Neither do I with Lablanca, admit a class of documentary sciences, although that seems very plausible. But documentary evidence, as Lablanca admits, comes in everywhere; and though some sciences are peculiarly documental, they cannot be said to contrast on the whole with others. He includes in this class, history, linguistics, geography, statistics, and political economy. These have little in common and are not remote from some other sciences.[/ured]

[u]† Many writers have given the first place to mathematics; as Cave, Wundt, Ribot, Comte, Schopenhauer, etc.[/u]
