[u]The Logic of Science[/u].

In beginning a course of lectures upon the Logic of Science, it is first of all necessary to
define the subject. What is meant by Logic, and what is meant by Science?

By Logic, I mean the science of the laws of stable belief.

By Science, I mean the pursuit of knowledge for its own sake, and by those who have been
trained in the methods of research.

The definition of Logic, as the science of the laws of stable belief, is one which requires
explanation.

In the first place, what is belief?

Belief is a state of mind which is characterized by three features.

First, it is a state of mind of which we are conscious, and which we can describe.

Second, it is a state of mind which involves a willingness to act in a certain way, if
the occasion for such action should arise.

Third, it is a state of mind which is accompanied by a feeling of satisfaction, or at least
by the absence of that feeling of dissatisfaction which accompanies doubt.

Doubt is the opposite of belief. It is an unstable state of mind, from which we seek to
escape into the stable state of belief.

The process by which we pass from doubt to belief is called inquiry.

The object of inquiry is to settle opinion, or to produce belief.

But not all beliefs are stable. A belief may be shaken by new experiences, or by the
arguments of others.

A stable belief is one which cannot be shaken by any further inquiry.

The laws of stable belief are the laws which determine what beliefs are stable, and how
they may be attained.

These laws are the subject-matter of Logic.
