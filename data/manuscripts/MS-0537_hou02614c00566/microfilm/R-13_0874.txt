[del]The first step towards the solution of the problem is to see as clearly as possible[/del] what the problem is.
[del]What is it that we want to know? We want to know how far, if at all, those of our beliefs[/del]
[del]which we cannot help having, but which we cannot prove, are trustworthy. At the very[/del]
[del]outset, we are met by a difficulty, which we must, with after reflection, resolve.[/del]
[del]We want to know whether our basic beliefs, that is to say, our primary beliefs, are[/del]
[del]trustworthy. But what is meant by "trustworthy"? In the case of a witness of a[/del]
[del]matter of fact, we say he is trustworthy when his statement is true. But is that[/del]
[del]what we mean by the trustworthiness of our primary beliefs? Let us see. If we[/del]
[del]could know that they were true, we should know it, either through our senses, or[/del]
[del]by reasoning. But we cannot know them through our senses, for they are not[/del]
[del]objects of sense; nor can we know them by reasoning, for by hypothesis, they are[/del]
[del]primary beliefs, and therefore cannot be proved. It seems, therefore, that we[/del]
[del]cannot know whether they are true or not. But if we cannot know whether they[/del]
[del]are true, how can we know whether they are trustworthy? It seems that we[/del]
[del]cannot. But is this really so? Let us look at the matter a little more closely.[/del]
[del]What is a belief? A belief is a state of mind in which we are prepared to act[/del]
[del]upon a certain proposition as if it were true. If we are prepared to act upon[/del]
[del]it, we must have some reason for doing so. If we have no reason, we are not[/del]
[del]acting rationally. But if we have a reason, that reason must be either[/del]
[del]that we know the proposition to be true, or that we have some other reason[/del]
[del]for believing it. If we know it to be true, then, as we have seen, it cannot[/del]
[del]be a primary belief. If we have some other reason, what can that reason be?[/del]
[del]It can only be that we cannot help believing it. But is this a reason?[/del]
[del]It is a reason in this sense, that we cannot do otherwise. But is it a[/del]
[del]reason in the sense of a justification? That is the question. If we cannot[/del]
[del]help believing it, we are, of course, justified in believing it, in the sense that[/del]
[del]we cannot be blamed for doing so. But are we justified in the sense that[/del]
[del]our belief is likely to be true? That is the question. And to this question,[/del]
[del]it seems, we can give no answer. For if we could, we should have some[/del]
[del]reason for our belief, other than the mere fact that we cannot help believing[/del]
[del]it; and then it would no longer be a primary belief. It seems, therefore, that[/del]
[del]we can never know whether our primary beliefs are trustworthy, in the sense of[/del]
[del]being likely to be true. But is this the only sense of trustworthiness?[/del]
