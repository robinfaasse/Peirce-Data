Here again the element of arbitrary choice comes
in to determine what shall be inferred from given
premises. We see that it will not do to speak of
"the" conclusion from given premises, because there
are [del]endless[/del] [ins]innumerable[/ins] different conclusions.

§184. [del]Step[/del] [ins]Third Step[/ins]. This will not, by any means,
always be required. It consists in generalizing indices,
when this is useful, in order to make the selections
out of the same collections. If the lectical part, or
"logisterium" (if the student wishes he has my
permission to call it a [u]Peircian[/u])

[ins]contains $\Sigma$,[/ins] and we wish to replace the $x$
by a new index $i$, not already in the [del]Quantifier[/del] [ins]logisterium[/ins], and such that every $x$ is an $i$,
we can do so at once by simply [del]multiplying[/del] [ins]compounding[/ins] every letter of the Boolian having
$x$ as an index [del]by $x_i$[/del]. Thus, if we have "some woman is an angel" written in
the form $\Sigma_w a_w$ we may replace this by $\Sigma_i (a_i w_i)$. It will be more often useful to
replace the index of a $\Pi$ by a wider one; and this will be done by [del]adding $\bar{x}_i$[/del] [ins]aggregating[/ins] to
every letter having $x$ as an index. Thus, if we have "all dogs are animals, and
all animals are vertebrates" written thus
$$\Pi_d a_d \Pi_a v_a,$$

where $a$ and $\alpha$ alike mean [u]animal[/u], it is of
course easy enough to remember that the
indices are virtually antecedents, and to
conclude at once
$$v_d \alpha_d$$
$$\therefore v_d.$$

But
it will be found convenient to replace the last
index by $i$, standing for any object, and to write the proposition
$$\Pi_i (\bar{a}_i v_i).$$
