§12. [u]1[ins]st[/ins] Step[/u]./ The different premises having been written with distinct indices (the
same index not used in two propositions) are [del]written[/del] [ins]compounded[/ins] together, and all the $\Pi$'s
and $\Sigma$'s are to be brought to the left. This can evidently be done, for
$\Pi_i x_i \Pi_j y_j = \Pi_i \Pi_j x_i y_j$
$\Sigma_i x_i \Pi_j y_j = \Sigma_i \Pi_j x_i y_j$
$\Sigma_i x_i \Sigma_j y_j = \Sigma_i \Sigma_j x_i y_j$.

The same premise may be written in more than
once, different conclusions resulting according
to the number of times each is used. In [del]each[/del] [ins]the different[/ins] repe-
titions of a premise it is to be written with different
indices.

Already the logic of relatives teaches us here
a novel lesson about reasoning in general.
The student of exclusively non-relative logic
is quite unaware that anything can be gained
by bringing in again a premise already used. Or-
dinary syllogistic gives no hint of such a thing; nay
seems rather opposed to it. Yet there are traces of
this great truth even in the ordinary Boolean
Calculus.
