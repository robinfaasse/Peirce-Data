I endeavor in drawing up these rules to pursue a middle
[del]course between sending the student too far back to first[/del]
[del]principles, and loading him with too many rules. The[/del]
[del]question how many, not indispensable rules he wants[/del]
[del]depends on how much he is going to use the calculus.[/del]
If he is going to use it every day, it will pay him to take
considerable trouble, once for all, [ins]in order[/ins] to learn rules which,
though not saving any large proportion of the trouble,
yet effect a large aggregate saving of labor. But I have,
for example, not considered such things as this: that
the last part of the copulendum may be taken away
from its position at the head of the whole proposition and
be placed before the part which alone it concerns.

The student ought to learn to [u]think[/u] [del]in[/del] [ins]and write[/ins] [ins]in[/ins] this notation
and not translate it. It must be his language; and
he must learn to use it with freedom, mastery, intel-
ligence. In fact, [del]he ought[/del] it should become to him
what trigonometry is to the expert mathematician,
a quasi-instinct.
