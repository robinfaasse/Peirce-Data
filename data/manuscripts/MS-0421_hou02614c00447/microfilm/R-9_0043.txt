§216. [del]Of[/del] [u]Natural Classification[/u].

J. S. Mill defines a "real Kind" as [del]as a class[/del] [ins]a group[/ins] distinguished by unknown multitudes of properties, while an artificial class has only a few properties and these determinate. Mill evidently uses Kind in the old sense of a species [ins]*[/ins] [ins][* Southey, who seldom blunders about English, was, nevertheless totally mistaken in saying (The Doctor, ch. lxxii) that Kind means a genus and not a species. Wilson (Rule of Reason, 1551) says, "Every species, or kinde, in thinges is of two sortes. The one is called the lowest kinde, which is alwaies spoken of every propre name, and ever is the kinde, neither can it at any time be the general woorde." Blundevile (Arte of Logicke, 1599, Bk. I., ch. iv) says, "Genus is a generall kind, which may be spoken of many things differing in speciall kind."][/ins]; and at the time he wrote, [del]zoological[/del] [ins]zoölogical[/ins] and botanical species [del]were supposed to be[/del] [ins]generally believed to be[/ins] the only natural classes. [ins]†[/ins]
