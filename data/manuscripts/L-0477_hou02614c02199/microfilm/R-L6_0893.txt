The other person whom I met in Washington for the special
purpose of hearing about Charles Peirce was a lady closely
connected by marriage. I am sorry that I don't exactly re-
member her name; only that it was something like Ellis and
began with E. Mrs. Charles Inches could undoubtedly give you
her name. I got a rather definite and pleasant impression
from what she told me. All I remember is that Peirce would
make himself very charming at a fashionable dinner party, in
talking to one person at a time. This I thought interesting,
considering the well-known utter inability of Peirce to
make himself intelligible to classes and audiences. I
suppose that his instincts as a gentleman made him adapt
himself in sympathy to the interests of his listener, what-
ever these interests might be; but that before several people
he had to be his real self and then he was completely over
almost everybody's head.

Professor Sedgewick (at that time curator of the Lowell
Institute) told me that they had him once to lecture at the
Lowell Lectures, and that there were plenty in the audience
for the first lecture, but that nobody understood a thing,
and that by the second lecture there were only three or four
people. As I remember the story, there was only one man who
stuck it out, and he was a man who always went to the Lowell
Lectures because they were free. But I will not swear to
the latter as it may be my own invention. I remember that it
was rather funny as well as a pathetic failure.
