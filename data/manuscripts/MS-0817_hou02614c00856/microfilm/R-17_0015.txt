An important question in formal logic is whether a hypothetical proposition is essentially the same as a categorical proposition, or not. I maintain that it is so, that not only can a hypothetical be expressed as a categorical, but also that conversely there is [del]no[/del] [ins]logical[/ins] species of categorical which is not logically equivalent to some species of hypothetical.

For example, "It does not follow [del]that[/del] because A is true that B is necessarily true;" "[del]If[/del] [ins]Every[/ins] the government could be ridiculed with impunity [del]that[/del] would necessarily tend to bring about a license to attack the rights of property;" "That I should [ins]prefer to[/ins] be killed in a duel [del]would be preferable[/del] [ins]rather than to be[/ins] [del]to being[/del] [ins]may[/ins] dishonored in the eyes of society." All these are hypothetical propositions in the sense defined.

In the first place every hypothetical has to do with a range of possibilities. That is possible which in an assumed state of information is not known to be false. The state of information may be the actual state of the speaker or it may be some imaginary state in which either more or (more usually) less is assumed to be known than actually is known. This gives rise to the various kinds of possibility.
