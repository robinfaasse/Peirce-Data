[u]§ 1. On the Numbers of Forms of Sets.[/u] [ins]f x[/ins]
By [del]C. S. Peirce.[/del]

[del]f x[/del] The number of objects (say, letters) in a set may [del]be called[/del] the [u]plurality[/u] of the set. If the places for single objects in a set are all distinguished from one another (say, as [u]first[/u], [u]second[/u], etc), the set may [del]be called[/del] a [u]perfectly ordered set[/u], or [u]ad[/u]; or, according to its plurality, a monad, dyad, [u]triad[/u], [u]tetrad[/u], etc. Some of the objects of a set may [del]be[/del] alike, so that the same kind may go in several places, although one place can contain but one kind. Two sets of the same plurality and diversity are said to have the same [u]form[/u], if one can be changed into the other by simply changing the characters of its objects; but an interchange of two places is regarded [del]as a change[/del] of form. Thus, AAB and BBA are of the same [del]form[/del], but ABA is of a different form. The [del]number of different[/del] forms may be called the [u]formality[/u].
