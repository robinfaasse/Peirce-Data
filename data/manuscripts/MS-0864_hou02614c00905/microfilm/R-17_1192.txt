[u]An Classification of the Sciences.[/u]

This classification, which aims to [del]be based[/del] [ins]base itself[/ins] on the principal affinities of the objects classified, is concerned not with [del]possible[/del] [ins]all[/ins] sciences, nor with so many branches of knowledge, but with sciences in their present condition, as so many businesses of groups of living men. It borrows its idea from Comte's classification; namely, the idea that one science depends upon another for fundamental principles, but does not furnish those such principles to that other. It turns out that in most cases the divisions are trichotomic; the first of the three members relating to [ins]universal[/ins] fundamental elements or laws, the second arranging [del]classes[/del] [ins]varieties[/ins] of forms and seeking to bring them under universal laws, the third going into the utmost detail [del]and[/del] [ins]describing individual phenomena and[/ins] endeavoring to explain them. But not all the divisions are of this character.

The classification has been carried into great detail; but only its broader divisions are here given.
