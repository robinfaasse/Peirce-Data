But it may be objected that perhaps the chair vacated at the
table will not at once be filled, so that the banker is deprived of the
opportunity to recoup himself for the loss he has just sustained.
The reply is, that the opportunity is of no value, because, the game
being even, the banker would be just as likely to lose more as to regain
what he had lost. But at this point some objector will be pretty sure to
start a certain fallacious mode of reasoning which is widely current.
Namely, it will be said that because the game is even, the banker's gains
and losses will be equal in the long run; and therefore, if he has lost,
there must be a tendency in future play to make up that loss. The reply
to that is, that the game is not even in the sense that the banker will neither
lose nor gain one franc as the result of a million bets, but only that
he will lose or gain in a million bets only about ten times as much
as in ten thousand bets, or about a hundred times as much as in
a hundred bets. This law has often been verified by experiment;
and it is the mathematical consequence of the condition that a loss,
once incurred, has no effect upon subsequent bets.
