Or rather, since it is evidently logically quite possible he should never win a single throw, it would be more accurate to say that the probability of his winning the desired franc is so great as to be numerically indistinguishable from certainty. To continue our hypothesis: as soon as this player has won his franc, as he probably will in a few throws, and is quasi-sure to do some time, let him vacate his place at the table and make room for another player. Suppose that these are indefinitely many players, and a constant crowd of onlookers anxiously waiting for places at the tables, and suppose every man of them pursues the same policy of one-franc bets and of quitting forever, as soon as he has gained one franc, and not before. Then it is clear that every man of them will win his franc. Nevertheless, the game being perfectly even, it is equally manifest that the bank will neither win nor lose anything to speak of; nor is the banker a whit worse off than if his whole clientele were to reverse their tactics, and were always to quit when they began to net a loss.

This sounds mighty paradoxical. But we must expect to slump into paradox at almost every step, when we venture upon the treacherous ground of the probabilities of hypotheses involving infinite quantities. Yet infinities are really unavoidable. They lurk beneath the surface of every supposition whatsoever.
