Suppose that instead of betting one franc each time,
the player tried a "martingale", that is, doubled his bet every
time he lost, so that the first bet he won should net him a
franc. If then, his fortune were 1023 francs, the odds would be
612 to 1 that he would win a franc before his fortune was lost.
Hence of every 613 players, having fortunes of 1023 francs, 612
would win 612 francs from the bank while one would lose 1023 francs,
leaving a profit to the bank of 411 francs on 613 francs risked.
If the martingale-player's fortune was 1,048,575 francs, the odds
in favor of his winning his franc would be as 375899 to 1; so that
for every 375,900 players of this class, 375899 would win their
375,899 francs, while one would lose his 1,048,575 francs, leaving
a profit to the bank of 672,676 francs on 375,900 francs risked.
Were the fortune of the player 1,073,741,824 or over a milliard
of francs, the odds in favor of his winning his franc would be about
230,467,000 to 1, leaving a profit to the bank of 843,275,000
francs on 230,467,000 risked. We, thus, see how much more profitable
rich men are to the bank than poor ones, the moment they attempt to
use their wealth to force winnings. The natural course of events
is a bank like this.
