That it really makes no sort of difference to the
banker when the individual players break off play, can be
clearly proved in various ways. If the banker has to pay a
franc, it is all one to him whether he pays it to Mr. Uck or
to M. de La Roche-foucauld; and if he has to receive the same
coin he cares not at all whose purse it may smell of. Whether
a player, loser or winner, chooses to keep on or to yield his
seat to another is all one to him; for the roulette ball will go
where the physical forces make it go; and those forces to not
depend upon what man sits in a given chair. The game is perfectly
even; that is, every player will lose as often as he gains, however
he bets. That implies that for any one player the result in the
long run is substantially the same as if, instead of betting now
on odd and now on even, he were to bet always on odd or on even.
For that will be true if all the numbers occur equally often; and
to say that they do not do so, and yet that every player wins half
the time, however he may choose to bet, is to say that there is
some occult connection between the player's choice and the occur-
rence or non-occurrence of the number he choses. Now anybody can
believe such a mysterious connection exists, if he has sufficiently
silly to do so; but it is contrary to the hypothesis of the problem
now under consideration. Suppose, then, a player bets always on
even numbers, until he surrenders his place to another who continues
the same practice. There is certainly nothing in that change of
players which could cause the ball to rest in another hole, next
time. It cannot then affect the interest of the banker.
