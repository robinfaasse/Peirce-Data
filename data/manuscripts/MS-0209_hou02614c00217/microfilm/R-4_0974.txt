The government, as mathematics amply demonstrates can lose nothing at
all. The young man or woman so suddenly and happily provided with
this auspicious start in life could not, and would not, object to
the deduction from it of a tax; and if these persons were taxed one
per cent of their winnings, since there are nearly a million and
a half persons in the United States who annually reach the age of
21, the proceeds would be sufficient to carry on the national
government.

We must expect that this truly beneficent scheme will be
opposed by the whole confraternity of gold-bugs and usurers, since
it would make the people independent of them. They cannot attack
the truth of the mathematical principle upon which the project
reposes; but they will raise all sorts of embarrassing cavils con-
cerning details of the plan. It will be objected, for instance,
that a certain number of players will lose so deeply that they will
remain for years in the games.

In order to shut the mouths of these gentry, it might be
well for the government in the first place to advance to the young
people, during the period of their betting, the interest on the
lac they are about to receive, and in the second place, instead
of paying out gold, to simply transfer credits on the books of the
Treasurer of the United States, on the order of those having such
credits. These orders would be similar to bank cheques.
