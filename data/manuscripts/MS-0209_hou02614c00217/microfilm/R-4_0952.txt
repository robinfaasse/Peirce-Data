Were the resources, as well of the bank [del]as[/del] [ins]or[/ins] of every player, limitless,
and the game perfectly fair, sundry curious situations might arise.
Suppose, for example, a player to sit down, resolved to win one franc at one
franc bets, and then quit play forever. His staying-power being unlimited,
and the game perfectly even, he would be sure to succeed. Or rather, since
it is evidently, logically, quite possible he should never win a single
throw, it would be more accurate to say that the probability of his winning
the desired franc is so great as to be numerically indistinguishable from
certainty. To continue our hypothesis: as soon as this player has won
his franc, as he probably will in a few throws, and is quasi-sure to do some-
time, let him vacate his place at the table and make room for another
player. Suppose that there are indefinitely many players, and a constant
crowd of onlookers anxiously waiting for places at the table; and suppose
every man of them pursues the same policy of one-franc bets and of quitting
forever, as soon as he has gained one franc, and not before. Then it is clear
that every man of them will win his franc. Nevertheless, the game being
perfectly even, it is equally manifest that the bank will neither win nor lose
anything to speak of; nor is the banker a whit worse off than if his whole
clientèle were to reverse their tactics, and were always to quit when they
began to net a loss.

into paradox at almost every step, when we venture upon the
treacherous ground of the probabilities of hypotheses involving
