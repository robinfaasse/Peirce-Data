Knotty Points in the Doctrine of Chances

Should the head of the house of Rothschild, during a
sojourn at Monaco, take it into his head to earn one franc each
day by one-franc bets on [u]rouge[/u] or [u]noir[/u], [u]pair[/u] or [u]impair[/u], [u]passe[/u] or
[u]impasse[/u], the least hazardous chances at the roulette-table, and
having won his single franc to go back to his hotel to enjoy his
victory, then, supposing he were able to crowd into the day the
vast number of bets required, it would not be prudent in him to
prolong his stay to a full week. For since there are 18 ways of
winning and 20 of losing, all supposed to be equally probable,
so that the odds are as 9 in favor of the player's winning any
single bet to 10 in favor of the bank, it follows that the
probability that M. de Rothschild would win his first day's
franc before he was ruined would be [ins]9[/ins]/[del]10[/del][ins]10[/ins]. The mathematical proof
of this will be found in a note at the end of this paper. Then,
the probability that he would succeed in doing this for six days
running is ([ins]9[/ins]/[del]10[/del][ins]10[/ins])[sup]6[/sup] = 0.53144, or just a little more than an even
chance; and the probability that he would succeed in the first
seven days is ([ins]9[/ins]/[del]10[/del][ins]10[/ins])[sup]7[/sup] = 0.4782969, or just a little less than an
even chance. In other words, it is more likely than not than on
some day of the first week he would rise from the table a ruined
man.
