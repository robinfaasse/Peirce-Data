It is plain these form a Pascal's triangle, which is the icon of the development of the powers of a binomial. If we imagine any coefficient of this triangle to have its value changed, - say, for instance, by an error of calculation, - this will effect all the coefficients in subsequent columns that are not more distant from it in horrizontal lines than in columns, - and the effect will follow the simple law of spreading that every change extends to the line above and the line below in the next following column. Now when the winners leave the table, this is equivalent to changing all the coefficients of the line "Having won I" to zero. We need not inquire how coefficients in higher lines will be affected because they can only affect the lower ones (which are the only coefficients which have any meaning in our problem) through their effect on the coefficients of the line "Having won I". We have then to subtract from the coefficients of the line the series of values, 1, 3, 10, 35, etc. and from all other coefficients the amounts which result from the law of spreading. But cannot we replace this complicated statement by another which shall explain all the changes by spreading from the change of a single coefficient? We can do so provided we can find a coefficient fulfilling the following two conditions, first, some possible change in it must produce the series of changes -1, -3, -10, -35, etc in line "Having won I", and second it must affect no coefficients below that line except by affecting those of that line.
