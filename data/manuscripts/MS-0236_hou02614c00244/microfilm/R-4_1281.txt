But if the right triangle whose legs and
hypothenuse are a, b, h, had its area,
\frac{1}{2}ab a square, [del]if[/del] [ins]then[/ins] h^2 and 4(\frac{1}{2}ab)
would be two squares whose sum and
difference were both squares.
h^2 + 4(\frac{1}{2}ab) = (a+b)^2
h^2 - 4(\frac{1}{2}ab) = (a-b)^2
Hence there is no such triangle. It
seems to be quite clear that that is
what Fermat had in mind.

says that the area of a [ins]right[/ins] triangle whose
legs and hypothenuse are whole numbers
cannot be a square; and gives [del]this[/del] as the
reason that if two squares have their
sum and difference both squares, it
would follow that there was another
pair of squares of which the same was
true, the sum of the second pair being
less than that of the first. On this M.
Cantor remarks "Er hat den Beweis jener
Unmöglichkeit in räthselhafter Kürze
angedeutet, dessen Schluss allein ganz
klar und verständlich ist." This seems
to me rather obtuse. Let M^2 and N^2 be
such a pair of squares and assume that
their sum is as small as that of any such
pair. Then if they had any common
divisor, division would give another pair
of squares having the same property and
a smaller sum, contrary to hypothesis.

prime. As such both are not even. Nor can
both be odd, since the sum of an odd square is

smaller even. Let M^2 > N^2 and write
