2[u]nd[/u], Plato, apparently quite unaware that if a thing is absurd,
it can, at once, be proved both to possess and to want every
character, proceeds, at intolerable length, and with the same,
or even worse, triviality, to disprove everything of the one
which he had just proved. This fills fifteen pages (142A—157B),
unrelieved by one glimmer of good sense.

3[u]rd[/u], Equally unaware that from an absurd hypothesis any
consequence whatever may be deduced, without the resort to
flimsy reasons in which he indulges, Plato next proceeds to
show that if the [del]One[/del] [ins]will[/ins] is, other things partake of the One (157C), and
the things which partake of the one [ins]will be[/ins] are innumerable (158B), and
yet limited, and they [ins]will be[/ins] are both like and unlike one another and
themselves (158E), and have contrary attributes of all kinds.

4[u]th[/u], He next shows that from the same hypothesis, the
reverse conclusions follow in regard to the not One.
