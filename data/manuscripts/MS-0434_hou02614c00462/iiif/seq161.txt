How will you manage to investigate without the remotest idea
of what it is that you want to investigate? Or if you [del]chance[/del] [ins]light[/ins]
on what you want, how shall you know that it what you
wanted?" All that Plato can answer to this is that it is captious,
[greek]ὡς ἐριστικὸν λόγον[/greek], that it makes men sluggards, and the like,
and then brings forward his famous doctrine of reminiscence,
as if that met the point. To prove this nonsense, he leads a slave
to perform a little process of mathematical reasoning. He
also notices that it is better not to know and to know one does
not know, than not to know but fancy that one knows.
Also it is better to inquire than idly to think that it is hopeless to
inquire. The last part of the dialogue is intended to prove
that virtue is knowledge because it can be taught; although
it is so presented that some readers have imagined that
it was intended to show that it can be taught if it is
knowledge.
