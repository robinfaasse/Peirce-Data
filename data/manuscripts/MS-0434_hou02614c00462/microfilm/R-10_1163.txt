Why not, asks Socrates, fears can be false? Why not pleasures? Opinions
only, says Protarchus can be false, fears cannot. But he very
needlessly confesses that a person may think he is pleased when
he is not at all so, and vice versa. (36E) The pseudo-Socrates, however,
maintains that to say that pleasures cannot be false is like
insisting that an opinion, because it really is [ins]the[/ins] [del]an[/del] opinion it
conceives itself to be, cannot be false. (37A). Socrates argues that
a picture may be false [ins](38E)[/ins] [del]which[/del] is plainly wrong. Not the picture
but the legend of the picture may be false. On the whole, I do
not think he does justice to his own position. All that he makes
out is that pleasure may be based on error. He now asks
Protarchus to admit that pleasures are only bad in so far as
they are false. (40E). Against this Protarchus protests that pleasures
and pains, if bad, are so in themselves, or their tendencies, but not
as being false (41A). Socrates says that as magnitudes seen from
a distance are inaccurately estimated, so the same thing happens
