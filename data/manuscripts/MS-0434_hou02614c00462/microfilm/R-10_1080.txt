τοῦτο τί ἄν ἡμῖν διαιρετέον εἴη; ἆρ' οὐκ αὐτῶν τούτων οἵτινες
ἄρχουσί τε καὶ ἄρχονται; (412 B.) The elders are to rule; and of
these the wisest and ablest. There must be a selection, from among
the other Defenders, of such men who have shown a lifelong
zeal for the good of the state. They must have been closely watched
to see that no influence has ever been able to swerve them from
that line from that conduct. Book III ends with an elaborate
lie which is to be carefully inculcated. This is quite of a piece
with Plato's endeavour to mark out beforehand just what
the morals of the state are to be. Book IV [del]begins[/del] [ins]opens[/ins] by consider-
ing the advantage of [del]or or[/del] allowing the state to become too rich
or great. No innovations or reforms are to be countenanced.
The matter of religion is to be left to Apollo of Delphi. The
right constitution of state having thus been ordered, we are
now to ask where is the righteousness in the arrangement. (427 D)
