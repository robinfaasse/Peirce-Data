[u]Plato's preference for dichotomy is evident, and plainly,
for the purposes of definition, it is the most economical, in
general. We shall see, however, that he does not absolutely in-
sist upon it.[/u]

The sophist follows a ctetic art, like the angler. They are both
hunters; but the sophist is a terrestrial hunter. [del]Of land[/del] hunting
is either of tame [del]or of[/del] [ins]or of[/ins] wild animals ([greek]Τὸ μὲν τῶν
ἡμέρων, τὸ δὲ τῶν ἀγρίων[/greek] 222 B). Theatetus, the young fellow who
stands by and makes the responses, [del]is little[/del] here has the good
tact to express surprise [del]that[/del] at the idea that tame animals are
hunted. But the Elean says that in case man is to be
reckoned as a tame animal (which I should say very
decidedly he was not[ins],[/ins] for he is under the dominion of
no other animal) then some tame animal is hunted.
It seems a very slouchy method which does not stop to inquire
the meaning of tame and wild. The Greek word for tame, [greek]ἥμερος[/greek],
seems to mean sitting, opposed to [greek]ἄγριος[/greek], wild, roaming the fields.
