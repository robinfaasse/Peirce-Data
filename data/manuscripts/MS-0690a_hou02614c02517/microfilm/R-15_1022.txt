[u]Logic[/u] plainly is of the discovery, of Hippocrates of Chios that certain lunes, or figures bounded by two arcs of circles, were equal to rectilinear figures and capable of being squared; and Aristotle plainly means that this fact justified the hope, which we know was entertained on this ground, that the circle could be squared. [del]Aristotle and[/del] Mathematics was not Aristotle's strong point, and he probably did not clearly understand that it was only two or three special lunes that Hippocrates had squared. [del]He may, therefore, suppose[/del] [ins]It is likely, however,[/ins] that he understood the argument to be the inference of the minor premise of the following syllogism from its other two propositions:

Whatever is [del]equal to a constructible[/del] [ins]constructible[/ins] rectilinear figure is equal to a sum of lunes;
The circle is equal to [del]a[/del] [ins]equal to[/ins] rectilinear figure;
[del]$\therefore$[/del] The circle is [del]equal to[/del] a sum of lunes.

To make this out, we have to change just one word of the text. In place of saying that the major term is [greek]τετραγωνίζεσθαι[/greek] we have to put [greek]ἴσον μηνίσκοις[/greek].
