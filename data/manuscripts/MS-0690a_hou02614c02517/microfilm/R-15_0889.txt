All mathematical reasoning, even although it relates to probability, is of the nature of necessary reasoning. All necessary reasoning [del]is of[/del] consists of tracing out the [del]conseq[/del] what is [del]in[/del] virtually asserted in the assumed premises. While some of these may be new observations, yet the principal ones [del]are propositions[/del] relate to states of things not capable of being directly observed. As has often been said, especially since Kant, such reasoning really does not amplify our positive knowledge, although it may render our understanding of our own assumptions more perfect. It is the kind of reasoning proper for any application of science. For example, it is by such reasoning that, assuming the law of gravitation to have been scientifically established, we go on to predict the time and place of an eclipse of the sun.
