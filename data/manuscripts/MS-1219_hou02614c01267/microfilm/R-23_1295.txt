A protasis has a comma after it; but in other cases, when the sense of the first part of a sentence is not complete without what immediately follows, these parts are not separated by commas. Thus, we do not write in English
He tells me, that he is coming to see me.

This rule leads to another: that when a phrase [del]may[/del] [ins]might[/ins] either be understood as explaining the meaning of what immediately precedes it, or as modifying that meaning, then the comma will show it is to be taken in the latter sense, while the omission will show it is to be taken in the former sense. It is true the omission may be objectionable [del]as[/del] as giving rise to a possible misunderstanding or beginning of a misunderstanding; and in such case, I think a dash maybe used. But I don't know.

If a conjunction or other [del]not[/del] necessary word is omitted, there is an interruption in the flow of meaning; and a comma must be used.

"Who, to the enraptur'd heart and ear and eye,
Teach beauty, virtue, truth, and love, and melody."
Geb. puts commas after "heart" and "ear". But I see no reason for it. After "truth" and "love" commas are required to prevent the reader from understanding that these are taken with the items following them as making one item.
