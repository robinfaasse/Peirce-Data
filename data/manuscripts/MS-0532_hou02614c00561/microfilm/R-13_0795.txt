§2. In all reasoning, we pass from
belief in one proposition, say A, to [del]belief[/del] [ins]belief[/ins]
in another, say B. In demonstrative rea-
soning, we hold that if A is true, B is true.
What should it mean to say that if A is true,
B is true? Every kind of state of things
which we do not know not to be the ac-
tual state of things is said to be [u]possible[/u].
The possible is that which is not known
to be nonexistent, or [del]the[/del] [ins]what[/ins] is either exis-
tent or unknown in respect of existence.
To know that if A is true, B is true, is to
have such knowledge that should we
come to know A to be true, we shall at
once know B is true. That is to say, among
the states of things our knowledge admits,
there is none in which A is true while B
is false, but in every [ins]possible[/ins] case either A is false
or B is true. It may [del]not[/del] be that A is false
