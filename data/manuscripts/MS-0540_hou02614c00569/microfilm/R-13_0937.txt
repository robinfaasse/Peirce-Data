[pn]Syllabus[/pn] [pn]134[/pn] [pn]L 94[/pn] [u]Nomenclature and Divisions of Triadic Relations, so far as they are determined.[/u] [pn]MS 540[/pn]

[del]The principles and analogies of Phenomenology enable[/del] us to describe, in a distant way, what the divisions of triadic relations must be. But until we have met with the different kinds [u]a posteriori[/u], and have [del]to[/del] in that way been led to recognize their importance, the [u]a priori[/u] descriptions mean little;— not nothing at all, but little. Even after we seem to identify the varieties called for [u]a priori[/u] with varieties which [ins]the[/ins] experience of reflexion leads us to think important, no slight labour is required to make sure that the divisions we have found [u]a posteriori[/u] are precisely those that have been predicted [u]a priori[/u]. In most cases, we find that they are not precisely identical, owing to the narrowness of our reflexional experience. It is only after much further arduous analysis that we are able finally to place in the system the conceptions to which experience has led us. In the case of triadic relations, no part of this work has, as yet, been satisfactorily performed, except in some measure for the most important class of triadic relations, those of signs, or representamens, to their objects and interpretants.

Provisionally, we may make a rude division of triadic relations, which, we need not doubt, contains important truth, however imperfectly apprehended, into
Triadic relations of comparison,
Triadic relations of performance, and
Triadic relations of thought.
