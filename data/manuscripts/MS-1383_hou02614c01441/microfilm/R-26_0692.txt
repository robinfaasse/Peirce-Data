On the contrary, if Spencer's theory be correct, — and be it remarked that it does not necessarily suppose the ideas to be due to strict heredity from the mother's womb: they may come by inheritance in the mother's lap, — the inevitable conclusion will be that while inheritance explains the tremendous forcefulness of these beliefs, yet there is no warrant for accepting them as absolutely universal and exact. Consider, for example, the belief that two lines in a plane may be parallel, that is, may never meet, but that, if one of them be turned through ever so small an angle, they will meet. This belief, according to Spencer, is founded on the experience of [del]past[/del] generations. [del](It is implied in the conceptions of direction and of relative motion which the child only acquires.)[/del] If so, there is no reason to suppose it absolutely exact. A Weismannian might argue that the belief was the result of breeding, those who [ins]naturally[/ins] possessed the idea having a certain advantage over others.
