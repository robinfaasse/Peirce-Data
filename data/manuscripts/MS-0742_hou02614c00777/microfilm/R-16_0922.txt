Nor is there an argument without a leading-principle, for if nothing is implied the conclusion is already stated in the premises. But a mere statement is not an argument.

§5. That there is a certain minimum leading principle, that cannot be got rid of may be illustrated as follows. Let a certain argument be A and its conclusion, B. Then we may say that the leading principle is that "If A is true B is true." Take this as an additional premise and the argument becomes —
If A, B
But A
Ergo B.

The leading principle of this plainly is that if two facts are related as reason and consequent and the reason be true the consequent is true. Make another premise of this and the argument becomes —

§3. A [u]valid[/u] argument (opposed to a [u]fallacious[/u] argument or [u]fallacy[/u]) is one whose leading principle is true.

A [u]demonstrative[/u] (opposed to a [u]mere[/u] [del]by[/del] [ins]or[/ins] [u]probable[/u]) argument is one whose leading principle would make every such conclusion true & not merely the greater number of them.
