I use your definition of greater, less, and equal, as applied
to collections, merely stating it in slightly different form.

Namely it is assumed that, given any two collections, the As, or
{A}, and the Bs, or {B}, whatsoever, there is either some relation
r such that every A stands [del]to some[/del] in the relation r to some [ins]every[/ins] B
to which no other A stands in the relation r, or [del]some[/del] B
stands [del]to some A[/del] in the relation r to some A to which no other
B stands in the relation r. This I write thus:
$$\Sigma_r \Pi_i \Sigma_k \Pi_j \Pi_p \Sigma_s \Pi_q (\bar{A}_i + \bar{r}_{ik} + B_k) \cdot (\bar{A}_j + [del]l_{ij}[/del] [ins]r_{ij}[/ins] + \bar{r}_{jk}) + (\bar{B}_p + \bar{r}_{ps} + A_s) \cdot (\bar{B}_q + r_{qs} + \bar{r}_{qs})$$
Then the collection {A} is [u]greater[/u] than the collection {B}
and the collection {B} is [u]less[/u] than the collection {A}
if, and only if, no relation, g, can be found such that every A is in the
relation, g, to a B to which no other A is in the relation g.

If neither of two collections is greater than the other, they are equal.

By the [u]multitude[/u] of a collection, I mean that character of it
by virtue of which it is greater than some collections and less than
others. ([u]Mächtigkeit[/u]). By the [u]arithm[/u] of a collection, I mean that one
of a series of vocables [del]which[/del] [ins]or signs[/ins] which is applied as an adjective to a col-
lection to express its [ins]ordinal[/ins] place in the scale of multitudes. ([u]Cardinalzahl[/u].)

Now the first step toward the result to which I beg leave to [del]dem[/del]
submit to your kind consideration is to show that, no matter how
small or how great a collection [ins]As[/ins] may be, the multitude of the collection
of different possible collections of [del]the[/del] ineunts of {A} is greater than the
multitude of {A}. That is to say, calling the B's the different collec-
tions whose ineunts are ineunts of A, no relation g can be found such
that every [del]A[/del] B is in the relation g to an A to which no other B is
in the relation g. For, given any relation, g, whatever, I will describe
a B which does not stand in the relation g to any A to which no other
B stands in the same relation. Namely, I first divide the As
into four classes, as follows:

1[sup]st[/sup], those (if such there be) to which no B is in the relation g;

2[sup]nd[/sup], those (if such there be) to [del]which[/del] [ins]each of[/ins] which more than one B is in the
relation g;

3[sup]rd[/sup], those (if such there be) each of which is an ineunt of that B which is g
to it, and to no other A;

4[sup]th[/sup], those (if such there be) each of which is an exeunt of the B which alone of the Bs
stands in the relation g to it.
