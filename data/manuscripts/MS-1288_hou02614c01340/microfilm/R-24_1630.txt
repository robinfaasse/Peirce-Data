<u>I.</u>

If we endeavor to form our conceptions upon history
and life, we remark three classes of men. The first con-
sists of those for whom the chief thing is the qualities
of feelings. These men create Art. The second consists of
the practical men, [del]of the[/del] who carry on the business of
the world. They respect nothing but power, and respect
power only so far as it exercised. The third class
consists of men to whom nothing seems great but Reason.
If force interests them, it is not in its exertion, but in that
it has a reason and a law. For men of the first class, na-
ture is a picture; for men of the second class it is an oppor-
tunity; for men of the third class it is a cosmos, so
admirable, that to penetrate to its ways seems to them
the only thing that makes life worth living. These are the
