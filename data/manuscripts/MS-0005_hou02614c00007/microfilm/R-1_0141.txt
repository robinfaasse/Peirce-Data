[u]Dichotomic Mathematics[/u].
By C. S. Peirce.

[u]Hypothesis I[/u]. We are supposed to be provided with a multitude of things called [u]symbols[/u].
Some of these symbols are transformable (permissively) into others.
All transformations that are permissible are of two kinds, the insertions and the omissions.
If two symbols are each permissively transformable into the other, they are said to be [u]the same symbol[/u], of which each a [u]replica[/u].
The symbols are of three classes, which may be distinguished as the [u]singular[/u], the [u]vague[/u], and the [u]general[/u].
Each replica of a singular symbol is capable, whether permissively or [del]not[/del] [ins]by[/ins], of being [del]attached[/del] joined to another replica of any kind of symbol, which may, [ins]1[/ins] be of either class.
