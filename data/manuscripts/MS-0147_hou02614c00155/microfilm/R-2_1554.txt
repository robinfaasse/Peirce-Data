[u]Chapter I. Unidimensional.[/u]

Art 1. [del]Of the[/del] [ins]Of[/ins] [del]general[/del] [ins]general[/ins] [u]logical notation[/u]. A [ins]lower case Italic[/ins] letter on the line has the power of a verb: it asserts something. Its subjects, — among which are to be counted its direct and indirect objects, — are denoted by subscript letters, attached to it. These letters [del]are[/del] usually have the power of relative pronouns, but sometimes of demonstrative pronouns or proper nouns. In other words, the subscript letters are similar to the letters on a geometrical diagram. Thus, $l_{\text{ABCD}}$ will [del]signify[/del] [ins]mean[/ins] the assertion that the points A, B, C, D are arranged on a self-returning line in this order, this statement being understood to include the possibility that one [ins]or more of the equations[/ins] [del]either[/del] $A=B$, [del]or[/del] $B=C$, [del]or[/del] $C=D$, or $D=A$, [ins]are[/ins] [del]is[/del] true.

The sign $\prec$, which is written, [ins]most readily,[/ins] by making first the lower line $\sim$ from left to right, and then the upper attachment from left to right, and is pronounced "supposed", means that either the assertion written before it is false or that the assertion written after it is true [ins](or both)[/ins]. The whole expression $\Gamma \prec \Delta$ is called a "consequence [u]ut nunc[/u]." Its properties are discussed by Duns Scotus (I Anal. pr. q. ix.)
