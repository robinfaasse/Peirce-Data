Prof. W. Watson of the Royal College of Science in London has
produced "A Text-Book of Physics" (Longmans) which by
patient explanations of matters of difficulty sets the principles
of the subject in as clear a light as a one-volume treatise
could well do. We notice in it a good many recent things
which probably [ins]there[/ins] appear in a textbook for the first time. In his
896 pages of fine print the author might have found room
for more complete tables of constants and for references to
the most important memoirs. No part of it strikes us as
at all masterly. It is not the ideal treatise that we still
await from some man with a [del]turn[/del] [ins]gift[/ins] for making such a work.
Occasionally, we meet with surprising statements by Mr.
Watson, as when he tells us that "Thales, who lived about the
commencement of the Christian era, discovered that amber
when rubbed acquires the property of attracting light bodies."
Some German higher critic may think that the report of Di-
ogenes Laertius [del]referred[/del] refers to some late Thales. But if
so, this late Thales did not discover a property mentioned
by Plato in that passage of the Timaeus in which he anti-
cipates Le Sage's theory of attraction. No critic since
Schelling has [del]attacked[/del] [ins]impugned[/ins] the authenticity of the Timaeus.
To say that Thales lived about the commencement of the
Christian era is like saying that Roger Bacon was pro-
fessor of physics in the Royal College of Science.
