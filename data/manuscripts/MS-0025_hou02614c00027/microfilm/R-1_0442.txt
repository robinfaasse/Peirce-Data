No two different $M$s are $g$ to the same $N$. For if an $M$ is not $r$ to some $N$, it is not necessitated by the definition to be $g$ to any $N$, and therefore is not $g$ to any $N$. If an $M$ is $r$ of an $N$, it is the sole $g$ of that $N$; for no $N$ is $r$'d by two different things, so that if it is $r$'d by an $M$ it is not $r$'d by any $N$ and therefore is not $g$'d by anything except the $M$ that is $r$ to it. If an $M$ is $g$ of an $N$ but not the sole $g$ of that $N$, it is not $r$ to that $N$, and is $g$ of the $N$ that is $r$ of the former $N$. But this $N$ that is $r$ of an $N$ is sole $r$ of that $N$. Hence, if an $M$ is $g$ of an $N$, but not the sole $g$ of that $N$, it is $g$ but not the sole $g$ of [ins]another[/ins] [del]an[/del] $N$ that is $r$ of that $N$. Hence, there would be a denumerable multitude of $N$s of which that $M$ was $g$. But since all the $N$s are enumerable, so is every part of the whole collection of $N$s; and therefore it is absurd to suppose an $M$ is $g$ of an $N$ but not the sole $g$ of that $N$. In like manner, no two different $N$s are $g$ to the same $M$.
