[u]Multitude[/u]

being nothing but a simple algebraical corollary from [del]the[/del] [ins]first[/ins] principles
of geometry. But as Euclid goes to work upon it, it seems like an import-
ant step. He bisects BC at E, draws the straight line AE, produces it and
takes F upon it so that EF shall be equal to AE. Then joining CF, he shows that
the two triangles ABE and FCE, have [del]two[/del] sides and the included angle of the
one equal to those of the other, so that the other parts are equal, and the angle
ECF equals the angle B. But $\angle$ ECF is a part of BCD, and hence the angle B is
less than the angle BCD. One naturally gets the idea from the style of proof
and from the theorem apparently being a step toward a demonstration that the
sum of the interior angles of a triangle are not less than 180°, that the theorem is
a vital one. But Euclid's labored proof is highly defective, because he never
considers the possibility of F falling outside the triangle near A, nor the
possibility of its falling inside the triangle. Had he made it a postulate that a
line can be produced so as to be infinitely long, instead of merely postulating
he would have avoided this.
