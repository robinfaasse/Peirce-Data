To say that [ins]any[/ins] [del]the[/del] exterior angle exceeds either [del]non-adjacent[/del] interior angle is merely to say that the sum of any two interior angles is less than $180^{\circ}$. Let us put $x$ for the (positive or negative) amount by which $A+B$ falls short of $180^{\circ}$, $y$ the amount by which $A+C$ falls short of $180^{\circ}$, and $z$ the amount by which $B+C$ falls short of $180^{\circ}$. Let us also suppose $A < B < C$. Then $x > y > z$. If therefore $z > 0$, it is true of the first triangle that all three pairs of interior angles exceed $180^{\circ}$. But if it is not true of this triangle it is true of the second triangle; for then $z < 0$, and the sums of pairs of interior angles of the second triangle are
$$\begin{matrix}
A + 180^{\circ}-B = 180^{\circ}-y+z < 180^{\circ} \\
A + 180^{\circ}-C = 180^{\circ}-x+z < 180^{\circ} \\
180^{\circ}-B + 180^{\circ}-C = 180^{\circ}+z < 180^{\circ}.
\end{matrix}$$

Of one of the triangles, then, it is true that the sum of any pair of interior angles is [del]less than (or equal to)[/del] [ins]at greatest, as small as[/ins] $180^{\circ}$. [del]We will neglect the limiting value throughout for simplicity.[/del] Let this be the triangle having $A, B, C$ as its angles. Then $z > 0$. Hence, the second triangle has the sums of two pairs less and that of one pair greater than $180^{\circ}$; the third triangle has the sums of one pair less [del]and those[/del] of two pairs greater than $180^{\circ}$; while, the fourth triangle has the sums of all pairs greater than $180^{\circ}$.
