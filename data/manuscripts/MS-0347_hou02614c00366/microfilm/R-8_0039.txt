The next [del]point[/del] [ins]step[/ins] is to ascertain which type of inference rests on a
ground which relates to things, which to symbols, and which to forms. Let
us take up the three types separately. The ground of inference _à priori_
must be established _à priori_; that is by reasoning from determinant
to determinate, or in other words by reasoning from definition. But
this kind of reasoning can only be applied to an object whose character-
istics depend upon its definition. [del]Of[/del] Of most objects the definition de-
pends upon the character and must itself be [del]formed[/del] [ins]rest on[/ins] some inference
of a weaker kind; but the principle of deduction must rest on nothing
but deduction, and therefore it must relate to something whose character is
determined by its definition. Now the only objects of which this can be
said are symbols; they indeed are created by their definition, which
neither forms nor things are; hence the principle of inference _à priori_ must
relate to symbols. The principle of inference _à posteriori_ must be established
_à posteriori_, that is by reasoning from determinate to determinant. Now
it is clear that this kind of reasoning is applicable only to that which
is determined by that which it determines; [del]or[/del] to that which is only
subject to the truth of its determinant and is of itself pure zero. Now
this is the case with nothing whatever except the pure forms; [del]of them[/del]
it is [del]true that[/del] though they determine the qualities of things and the
meanings of symbols, [del]yet that[/del] a form which does not determine anything,
either in a symbol or a thing, is nothing at all. In fact, [ins]a pure form[/ins] is the [del]the[/del]
[del]essence[/del] [ins]essence[/ins] of a valid hypothesis: Hence the principle of inference _à posteriori_
