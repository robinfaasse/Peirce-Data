[u]Demonstration[/u]. For suppose there were another element. Then, being an element, it must be indecomposable by logical analysis; and being an element of [u]form[/u], it [del]must[/del] cannot be distinguished by any material peculiarities. For mind you, I do not say that these three are the sole categories, but only that they are the sole [u]formal elements[/u] of thought. If then there were a fourth formal element, it must either (1) have infinite valency, or (2) have a higher finite valency than trivalency, or (3) have [u]nilvalency[/u], or (4) there must be two formal elements of the same valency.

In [del]the first[/del] alternative No. I, the formal element assumed must have [del]and[/del] a valency of the order of multitudinousness of some one of the endless series of infinite multitudes, which series begins with the denumeral multitude, (or multitude equal to that of the positive integers), and every multitude, [ins]M,[/ins] of the series is followed by an abnumeral multitude of an order higher by one than the abnumerability of the multitude M itself, the denumeral multitude being the multitude of abnumerability zero; and this relation of the multitude of abnumerability X+1 to the multitude of abnumerability X is that the former is the multitude of the collection of all possible collections composed exclusively of individuals of a collection of abnumerability X.
