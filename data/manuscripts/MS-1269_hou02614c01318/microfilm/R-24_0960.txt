THE HISTORY OF SCIENCE.
By C. S. Peirce.

[u]Introduction[/u].

Lord Bacon remarks that "the Sciences, as we now have them, are nothing but certain orderly arrangements of things previously discovered, not methods of discovery, or schemes for obtaining new results."* This is a fair anticipation of the contrast between the way in which an active Science appears to any devotee of it and to one who has a general reader's and a news-devourer's interest in the [del]most[/del] [ins]more[/ins] striking of the discoveries that are from time to time announced. The latter person, if he happens to be a lexicographer, will insist upon defining science as an "organized body of knowledge"; while [del]to[/del] [ins]for[/ins] the former science is a mode of life, like the [del]profession of priest, or practising physician, or active politician, and[/del] that which distinguishes the life of science, in the eyes of the scientific man, is not the [u]attainment[/u] of knowledge, but a single-minded absorption in the [u]search for it for its own sake[/u], — a singlemindedness that forgets every theory the moment the facts of observation appear against it.
