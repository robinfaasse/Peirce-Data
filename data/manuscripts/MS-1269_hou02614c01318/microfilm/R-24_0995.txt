Another objection to the hypothesis of MM. Revillout is that to suppose the papyrus to be a copy by a scribe quite ignorant of arithmetic from the blotter of a bad scholar is quite to reverse its real characteristics. Taken as a whole, it is not only accurate, but shows careful attention to such details of the computers art as the clear distinction [ins]between different[/ins] [del]of[/del] columns of figures. It is necessary to rate both the copyist and the original writer as good arithmeticians. The errors of calculation (if such they are and not intentional falsifications intended to keep the mind of the scholar on the alert, which would be quite in the Egyptian style) are not numerous. Even in our day the best professional computers do commit arithmetical mistakes and the tables of logarithms have only been freed from errors by the [del]reward[/del] offer of a gold piece for every one found. [ins]The very calculating machines sometimes go wrong.[/ins] If, in the infancy of the art of calculating, the errors [del]are[/del] [ins]were[/ins] somewhat more frequent, why what is there to surprise us in that?

Another objection to the theory is, that, to a person having an intimate acquaintance with school arithmetics of all ages, the style of this work, — the occasional errors included, — is just what would be expected in a primitive text-book. The formal statement of rules is quite a modern abomination; it does not help the scholar to learn arithmetic, but draws his attention away to another object. The full working out of the first example under each rule has always been customary in school-books.
