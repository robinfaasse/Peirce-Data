The Rhind Papyrus answers this question by showing that
he always performed multiplication by [ins]successive[/ins] doublings and
adding. Thus, in order to multiply 5 by 7 he might
proceed as follows:
Once - - - - - - - - - - - - = 5
Twice - - - - - - - - - - - - = 10
Four times - - - - - - - - - = 20
[u]Seven times = Four times plus twice, plus once = 35.[/u]
It is evident, then, that to him multiplication was simply such
an operation that [del]of the double[/del] [ins]of any[/ins] multiplier would double the product,
and the sum of two multipliers would add the two products. We
see that to him the operation of doubling was something apart
from multiplication, generally. It is a significant fact, in
this connection, that all the old writers on our Vulgar
arithmetic, beginning with Muhammed ben Mûsâ, the
Khorasmian, in the IXth century A.D., treat doubling, or "dupla-
tion", as an operation distinct from multiplication. There
was no sense in their doing so; for they doubled just as they
multiplied by 3 or by 7, as we do. But it was doubtless a
tradition derived from earlier ages when the multipli-
cation was performed in the Egyptian fashion.
