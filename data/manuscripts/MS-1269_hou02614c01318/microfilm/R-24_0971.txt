The Egyptian showed an excessive reverence for antiquity, [ins]such as [del]we see[/del] [ins]perhaps[/ins] [ins]exists[/ins][/ins] throughout the East today. Were not the Pyramids and still older Sphinx before his eyes to prove that, back in the densest mists of time, men were wiser and abler than in his own age? So convinced was he of this, that a skillful author would hardly fail to recommend the book which might [del]embody[/del] [ins]have embodied[/ins] his own discoveries, had he made any, by pretending that it was copied from a manuscript found among the relics of some famous monarch of by-gone times. This readiness to accept the wisdom of the ancients shows how little impulse there was toward independent investigation; while it betrays the decadence of the intellect of the Egyptian race.

But notwithstanding his worship of the ancients, concerning history as such the Egyptian had little curiosity. A king would cover his monument with accounts of his prowess and dignity; or, if these themes failed him, he would show how he proposed to occupy himself in the world to come.
