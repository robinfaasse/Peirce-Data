We now begin to penetrate a little into the Egyptian idea of a
fraction. But there are other points to be considered. The Egyptian
was, of course, perfectly aware that if a circular cake be cut into
five equal sectors, each of those sectors will be equal to $\frac{1}{5}$
of the whole. But two sectors can [ins]obviously[/ins] [del]be[/del] aggregated into a pair. How
is it, then, that the Egyptian idea is that $\frac{1}{5}$ cannot be added to
itself? Plainly, it is because arithmetic does not deal with
sectors of a pie, but with abstract numbers. Late in the eighth
century B.C. the Greek Pythagoras spent many years in Egypt
in study. From this and other travels he returned full of arithmetic.
Now the Pythagoreans always call [del]two[/del] [ins]two[/ins] "the dyad," four "the tetrad",
five "the pentad," and so with the other numbers. That must
have been the mode of thought of the Egyptian arithmetician; and
he must have extended it to fractions.
