[u]Chapter I. EGYPT AND SCIENCE.[/u]

In high antiquity, three countries only, so far as we know,
Babylonia, Egypt, and Northern China, attained to states of civiliza-
tion in which science could at all be looked for. All [del]these three[/del]
countries were annually flooded by the rivers that traversed them,
while their agriculture depended upon irrigation. Now in order
to carry on successful irrigation, a strong government is needed;
and [del]finally,[/del] [ins]the[/ins] natural course of events tends to develope such a govern-
ment. Besides, where occasional inevitable derangements of
the irrigation system, due to the absence of a strong government,
will produce famines, the man who had at such times always
stored large quantities of grain, would, after a succession of
such events, find himself in a position to enslave the whole
population, and make himself king. But a king ruling over a
people which, [ins]in a tropical country,[/ins] during a considerable part of the year [ins]with nothing growing to eat,[/ins] was reduced
to agricultural idleness, [del]would[/del] [ins]then[/ins] be obliged to feed [del]his people[/del] during
that season, and, by way of keeping up order and discipline, he would
be forced to find [del]them[/del] some work [ins]for them[/ins] to do. Under such circumstances,
great operations of engineering, [ins]which had begun[/ins] beginning with the irrigation system,
would necessarily be carried further. When a civilization had reached
this stage, we might hope to find the birth of science; for engineering
must at least have trained men to exactitude and definiteness of con-
ception.
