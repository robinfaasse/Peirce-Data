* MM. Revillout compare the Egyptian mode of expression to the reduction of fractions to a common denominator. Had the Egyptians written 2/15 in the form [del]1/[/del][ins]1/[/ins]7½, the comparison, without being just, would have been intelligible. But in fact the Egyptians reduced nothing. Bringing fractions to a common denominator makes the equivalence of different forms of sums manifest; but nobody can tell without calculation whether 1/8 + 1/120, 1/9 + 1/45, 1/10 + 1/30, are equal or not. The reduction of fractions to a common denominator facilitates computation; the Egyptian system only renders it difficult. In short, the suggested comparison only puts in a strong light the ineptitude of the Egyptian arithmetic.

[u]Text resumed[/u].

Further evidence appears from their making an exception of the fraction 2/3, which they wrote with a single character, which in hieroglyphics was [fig]hieroglyph for 2/3[/fig], meaning 1/1½. To a mathematical mind there are no exceptions in things mathematical. An apparent exception is, for him, but the signal for an act of generalization. The Egyptian, however, never generalized; he lacked wings to lift him from the [u]terra firma[/u] of fact.

It is not easy to [del]comprehend[/del] [ins]understand[/ins] the Egyptian conception of fractions. It is never easy to apprehend the unclearness of a confused idea. One thing, however is manifest, namely, since the Egyptians permitted different fractions to be added together, but no fraction to be added to itself, he must have conceived of addition as an aggregation of objects. The fact that nothing can be put along with itself to make a heap is the only way of explaining how it appeared to the Egyptian mind impossible to add a fraction to itself.
