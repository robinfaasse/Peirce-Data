We have here two coefficients $\frac{0}{0}$ & $\frac{1}{0}$ which we have not yet interpreted. To find what $\frac{0}{0}$ means let it equal to $x$
$\frac{0}{0} = x$ multiply by zero & we have $0 \times x = 0$. Now this is true whatever be the value of $x$. $\frac{0}{0}$ therefore is a wholly indeterminate class & means [del]some[/del], all, some or none. $\frac{0}{0}(1-a)b$ means some all or none of $b$ which is not $a$.

To find what $\frac{(1-a)(1-b)}{0}$ means we must [del]consider[/del] [ins]remember[/ins] that when one letter is divided by another as $\frac{x}{y}$ it must be that the dividend contains the divisor as a factor. Now to say that $(1-a)(1-b)$ contains zero as a factor is to say, not merely that $(1-a)(1-b)$ does not enter into the meaning of [del]$\frac{1-(a-b)^2}{a}$[/del] but that it enters into the meaning of nothing & does not exist. This same result may also be obtained thus. Let $\frac{(1-a)(1-b)}{0} = y$ then multiplying by zero $(1-a)(1-b) = 0$ while the value of $y$ is wholly indeterminate [del]except that it is no class at all.[/del]

Such being the meaning of $\frac{0}{0}$ & $\frac{1}{0}$. I pass to the second fundamental theorem which enables us to [del]eliminate[/del] [ins]sphheresy[/ins] [del]any[/del] [ins]a[/ins] letter from [del]an expression[/del] [ins]equation[/ins]. Let $fx$ be an [del]expression[/del] [ins]equation[/ins] from which we wish to eliminate $x$. As $x$ is either $=1$ or $=0$ we have
Either $f(1) = 0$ or $f(0) = 0$ In any case $f(1)f(0) = 0$

When $b=0$ it becomes $c=0$ In any case then $ac=0$
