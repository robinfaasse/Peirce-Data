My dear Prof. Dewey:

I promise, if I can manage it, to get some notice of the work of your Logical School in the Nation. But, as I don't write the editorials, I write about it as is necessary to dilate on the result has not made itself for serious students. I will therefore write to them with the utmost openness, reduced from mind. I am which leaves us the advantage of [del]not[/del] lying under your [ins]a sort of maternity[/ins] thoroughly implacable [del]your[/del] efforts to get them on their own legs.

As to your style of reasoning, about reasoning, has to my mind the fault of reasoning about reasoning, in this subject, as to seem to think that no reasoning can be too loose, that does not use the [del]syllogistic[/del] [ins]mathematical[/ins] arguments as they themselves would not dream of using in any other branch of science. You seem to substitute for the Normative Science, which in my judgment is the greatest need of our age, a "Natural History of Thought" or "experientialism." For, talk from me to do anything to hinder a man's finding out whatever kind of truth he is on the way to find, [del]gent.[/del] But I do not think anything like a natural history can answer the terrible need there is of checking the awful waste of thought, [del]of[/del] [ins]of energy, going[/ins] on, in consequence of men's not understanding the theory of inference. Though you use the expression "Natural History," yet of the two branches of Natural History, physiology and anatomy, which are as sharply sundered today as they ever were, you seem to be attending only to the latter, since you speak of its being revolutionized by conceptions of evolution. Now the doctrine of evolution has not affected physiology either much or little, unless by lending a competing interest to anatomy, [marginalia]The idea that two such elements as Evolution and Function can in the same sense depend upon each other is to me a very strange one.[/marginalia] as Moral Conduct is Self-controlled Conduct, so Logical Thought is Moral, or Self-controlled, Thought. The Germans have always been in favor of giving thought the rein. What is taught in German Universities keeps only the fashion of the day. No doubt a slow evolutionary process will gradually bring them round to the truth. But, this is the very path we have applied to thought. It involves unspeakable waste.

Although I am strongly in favor of your Pragmatism, I find the whole volume penetrated with this spirit of intellectual licentiousness that does not see that anything is so very false. Of course, you will understand that I should not write in such undecorated terms to any man with whom I did not feel a very deep respect and sympathy. I am simply [del]reco[/del] projecting upon the horizon, where its magnitude is definite, the direction of your standpoint as viewed from mine.

There are three sciences according to me to which Logic must appeal for principles, because they do not depend upon Logic. They are Mathematics, Phenomenology, and Ethics. There are three sciences which logicians often make appeal to, by arguments which would be circular if they rose to the degree of correctness necessary to their kind of fallacy. They are Metaphysical Philosophy, Psychology, Linguistics (of which they barely know that of the Aryan Languages, and ignore that which is most instructive, such as the American and Melanesian), History, etc.

Very faithfully,
C. S. Peirce
