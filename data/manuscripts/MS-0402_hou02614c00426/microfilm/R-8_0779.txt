[u]The Association of Ideas.[/u]

In the absence of external impressions, thoughts dance through the mind, each one gently leading in another, like a train of Bacchants on a Grecian vase. After awhile, the clear train of thought is broken, the ideas remain scattered for a time, and then reconcentrate in another train. Many psychologists hold that this Law of the Association of Ideas as strictly necessitates [del]just[/del] what idea shall follow a given idea, [ins]in a mind of a given experience[/ins] as the laws of mechanics necessitate the precise orbit of a planet. Perhaps this theory cannot be disproved, but I am sure the [ins]no observed[/ins] facts will not warrant such a [del]conclusion[/del].

What is, at any rate, far more characteristic of the force of association is its gentleness. It resembles less a mechanical action, than that of heredity, which [del]gives[/del] [ins]leaves[/ins] room for sporting; and just as [del]biological[/del] [ins]the[/ins] development [del]of species[/del] would be impossible without individual variations in spite of hereditary influence, so the development of thought would come to nothing without the element of spontaneous originality.*
