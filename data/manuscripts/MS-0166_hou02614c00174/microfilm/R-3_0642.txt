[u]Art. 60. Definition.[/u] A set of objects is said to be arranged in a [u]ring[/u], when [ins]all are connected together, mediately or immediately, but so that[/ins] taking any tetrad or set of four of them, each one of the tetrad has another opposite to it in [ins]the[/ins] tetrad; so that if two of the tetrad [u]not[/u] opposite were taken away [del]and all their connections broken[/del] [ins]severed[/ins], the other two would still remain connected, but if two opposites were taken away and [del]all their connections broken[/del] [ins]severed[/ins], the other two would be disconnected.

[fig]A circular arrangement of the alphabet letters from A to Z in a ring.[/fig] [u]Illustration[/u]. Thus if A is joined to Z the letters of the alphabet are arranged in a ring; for taking any four as E, I, R, V, we find E and R opposites, and I and V opposites; so that if the ring were cut at I and R, the series VWXYZABCDE would still join V to E; [del]but[/del] [ins]while[/ins] if the ring were broken at E and R, I and V would be quite disconnected, and so in the other cases.

[u]Theorem[/u]. [u]The rational numbers are, in the order of their values, arranged in a ring.[/u]
