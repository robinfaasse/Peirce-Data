[u]Art. 25.[/u] Certain general propositions concerning the motions of fluids are of a purely topical kind. If a fluid is incompressible metrical considerations come in at the outset. But if [del]its[/del] it is capable of compression and expansion. The assumption which mathematicians usually make is that the fluid and its motions are continuous in such a sense that it cannot have two different motions at the same place. If [del]it[/del] [ins]it[/ins] be asked upon what physical evidence this is founded, the reply is, that if the fluid coheres [del]&[/del] ever so little it should prevent all slipping [del]off[/del] [ins]of[/ins] it over itself, and even if it did not cohere it is difficult to imagine what force could make two different motions at the same place. Still, there are reasons for thinking that, in fact, [del]that[/del] such motions do take place, but not to a sufficient extent to falsify the general outlines of the description of their motions based on the assumption of perfect continuity. It must be remembered, that after all, fluids are not really continuous, but consist of molecules; but in a general way, they move as if they were continuous.

The first consequence of this is that the outside parts of a fluid are always outside parts; and parts that are contiguous are always contiguous. For example, according to this, if we could mark a portion of water lying contiguous to a boat in a river, we should see it elongate and stretch out till it reaches the sea; but it never would entirely leave the boat.
