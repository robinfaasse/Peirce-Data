[u]Art. 29[/u]. Axiom 6. (The Associative Principle of Addition.)
No matter what the values of X, Y, Z
(X + Y) + Z = (X + Y) + Z.

Axiom 7. (The Commutative Principle of Addition.) No matter
what the values of X and Y
X + Y = Y + X.

Axiom 8. No matter what the value of X
0 + X = X.

Axiom 9. No matter what [ins]whole[/ins] number X may be, X + 1 is the
[del]whole sum[/del] [ins]whole[/ins] number next greater than X.

[u]Scholium 10[/u]. It would be easy to prove the above axioms
from an exact definition of addition; but as the pupil is supposed
to understand them perfectly, it is thought preferable to assume them
as unquestionable truths, without stopping to discuss them.

[u]Art. 30[/u]. [u]Scholium 11[/u]. As long as numbers are only used
to count enumerable collections, the system of whole numbers
is all that is required. But [ins]when[/ins] numbers are used to distinguish
objects in a sequence without beginning, [del]as[/del] [ins]as[/ins] well as without end,
it becomes convenient to enlarge the system of numbers. Take, for
example, the reckoning of years. Our reckoning is excellent for
dates subsequent to the era from which we count; but it is so
awkward before that that [del]astronomers do not commonly[/del] [ins]refuse to[/ins] use it.
