[u]§5. Analysis of the Proposition[/u].

[del]A[/del] [ins]Every[/ins] proposition has two parts, that part which conveys the idea, the predicate, and that part which indicates what it is to which this idea is said to be applicable, the subject. These might be termed the internal element and the external element; because the predicate, conveying a mere idea, might be [del]understood[/del] [ins]dreamed[/ins] by one who had been born and [del]reared[/del] [ins]kept[/ins] on a desert island, while [del]the subject[/del] [ins]it is only by[/ins] actual continuity of experience that anybody can know what is intended by the subject. The predicate [del]refers[/del] [ins]appeals[/ins] to [ins]the hearer's [del]almost[/del][/ins] store of internal experience, the subject to his store of external experience. Suppose I say, "There is a pretty house." Everybody can imagine a pretty house; but the [u]there[/u] is quite unintelligible to one who is not in a situation to join his experience to mine.

We have [del]been[/del] forced to draw this distinction by the developments of the last section.
