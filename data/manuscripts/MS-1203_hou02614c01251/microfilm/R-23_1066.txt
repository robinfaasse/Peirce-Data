Of words in which the termination [u]-esque[/u] is affixed to an English stem, the most familiar is [u]picturesque[/u]. (French, [u]pittoresque[/u]; Italian, [u]pittoresco[/u].)

The spelling [u]check[/u] is now preferred to [u]cheque[/u], for an order on a bank; and [u]checker[/u] to [u]chequer[/u]. So [u]cue[/u], not [u]queue[/u]. [u]Quif[/u] for [u]coif[/u] never had any justification, since the French is [u]coiffe[/u] or [u]coif[/u], from Latin [u]cufia[/u], [u]cufea[/u], from German [del]kopfe[/del] [ins]Kopf[/ins]. The same may be said of [u]Quoin[/u] for [u]coin[/u], French [u]coin[/u] from Latin [u]cuneus[/u]. Nor is any reason known for spelling [u]coit[/u] with a [u]qu[/u], although this is still adhered to in England. The word [u]fakir[/u] is spelled [u]faquir[/u] by some good proof-readers. This suggests the accent that some speakers place upon the last syllable; and also the letter Qof of the Arabic. But the best usage inclines to [u]fakir[/u]. American [del]newsp[/del] [ins]reporters[/ins] for the one cent newspapers sometimes confuse this word with [u]faker[/u]. The spelling [u]licorice[/u] is preferable to [u]liquorice[/u], since the [del]latter[/del] [ins]from which it[/ins] suggests [u]liquor[/u], though the Old French was [u]liquerice[/u] (the Modern French [u]réglisse[/u] is probably not a direct descendant) from Latin [u]liquiritia[/u]. The original word, still used in prescriptions is [u]glycyrrhiza[/u], the Greek for "sweet root." If usage permitted, [u]liquerice[/u] would not be a bad spelling. No o-sound is heard in it. The spelling [u]masque[/u], in the sense of [u]masquerade[/u], has the same convenience as [u]barque[/u]. [u]Mask[/u] and [u]masque[/u], [u]bark[/u] and [u]barque[/u], represent the first beginnings of differentiation.
