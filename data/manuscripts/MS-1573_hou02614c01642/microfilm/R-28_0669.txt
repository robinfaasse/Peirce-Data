His chief work is "An Essay concerning
Humane understanding," bearing date [date]1687[/date].
He there takes the position that the theory of cogni-
tion [del]is the[/del] must be the basis of philosophy, and
devotes his book to an inquiry "into the original,
certainty, and extent of human knowledge, together
with the grounds and degrees of belief, opinion,
and assent." He denies the existence of innate
ideas, and maintains that all knowledge is
derived from experience, namely from sensation,
the external, and reflection, the internal ex-
perience. The ideas thence derived are either
simple or complex. Simple ideas of sensation are
either of one sense, as colors, sounds, solidity or
hardness, or of several senses, to wit, space,
figure, rest, and motion. The simple ideas of
reflection are the idea of thinking or perception
and that of willing. There are besides some
simple ideas which come both from [del]of[/del] [ins]sensation[/ins]
and reflection, namely, pleasure, pain,
power, existence, [ins]succession, time,[/ins] and unity. The qualities of
things are either primary, secondary, or tertiary.
