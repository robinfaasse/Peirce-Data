§2. A sign must, from the nature of it, be applicable to
different objects, supposing there happen to [del]be[/del] [ins]exist[/ins] any
such objects. This is [ins]may[/ins] true even of a proper name. [del]Phillip[/del] [ins]Phillip[/ins] of
Macedon [del]stands[/del] [ins]stands[/ins] for Phillip drunk, [del]and[/del] [ins]or[/ins] for Phillip sober, or
for the collective Phillip. But, from a special point of view, a
sign may be regarded as proper to a single object. If a sign is
apt to represent many things, the option as to what single
thing it shall be taken to represent may be reserved by the
utterer of it, to whom it naturally belongs; in which case
it may be said to be used [u]vaguely[/u], or not definitely. The
utterer may, however, transfer this option to the interpreter;
in which case the sign may be said to be used [u]generally[/u], or
not individually. Obviously, the option cannot, in the same
respect, at once lie with both parties. Hence, a sign cannot
