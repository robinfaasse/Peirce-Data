Lecture, I.

[del]a subject which has been discussed[/del] [ins]the definitions of logic[/ins] with ability for two thousand years and more have been very various. They may however be divided into two classes; those which do not and those which do give to logic a psychological or human character.

Of the unpsychological views there are several that occur to me as interesting. In the first place, there is the definition attributed [del]somewhat[/del] [ins]rather[/ins] doubtfully to Aristotle that Logic is the science of Demonstration. Spalding in the Encyclopedia Britannica declares that it is the "theory of inference" which comes to the same thing. But apart from the narrowness of this view in identifying Logic with Syllogistic, these are mere word-definitions since they do not explain the nature of inference or demonstration. [ins]St. Augustine[/ins] calls it the science of truth, and [del]at[/del] [ins]several writers of the rennaissance (Peter Molineux, Vossius) and[/ins] least one [ins]only[/ins] modern [del]writer[/del] Reimarus ([date]1790[/date]) have advocated this definition. There is great merit in this view, but it is too broad; for logic does not consider how an object or idea may be presented but only how it may be represented; [del]e.g.[/del] sight that is to say, and inspiration are both beyond the province of logic. To [del]Thomas[/del] Aquinas, has been attributed a very curious definition, "the art of second notions or of second notions as applied to first;" but Aquinas only [ins]remarks[/ins] [del]says[/del] that Logic is more taken up with second notions than anything.
