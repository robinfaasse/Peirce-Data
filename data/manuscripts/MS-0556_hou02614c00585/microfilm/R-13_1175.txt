logical compound of two or more statements is the state-
ment which is true if all are true and false if any of them
is false. Thus, the disjunctive conjunction, "or," signifies
aggregation; the conjunctive conjunction, "and," signifies
composition. It is usual and convenient to write + for "or,"
and a sign of multiplication for "and."

The relations of these three signs for negation,
aggregation, and composition, involve certain simple rules,
the rules of the improved Buolian algebra, upon which there
are such excellent treatises* that I need only refer the
reader to them.

By means of leashes and the leashing of leashes, we
can express any relation between any definite and finite
number of members. But in regard to indefinite numbers it is
not so. Here we must generalize. We have to consider the
order of the world. We no longer say, John Doe does so and so,
but "every man", "one man in so many, on the average, as they
come along in a certain range of experience," or "Some man,"
does this thing. We thus find use for pro-demonstratives;
which we see are not mere devices for the "economy of thought,"
as the shallow nominalistic logic of Locke and others will
have them to be, but, on the contrary, serve to represent a
