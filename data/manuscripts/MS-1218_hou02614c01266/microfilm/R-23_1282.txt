I am not sure that Rule I is not more a matter of sense and less of the positions of words than he makes it to be. I am decidedly of opinion that this is the case with Rule II. His version is as follows:

Rule II.
Two words, of the same part of speech and in the same construction, if used without a conjunction between them, are separated from each other by a comma.

I would word it thus:
Two parts of one sentence in precisely the same relation of signification to the rest [del]and separated by a disjunctive or arithmetic conjunction[/del] [ins]conjunction (which is however understood)[/ins] must be separated by a comma, at least.

The rule appears to be without real exception in either version; but it has a number of apparent exceptions of much interest owing to their illustrating what fine distinctions of meaning can be expressed by punctuation. Thus, take the difference between "A beautiful white horse" and "A beautiful, white horse." In the former "beautiful" qualifies "white horse"; and it seems implied that its being white aided its being "beautiful". But with the comma both adjectives directly qualify "horse"; and are represented as if they were independent qualities of a horse.
