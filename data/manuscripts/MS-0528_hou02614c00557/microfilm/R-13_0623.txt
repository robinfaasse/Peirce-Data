I begin by briefly restating the principles of the logical
algebra, in the light of conceptions derived especially
from the study of the important memoir of Professor O.
H. Mitchell entitled "On a new Algebra of Logic."

When anything signifies of represents any thing or fact,
it does so in one or other of three ways: 1st, by its
likeness or analogy with the thing signified, 2nd, by
being physically connected therewith, or 3rd, by being
associated therewith in the mind, say habitual association.
It si by this third mode of signifying by which a proposi-
tion represents the fact it states; but this mode of
signifying cannot exist isolated from the other two.
The universre which is the real subject of discourse,
can only be pointed out -- never desctibed. If you tell
me that there is or that there is not a black swan or a
white raven, I want to know where you mean; and that
you can only show me,-- no description will answer the
purpose. Such showing or pointing out consists in putting
me into a physical connection with the thing signified.
