This personage was never heard of until, in [date]1599[/date],
Johann Thölde published, as editor, the first five treatises
in the German language which he successively gave to the
public as having been written by "Basilius Valentinus, Benedictine
monk." The last of these, published, according to Kopp, in [date]1604[/date], and
entitled [u]Triumph-Wagen des Antimonii[/u], is the one upon which the
renown of Valentine exclusively rests. All are works of alchemy,
a doctrine that, at the time of their publication, had for generations
been a mark of derision.[ins]*[/ins] I have only seen four of them, the
[u]Triumph-Wagen[/u], the [u]Welt in Kleinen[/u], the [u]Grosse Stein der uhr-alten[/u]
[u]Weisen[/u], and the treatise [u]Von natürlicher und über-natürlichen[/u]
[u]Dingen[/u].[ins]†[/ins] The [u]Triumph-Wagen[/u] is the only one I have carefully studied.

* Thus, Gulielmus Gratarolus, (an Italian physician, inclined to Lutheranism, b.
[date]1516[/date], d. [date]1568[/date]), who in [date]1561[/date] published a collection of alchemical writings in two
volumes folio, concludes an introductory dialogue on the subject with these
words: "Sapientiae autem praemia divitias esse.... nemo unquam sanae
mentis fatebitur... Necessarium est, ut priusquam sapere incipiant, aerumnam
infortunium, et ipsa [del]Atry[/del] [ins]Ate[/ins], illo miserando casu foedoque interritu dejiciat atque
pessundet."

† I only know these in the edition of [date]1740[/date], which professes (for these treatises)
to follow the text of Thölde, except that it corrects obvious misprints and
adds some plates to the [u]Grosse Stein[/u]. I have seen
these same figures attached to a different text attributed to Valentine and bearing
the date [date]1625[/date].
