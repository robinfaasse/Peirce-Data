[u]Note on the Age of Basil Valentine[/u]. [ins]J. M. Peirce[/ins]

Basil Valentine is usually reckoned as the earliest of
scientific chemists and a great light of human culture[ins]<sup>*</sup>[/ins].
He is supposed to have lived [del]a[/del] [ins]nearly a[/ins] hundred years before Paracelsus,
[ins]or at[/ins] [del]the[/del] some time in the XV[ins]<sup>th</sup>[/ins] century. One of the works which bears his
name undoubtedly [del]gives[/del] [ins]embodies[/ins] [del]affords[/del] a tolerably complete conception of
the behaviour of antimony. Popular writers absurdly attribute to
him the discovery and the name of that metal[ins]<sup>†</sup>[/ins], together with some
of the most elementary operations of chemistry. But Hermann
Kopp, the [del]least unsatisfactory[/del] of the historians of Western alchemy[ins]<sup>‡</sup>[/ins],
has emphasized certain doubts about the [del]person[/del] Valentine which it is
the purpose of this note to resolve.

<sup>*</sup> Thus, one historian, Schmieder, opens his chapter about Valentine with
the words, "Ein Nordlicht lodert in farbigen Strahlen am Deutschlands
Horizont empor."

<sup>†</sup> Berthelot confines himself to Greek and Arabian authors. The work of
Dr. Latz, himself a believer in alchemy, gives some account of the contents
of the alchemical books. Kopp does not pretend to have examined the majority
of the works he mentions. Hoefer's history never had a high critical value.
Schmieder and Gmelin are superseded.
