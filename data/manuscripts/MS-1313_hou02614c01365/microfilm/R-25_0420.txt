It is doubtful whether the malady was brought to Germany from France or from Naples. Trithemius, a contemporary German abbot, says it came both ways[ins]*[/ins]. It was [del]much[/del] [ins]much[/ins] later, during the XVIth century, that the theory of a French origin became [del]under[/del] generally accepted as certain.

But passing by this difficulty, and continuing to accept the seven passages as bona fides, [ins]to[/ins] what date do they point? Johannes Salicetus, whose work on this pestilence was printed in 1501, says that it [del]had[/del] had prevailed in Germany since 1457.[ins]†[/ins] The records of an monastery at Mainz (near which Valentine [del]says he[/del] [ins]must have[/ins] lived) show that a chorister there was attacked by it in 1472[ins]‡[/ins], so that it was already spreading beyond the army; and so [del]great[/del] [ins]famous[/ins] a physician as Basil Valentine [del]boasts of being[/del] would certainly have heard of that case. But Valentine says it was brought to Germany, not by German soldiers coming home, but by French soldiers[ins],[/ins] in the "present war." I do not know what war that could have been, unless it was the struggle of Charles the Bold, not far from the Rheingau, which lasted from 1464 to 1477. Thus, if the book is genuine at all, we find again that it must have been written about 1470.
