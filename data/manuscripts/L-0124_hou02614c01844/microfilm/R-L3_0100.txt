The sentence you quote from p. 128 of Jevons’s [u]Lessons[/u] is not intended as a definition of the major and minor terms of a syllogism. The definition is contained in the preceding sentence. It ought to have been more formally expressed.

The minor term of a syllogism is the subject of the conclusion.
The major term is the predicate of the conclusion.

Applying this to the syllogism AEE of the second figure
Any X is Y,
No Z is Y;
∴ No Z is X,
we see that Z is the minor term and X is the major term. That the conclusion is valid, is obvious from an example
All men are entitled to life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness
No Phillipino is entitled to life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness
Hence, No Phillipino is a man.

The sentence on p. 128 which gives you trouble is intended to explain why Aristotle called the subject of the conclusion the minor extreme and the predicate of the conclusion the major extreme (or terms). This reason is, I think, undoubtedly correct. Aristotle took A as the typical form of proposition, and since in A the predicate is greater than the subject in logical extension or breadth, he confirmed the use of the terms words τὸ μεῖζον ἄκρον and τὸ ἔλαττον ἄκρον even when as in E, I, O the predicate is not necessarily of greater breadth than the subject. There are many technical phrases in logic which are consecrated by ages of use and which have the merit of being perfectly precise, but which are otherwise most inappropriate. If I can be of any further help to you, you have only to write & I shall answer with alacrity.

Yours truly C. S. Peirce
