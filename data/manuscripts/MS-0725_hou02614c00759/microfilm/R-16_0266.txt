[u]§ 3. [del]Of the Different Senses in which the Terms Extension and Com-[/del][/u]
[u]prehension have been accepted.[/u]

[u]The terms extension and comprehension, and their synonymes, are[/u]
[u]taken in different senses by different writers. This is partly owing to[/u]
[u]the fact that while most writers speak only of the extension and com-[/u]
[u]prehension of concepts, others apply these terms equally to concepts[/u]
[u]and judgments (Rösling), others to any mental representation (Über-[/u]
[u]weg and many French writers), others to cognition generally (Baum-[/u]
[u]garten), others to "terms" (Fowler, Spalding), others to names[/u]
[u]([del]Shedden[/del]), others to words (McGregor), others to "meanings" (Jev-[/u]
[u]ons), while one writer speaks only of the extension of classes and the[/u]
[u]comprehension of attributes (De Morgan in his Syllabus).[/u]

[u]Comprehension is defined by the Port Royalists as "those attributes[/u]
[u]which an idea involves in itself, and which cannot be taken away from[/u]
[u]it without destroying it."[/u]

[u]It will be remembered that the marks of a term are divided by logi-[/u]
[u]cians first into the necessary and the accidental, and that then the[/u]
[u]necessary marks are subdivided into such as are strictly essential, that is,[/u]
[u]contained in the definition, and such as are called proper. Thus it is an[/u]
[u]essential mark of a triangle to have three sides; it is a proper mark to[/u]
[u]have its three angles equal to two right angles; and it is an accidental[/u]
[u]mark to be treated of by Euclid.[/u]

[u]The definition of the Port Royalists, therefore, makes comprehen-[/u]
[u]sion include all necessary marks, whether essential or proper.[/u]

[u]The Port Royalists attribute comprehension immediately to any[/u]
[u]ideas. Very many logicians attribute it immediately only to concepts.[/u]
[u]Now a concept, as defined by them, is strictly only the essence of an[/u]
[u]idea; they ought therefore to include in the comprehension only the[/u]
[u]essential marks of a term. These logicians, however, abstract so[/u]
[u]entirely from the real world, that it is difficult to see why these essential[/u]
[u]marks are not at the same time all the marks of the object as they sup-[/u]
[u]pose it.[/u]

[u]There can, I think, be no doubt that such writers as Gerlach and[/u]
[u]Sigwart make comprehension include all marks, necessary or accidental,[/u]
[u]which are universally predicable of the object of the concept.[/u]
