This redness, which you entirely see before you; for if there be any element of it you do not see, that is not what I refer to, this redness when regarded as "in" (whatever that is imagined to mean) your mind, — though how that [del]which[/del] [ins]whose essence[/ins] you entirely see, [del]for it only consists in what you see,[/del] can be "in" anything you cannot see, I cannot understand; and to me it is flat nonsense; — but this redness regarded in that fanciful way is by many persons (yea, and by a good many psychologists too, who ought to know better) — called a "feeling." That, however, is not the sense in which I use the term "feeling." What I mean is that [ins]an decomposable[/ins] element of what you [del]see[/del] see which involves no reference to anything else; the test of its not doing so being that it might conceivably be spread over an extension and homogeneously fill the same and might conceivably last through any length of time occupying the whole homogeneously. By "homo-

geneously," I mean it would fill the whole space and time by filling every indefinitely small part thereof and being perfectly alike in every part. Such an element of experience I call a [u]feeling[/u], or monadic experience; where I use "monad" in the chemical sense of a univalent, or [del]one[/del] singly-connected element. For feeling is connected with, and relative to, the world of my experience, and nothing else. A [del]pure[/del] feeling, in its purity, is absolutely simple, since otherwise it would be relative to its parts. Not that it may not be capable of being "analyzed," as we say; but only that this analysis does not consist in separating the feeling itself but only in separating a representative of it.
