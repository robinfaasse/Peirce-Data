[u]Little Dictionary[/u]

The English verb [del]must[/del] [ins]properly[/ins] means to leave
[del](a person or thing)[/del] to the disposition of another.
Thus the great Scotch metaphysician Hume, has
"to abandon that place to the merciless fury
of the enemy." And the historian Macauley speaks
of "those who completely abandoned to others the
direction of the vessels." The wonderful novelist
De Foe says that some people during the great plague
in London in the [u]XVII[/u]th century "abandoned them-
selves to their despair." The modern writer McCarthy
says "he seldom abandons himself altogether to the
inspiration of the past." It is also sometimes
used without saying to what or to whom the
person or thing is abandoned. But in that
case "forsake" [ins]"desert"[/ins] or "leave" seems a better word.
The modern novelist Miss Braddon says "I felt
myself abandoned and alone in the world."
The word "abandoned" is often used to mean
abandoned to the devil, or to one's own evil
passions; and is a very strong expression.

[u]To abase[/u] means to degrade or lower in
social position [u]extremely[/u].

[u]To abash[/u] means to put out of countenance, to make
ashamed for the moment, to disconcert. Gladstone
says "I might have been abashed by their autho-
rity." It is most often thus used in the passive.

[u]To abate[/u] meant originally to beat down, and in law it
is used to mean to put an end to some state of
things by a legal proceeding. Otherwise, it
means merely to weaken some feeling or action
that is not physical, especially the force of a
reason, or the course of a disease or a demand
