[u]BOOLIAN ALGEBRA.[/u]

The algebra of Logic was first invented by the
celebrated English mathematician, George Boole,
and has been subsequently improved by the labors
of a number of writers in England, France, Germany,
and America. The deficiency of pronouns in English,
as in every other tongue, begins to be felt as soon as
there is occasion to discourse of the relations of more
than two objects, and forces the lawyers of today in
speaking of parties, as it did Euclid of old in rea-
soning about the relative situations of many points,
to designate them as A, B, C, etc. This device is al-
ready a long stride toward an algebraical nota-
tion. Two other kinds of signs must, however, be
introduced, at once. The first embraces the pa-
theses and brackets which are the punctuation
marks of algebra. The imperfection of the ordinary
system of punctuation is notorious. It is too stale
a joke to fill the corner of a newspaper to show
how a phrase may be ambiguous when written down
from which the pauses of speech would exclude all
uncertainty. In algebraical notation, we simply
enclose an expression in a parenthesis to show that
it is to be taken together and treated as a unit. We
thus distinguish the "black (lady's veil)" from the
"(black lady)'s veil." In case it becomes necessary
to enclose one parenthesis within another, we use
the square brackets [ ] for the outer one. These may
