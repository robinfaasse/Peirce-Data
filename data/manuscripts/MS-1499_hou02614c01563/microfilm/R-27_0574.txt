[u]Cheremon[/u], [u]Cherremon[/u], [u]Χαιρήμων[/u], of
Alexandria, was librarian of the Serapion,
and wrote a book called [u]Ἱερογλυφικά[/u]. He
was a Stoic. Josephus calls him a liar.
Lived in first half of I[ins]st[/ins] Century A.D.

[u]Epius[/u] This should be some person named
[u]Ἔπειος[/u]; but I know no such.

[u]Orus Apollo Niliacus[/u], in [u]Horapollo[/u],
[u]Ὡραπόλλων[/u], of the reign of Theodosius,
of Phaenebythis in Egypt. His [u]Ἱερογλυφικά[/u]
is extant in Greek, purporting to be a trans-
lation by Philippus from the Egyptian. There
were five editions before Browne wrote.
His interpretations of single hierogly-
phics seem to be generally correct; but
he had not the slightest comprehension
of the system of them.

[u]Philippus[/u]. Nothing at all is known of him
except that he translated Horapollo.

[u]Pierius[/u]. This is [del]Pierius[/del] [ins]Valerianus[/ins] i.e.
Giampietro Valeriano 1477-1558 of the
family of the Bolzani. He was a domestic of
the Medici Leo X, Hippolyto & Alexander. On
the violent death of these, 1535 & 1537 he went
to live in Venice and his renown began. His
[u]Hieroglyphica[/u] 1556 has little to do with
Egyptian hieroglyphics, it is about emblems
in general.

[u]Kircherus[/u] Athanasius Kircher, a Jesuit, b.
in Geisen 1602 (8 years before Browne) d. Rome
1680. Inventor of the Magic Lantern. He
introduced Europe to the Coptic language and
became so [del]immensely[/del] expert in reading
the hieroglyphics (quite differently from those
who inherited them) that when Andreas Müller
made some fancy characters on a piece of
parchment [del]and sent it to Kircher, the latter[/del]
pronounced it to be hieroglyphics and gave
a marvellous translation thereof. His
[u]Lingua Aegyptiaca Restituta[/u], his chief, but
not his first work on Hieroglyphics was pub-
lished in 1643 (3 years before the [u]Pseudodoxia[/u]
[u]Epidemica[/u]).

[u]Euclide[/u], the geometer, constantly confounded
in old books with Euclid of Megara, He
was about a century later than Aristotle.
As to making him believe a circle has more
than one centre, it is plain that any point at
infinity is a centre, since it bisects chords
through it. And there being more than one
right angle in a triangle (meaning a
plane triangle) if [del]the[/del] one vertex is at infinity
there may be two in Euclidean geometry, and
if the fallacy of Prop XVI were pointed out to
Euclid, he would see that even a finite
triangle might have two.
