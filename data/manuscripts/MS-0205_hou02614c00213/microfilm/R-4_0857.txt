multitude is mere succession, yet that all quantity involving measurement,
including [ins]all rational[/ins] [del]free all[/del] fractions, involve an idea of [u]equality[/u], which is irreducible to
an affair of succession, and that this is shown by the circumstance that the

To a Scottish, like myself, the logic of such an objection cannot appear
accurate; and I believe it might be answered in more ways than one. But
since our purpose here is mere entertainment I will meet it by first
defining fractions without reference to any higher operation than addition
and then by [del]arranging[/del] [ins]showing how to arrange[/ins] them in the order of their values by numeration only.

In speaking of cardinal numbers, I have confined myself entirely
to two relations, that of G, and that of "greater than". I have never spoken
of equality. Now in speaking of fractions I will not use those relations at
all; but only another relation, that of [ins](at least as)[/ins] "being as great as". If one fraction, [u]f[/u]₁,
is "as great as" [del]another[/del] [ins]a fraction[/ins], [u]f[/u]₂, I will, in order to avoid circumlocution, speak of
[u]f[/u]₂ as being [del](at least as)[/del] "as small as" [u]f[/u]₁.
