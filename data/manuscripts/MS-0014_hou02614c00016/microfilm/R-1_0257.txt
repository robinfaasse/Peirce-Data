[u]Quantity[/u]

The question is [del]whether[/del] [ins]if[/ins] two collections can be supposed to have such multitudes that, by reason of that circumstance, neither can have all its individuals in one-to-one correspondence with some (or all) of the individuals of the other.

It seems evident that nothing can prevent [ins]the possibility of[/ins] all the individuals of one collection [del]to be[/del] [ins]being[/ins] in one-to-one correspondence with any [del]others[/del] except that the multitude of the former collection is too great. And if two collections were in the relation supposed, it is plain that no addition of further individuals could make it possible for either to [del]be[/del] in one-to-one correspondence to a part or all of the other, unless there were some reason why they could be made to correspond with certain individuals but not with others, which supposition is excluded, since it is supposed that it is [del]the[/del] [ins]merely[/ins] degrees of multitude which prevent the possibility of such correspondence. Hence, if [del]these[/del] the relation supposed were to exist for any multitudes, it must exist for all equal or greater multitudes.

But it certainly does not exist for [del]all[/del] greater multitudes. For considering the aggregate of the two multitudes, either of these multitudes is smaller [del]since than that aggregate, since[/del] [ins]hypotheses[/ins] This aggregate could not be placed in one-to-one correspondence with either of the two first multitudes, while each of the latter [del]must[/del] [ins]by the relation of identity[/ins] be entirely in one-to-one correspondence with a part of that aggregate.

Hence, the supposition is absurd; and of any two multitudes whatever one can be [del]put into[/del] in one-to-one correspondence with the whole or a part of the other. In other words, of any two multitudes [del]which[/del] [ins]that[/ins] are not equal, one is greater than the other.

Art. 16. The smallest of all possible multitudes is none.
