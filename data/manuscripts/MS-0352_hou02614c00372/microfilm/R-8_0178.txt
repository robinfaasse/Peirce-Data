It is true we usually speak of the moods as different forms of syllogism; but this is but a figure of speech, for I believe we intend to maintain only that the forms of their parts are different, not that the essential form of inference is different.

But when we [del]ask[/del] [ins]come to[/ins] the same question respecting the figures there is a difference of opinion among logicians; the greater body considering them as essentially different, while many of the best modern authors consider them the same.

[del]In order to answer this question, I must begin with some observations, which will seem quite irrelevant, but which we shall find are not so.[/del]

[del]In this diagram [[fig]three pairs of crosses[/fig]] I have put down three pairs of crosses, illustrating all the ways in which a collection of crosses may be situated with reference to being blue. Either [ins]1st[/ins] all are blue, or 2nd a part are blue and a part not, or 3rd None are blue.[/del]

In order to answer this question; I must call your attention to the orders of the terms subjects & predicates in the three figures. We have seen that the [del]subject[/del] [ins]predicate[/ins] of the rule is the predicate of the case; and that the [del]subject[/del] [ins]predicate[/ins] of the result is the subject of the case and that the predicate of the result is the predicate of the case. Accordingly if we just write the subject & predicate of each proposition without the is, [del]is not[/del], all, or some. We have in the first figure $\left. \begin{matrix} M P \\ S M \\ S P \end{matrix} \right\}$ This may represent any mood; All M is P All S is M $\therefore$ All S is P or No M is P; some S is M $\therefore$ Some S is not P

In the second figure the case & result are contraposed and we have $\begin{matrix} M P \\ S P \\ S M \end{matrix}$. In the third figure the rule & result are contraposed and we have $\begin{matrix} S P \\ S M \\ M P \end{matrix}$. Now you perceive that in the first figure the term which is contained in the [ins]first[/ins] two propositions is the subject of the first & the predicate of the second, and of the other two terms one is subject twice & the other predicate twice. It is different with the second & third figures; in the second the term which is in [del]both[/del] [ins]the[/ins] two first propositions is predicate of both; in the third figure the term which is in the two first propositions is [del]predicate[/del] [ins]subject[/ins] of both. Now I shall show you that there are three ways in which the terms of the second figure & three ways in which the terms of the third figure can be transposed so as to give them the same arrangement which is found among [ins]the[/ins] terms of the first figure.
