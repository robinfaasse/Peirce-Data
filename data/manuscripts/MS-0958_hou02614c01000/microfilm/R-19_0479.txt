Dr. Carus says "that form is a quality of this
world, not of some samples of it, but throughout,
so far as we know of existence even in the
most superficial way, and thus we know
beforehand... that the laws of form hold
good so far as our telescopes sweep." As to
the antithesis with which this sentence opens,
I remark that [del]all[/del] [ins]the sum of[/ins] actual experience
is but a sample of all possible experience,
and therefore the mode of cognition described
is nothing but induction. Indeed, it is the
most usual form of induction. When Kant
concludes that that which is universal must
be involved in the conditions of experience
he is only laying down the principle of
induction; but when he jumps to the conclusion
that the conditions of experience in question are
subjective, he commits a paralogism.

He here refutes an argument against ne-
cessity which I did not use.
