[u]OUR TWO, THREE: KANTIAN CATEGORIES.[/u]

By C. S. Peirce.

If it were only for doubting axioms. With mathematicians, the question is settled; there is no reason to believe that the geometrical axioms are exactly true. Metaphysics is an imitation of geometry, and with the geometrical axioms the metaphysical axioms must go too.

Our first reason to think that the sum of the three angles of a triangle is exactly equal to two right-angles. All that we can say is that the excess or defect is proportional to the area of the triangle, and that it is excessively minute even for the most enormous triangles of astronomy. The sum of the three angles of a triangle of unit size is a physical constant nearly equal to 180 degrees; but its real value is unknown to us.

There is no reason to think that this constant is exactly equal to 180 degrees, and there is a infinite multitude of other values that it can equally well have, the odds are at present infinitely strong against its being exactly 180 degrees, so that that hypothesis ought to be entirely rejected. There is no reason for this.
