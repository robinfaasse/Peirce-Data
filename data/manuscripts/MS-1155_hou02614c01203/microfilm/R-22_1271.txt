eye. The color of a body is the same as that of the light which proceeds from it when it is ex-
posed to [del]ordinary[/del] daylight. The philosophy of chromatics, so far as it is essential to a rational
nomenclature for colors, must be briefly stated. All light, considered in itself, without refe-
rence to its effect upon the eye, certainly consists in undulations of an ether, or perhaps of
electricity. By means of [del]colors[/del] [ins]a prism or diffraction-grating[/ins] these undulations may be harmonically
analyzed, that is, resolved into a series of simple pendulous oscillations [ins]of different periods. But owing to the[/ins] [del]having only a quasi-periodicity, the result of this analysis is not, as in the case
of a musical note, a discrete series of harmonics, but is a continuous series of oscillations,
having every wave-length.[/del] [ins]quasi-periodicity of the oscillations,[/ins] Of this continuous series of waves, only those whose wave-lengths lie
between certain limits affect the eye, and of these latter no two affect it in the same way.
What we see when the light is so harmonically analyzed is a longitudinal spread of rainbow-colors
each of which is physically distinguished by its own peculiar rate of oscillation. The whole
spread of light is called a spectrum. The spectra of different lights are distinguished by the
different intensities of their different parts; but corresponding parts of any two spectra, when
brought to the same intensity, are absolutely alike in every respect. The colors of the spectrum,
which shade continuously into one another, were arbitrarily divided by Newton into red, orange,
yellow, green, blue, indigo, and violet, These are sometimes called the seven primary colors. By
indigo is meant a violet blue, very unlike the color of indigo as a pigment. The more judicious

When light strikes upon a body, a certain proportion, practically the same proportion for all
parts of the spectrum, is reflected. If the reflection is regular, this light is not seen except
in certain directions. If it is irregular, it communicates a dull, whitish appearance to the body
which will be in large part removed by wetting. Of the light which is not reflected, but pene-
trates the body, a part is absorbed, and the rest transmitted (it may be after internal reflec-
tion). The absorption of different parts of the spectrum by different bodies is [del]very[/del] different,
and it is the color of the residue of light which is not absorbed, but is either [ins]directly[/ins] transmitted, if
the body is transparent, or is emitted after internal reflection, which is mainly determinative
of the color of the bodies.

Two colors are said to match when they are exactly alike, and can only be distinguished as
belonging to different bodies. Two lights are said to be added when they simultaneously strike
the same part of the retina. It is an observed fact that if two lights, [del]A and B[/del], respectively
match two other lights, [del]C and D[/del], then the sum of the first two lights matches the sum of the
second two. Two colors are said to be mixed on a surface which emits light which would be matched
