[u]CAPILLARITY[/u], n.
1. Hairlike fineness of a tube.
2. Capillary action; the form of phenomena of the rise of liquids in capillary tubes, and in
chinks; and more generally, of every phenomenon due to the surface-tension of liquids.

CAPILLARY, a. 1. Resembling or pertaining to hair.
2. Resembling a single hair.
3. Having as a tube so small a bore that water cannot be poured into it.
4. Pertaining to the phenomena of the rise of liquids in tubes and to chinks; and more generally
to the collecting of liquids in drops, their spreading over surfaces [as oil on water], and
various other phenomena explicable by surface-tension and ultimately by cohesion and adhesion,
considered as forces acting at finite but insensible distances. See surface-tension.
Capillary attraction and repulsion, the excess or deficiency of the attraction of one of
two fluids [the other being generally air] which have a common line of contact with the walls
of a vessel for that wall the surface of the more attracted fluid makes an acuter angle with the
wall. It is frequently said that capillary attraction is accounted for by surface-tension; but the
the latter has to be accounted for by the attractions between molecules.

CENTRIFUGAL, a. 1. Tending to fly away from the centre of curvature of a path. Centrifugal force,
the reaction of a body constrained to move on a curved path against the force which prevents it
from moving in a straight line, this reaction being due to the inertia of the body. In accurate
language, centrifugal force is used in two senses, namely, [1] as an acceleration, equal to the
square of the angular velocity multiplied by the radius of curvature of the path, being the
negative of the acceleration which the body actually experiences in the direction of the centre
of motion, and [2] as the pressure, the product of this acceleration into the mass of the body.

CENTRIPETAL, a. For the definition of Centripetal force, substitute:
Centripetal force, any attraction causing a body to move in an orbit round another;
thus gravitation acts on the planets as a centripetal force.

NOTE FOR MR. SMITH. I observe a citation from the Encyclopædia Britannica, an article by Maxwell
which speaks of "the ratio of the numerical value of the stress to the numerical value of the
strain." This dictionary should give no countenance to such expressions. The di idea of Maxwell
is that that there can be no ratio except between numbers. This is against usage, and against
good sense. It is against usage, because the word ratio has its ratio essendi in the wish to
express the quotient of one quantity by another whether neither are numbers. That is the usual ap-
plication of the word with Euclid, and all the old mathematicians. But it has long been generally
recognized among mathematicians and physicists that it is better to consider all kinds of quan-
tities as subject to multiplication and division. It is this principle which has freed us from
the horrible prolixities of the older mathematical physics; and were Maxwell's notion admitted,
we should be plunged once more into this horrid slough. To give a very inadequate example, when
Newton wishes to say that an acceleration is equal to a space divided by the square of a time,
so that multiplied by the square of the time it gives the space, he expresses himself thus:
When bodies traversing similar arcs in proportional times are solicited by new forces equal and
applied in homologous directions, the deviations, that is to say, the distances from the points
where the bodies actually arrive to the points where they would have arrived without the action
of these new forces, are as the double ratios of the times during which the deviations have
been produced." Even were the modern mode of expression less accurate than the older, it would
be infinitely preferable on account of its superior perspicuity. But in point of fact, there is
no difficulty in getting a perfectly clear idea of what is meant by the division or multiplica-
