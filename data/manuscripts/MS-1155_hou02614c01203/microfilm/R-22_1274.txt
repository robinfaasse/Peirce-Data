Explanation of the nomenclature of color-sensations
[/marginalia]

COLOR, [u]n.[/u] 1. That quality of a body which is correlative to the sensation excited when, [del]it is[/del] [ins]being[/ins] exposed to daylight, [del]and[/del] rays from it strike the retina of the eye. The color of a ray of light corresponds to the sensation it excites when it strikes the retina. The ordinary colors of bodies are the same as those of the light they transmit. Namely, ordinary reflection the composition of the incident and reflected rays [del]is[/del] [ins]are[/ins] the same, so that the color of the latter can only differ from that of the former in consequence of its diminished intensity. [ins]Thus, the colors of bodies are not affected by the light they reflect from their surfaces, except by the admixture of ordinary daylight due to this reflection. And[/ins] [del]In[/del] the greater part of the light ordinarily penetrates the body. If the body is transparent, the color is that of the transmitted light. If it is not transparent in bulk, it will either not show any color, but will appear black, or else the light will be reflected from the further surfaces of the particles, or from particles of some other substance within the body, and then the light emitted is the residue that is not absorbed. [del]And[/del] The colors of metallic surfaces, [del]and[/del] of fluorescent bodies (such as the blue of a solution of quinine), as well as some part of the color of abnormally dispersive bodies (as the aniline dyes), are to be explained on other principles. [ins]The colors of light emitted by incandescent and phosphorescent bodies is altogether different from those of the bodies themselves. These colors produced by dispersion, interference, and diffraction are, nearly, alike for all bodies.[/ins] [del]These colors are, however, not commonly spoken of as colors of the bodies themselves.[/del] Two colors are said to match when they are exactly alike and can only be distinguished as belonging to different bodies. Two lights are said to be added when they simultaneously strike the same part of the retina. It is an observed fact, essential to the rational nomenclature of colors, that if two lights, A and B, respectively match two other lights, M and N, then the sum of A and B matches the sum of M and N. Two colors are said to be mixed on a surface which emits light which matches the one, only with some proportion of it replaced by light which matches the other. Thus, if a circular disk be then painted in two colors, the line of division being a diameter of the disk, and the disk be then set in rapid rotation in its own plane and about its own centre, [del]the line of division[/del] [ins]and rapidly rotated,[/ins] the shimmer disappears and the two colors will be mixed. Given any five colors, then it is a result of observation that some pair of them can be so mixed as to match some mixture of the other three, or else some four of them can be so mixed as to match the fifth; but only one of these things can be done, and that in only one way. Consequently, if we take A, B, C, D, E, to denote [del]any[/del] five colors, [ins]if we represent the addition of lights by the algebraical sign of addition, and if we represent the perfect matching of two colors by writing them with the sign of equality between them, we shall have[/ins] an equation of one or other of the two forms, aA+bB+cC=dD+eE or aA+bB+cC+dD=eE, where a, b, c, d, e, are positive numbers such that the sum of those on [del]each[/del] [ins]either[/ins] side of the equation is equal to 1. [ins]In such an equation, we may, by algebraical transposition, carry all the capital letters but one, say E, to one side of the equation, and then, after dividing throughout by the coefficient of that one, we shall have an equation of the form E=aA+bB+cC+dD, where a, b, c, d, are now numbers positive or negative such that a+b+c+d=1. If we now eliminate d, and put A'=A-D, B'=B-D, C'=C-D, E'=E-D, we shall have E'=aA'+bB'+cC', where a, b, c, are any numbers positive or negative. If therefore, we agree upon four fixed colors that will not make a match, to be denoted by A, B, C, and D, then any other color whatever can be exactly defined by means of three positive or negative numbers, a, b, and c referring to the four fixed colors, in the manner just set forth. It is natural to choose for one of our four fixed colors the blackest we can find, which is the color of a hole opening into a dark box blackened on the inside. For the other three fixed colors, it is convenient to choose a Chinese vermillion, schweinfurt green, and artificial ultramarine. [ins]By the equation of a color is meant the equation of its color-sensations.[/ins][/ins]

The Young-Helmholtz theory of color (named for [del]Dr.[/del] Thomas Young, b. 1773-d. 1829, who, however, contributed nothing of value to the theory, and Hermann Ludwig Ferdinand Helmholtz, b. 1821, [ins])[/ins] is that there are three kinds of nerves in the retina, giving respectively sensations of red, green, and violet, and that all other color sensations are due to the simultaneous excitation of two [del]or more[/del] kinds of nerves or of all three. Maxwell's law of colors (published by James Clerk Maxwell in [del]1853[/del] [ins]1860[/ins]) is that every color of the spectrum can be matched by a mixture of two only of three colors the scarlet of the vermillion of the spectrum at wave-length 0.630 [ins](Angstrom)[/ins], the pure blue of the spectrum at wave-length 0.484, and a green a little more intense than the pure green of the spectrum at wave-length 0.524, except only that the green of the spectrum contains a little of both the red and the blue. [del]The[/del] Since every color that can be produced is formed by some mixture of [del]the[/del] colors of the spectrum, it follows that if these three colors [ins]more[/ins] be taken as the colors of reference, A, B, C, the coefficients a, b, c, in the equation of every other color [del]will[/del] [ins]would[/ins] always be positive.

Color-sensations vary in three different ways, viz., in hue, in luminosity, and in chroma. [del]Hue[/del] [ins]Hue[/ins] is the quality of color in common speech often called color, simply, and is the most striking respect in which different parts of the spectrum differ. Variations of hue form a self-returning series.
