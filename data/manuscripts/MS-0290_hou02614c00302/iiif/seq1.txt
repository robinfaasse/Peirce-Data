CR 11 [del]THE[/del] ISSUES OF PRAGMATICISM.
Ms. 290
In continuation of a paper in the April number of the
Monist, I proceed to the consequences of "Pragmaticism", that is
to say of a certain variety of Pragmatism, in fact of the very
variety which the latter word was first invented by me to
describe. But now that, as commonly happens with names
invented to designate the doctrines put forth by individual
philosophists, its meaning has [ins]gradually[/ins] become stretched by wear, I
cheerfully assent to its looser application and strongly recom-
mend its use to denote [del]the[/del] [ins]common philosophical[/ins] tendency of a group of recent
writers, James, Schiller, Stout, and other "personal idealists", Royce,
the brilliant school of Dewey (if they will acknowledge the
affiliation) together with whoever else (including myself)
feels himself [del]and is felt by the others[/del] to share the same ways of
thinking.
For my own part, I quite agree with Mr. Schiller ([u]Hu-
manism[/u], p. 8,) in considering the most vital point to be the
recognition of the purposive element of intellectual pur-
port. Yet [del]I should think[/del] [ins]all of us surely would[/ins] a classification quite monstrously
unnatural which should exclude from the ranks, not to
say from [del]the[/del] leaderships in this school some thinkers
who do not particularly emphasize that element. But it
is Pragmaticism alone, my initial position which I retain,
though defining it more explicitly, that I can presume to
expound or defend.
