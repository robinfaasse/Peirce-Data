[pn]CP 1[/pn] [del]THE[/del] ISSUES OF PRAGMATICISM.

In continuation of a paper in the April number of the [u]Monist[/u], I proceed to the consequences of "Pragmaticism", that is to say of a certain variety of Pragmatism, in fact of the very variety which the latter word was first invented by me to describe. But now that, as commonly happens with names invented to designate the doctrines put forth by individual philosophers, its meaning has [ins]gradually[/ins] become stretched by wear, I cheerfully assent to its looser application and strongly recommend its use to denote the [ins]common philosophical[/ins] tendency of a group of recent writers, James, Schiller, Stout, and other "personal idealists", Royce, the brilliant school of Dewey (if they will acknowledge the affiliation) together with whoever else (including myself) feels himself and is felt by the others to share the same ways of thinking.

For my own part, I quite agree with Mr. Schiller ([u]Humanism[/u], p. 8) in considering the most vital point to be the recognition of the purposive element of intellectual purport. Yet [del]I should think[/del] [ins]all of us surely would[/ins] a classification quite monstrously unnatural which should exclude from the ranks, not to say from [del]the[/del] leaderships in this school some thinkers who do not particularly emphasize that element. But it is Pragmaticism alone, my initial position which I retain, though defining it more explicitly, that I can presume to expound or defend.
