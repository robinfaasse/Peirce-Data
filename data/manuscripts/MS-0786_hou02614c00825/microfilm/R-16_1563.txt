[u]Syllogism[/u]
There is thus no reason for restricting syllogism to non-
relative propositions.

There is also no authority for it beyond this, that it
is not usual in treatises on logic to consider relative
syllogism in any detail, and such reasonings as
a is greater than b
b is greater than c
[three_dots] a is greater than c
are rightly [del]said not to be syllogistic[/del]. No, but if the con-
clusion had been
a is greater than something greater than c
it would have been syllogism in [del]substance[/del] [ins]substance[/ins], and you are
defining syllogism as if it were a question of the substance
not of the form of statement of reasoning. For otherwise
stated, it is
Something than which a is greater is b
b is greater than c
[three_dots] Something than which a is greater is greater than c.
Examples are plenty in every age of logic of relative syllogisms
being called syllogisms. But of course not an argument
depending on a peculiar property of a peculiar relation.

"The syllogism is frequently defined as a group of three judgments
of which the last is necessitated by the two preceding." Instead of
vaguely saying that [del]this[/del] [ins]it[/ins] is so "frequently defined" I would say
that this is the [del]definition[/del] of Aristotle (substantially) all but
universally accepted by logicians. He gives it in the first
chapter of the Analytics: συλλογισμὸς δέ ἐστι λόγος ἐν ᾧ
τεθέντων τινῶν ἕτερόν τι τῶν κειμένων ἐξ ἀνάγκης συμβαίνει τῷ
