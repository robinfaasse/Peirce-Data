The example (m) is remarkably good, as an example of a syllogism a little out of the ordinary track.

It was Kant who introduced the ridiculous distinction between Schluss and immediate inference. The only difference is that the former colligates the premises into a copulative proposition. The form of inference from a single premise is the form almost universally employed by all the scholastic doctors and is called by them Consequentia. There are special treatises about it. The premiss was called the antecedens the conclusion the consequens. What modern logicians would call the suppressed premiss the called the consequentia, not distinguishing, in words, between the [del]consequen-[/del] [del]tial[/del] inference viewed as valid by the reasoner [ins]and[/ins] belonging to a general type of valid reasonings and the principle vaguely believed that it is valid. This confusion is not near so bad as [del]the one[/del] that involved in the modern term "suppressed premiss." A premiss suppressed ceases to be a premiss, and becomes part of the Leading Principle.

The [del]p[/del] conclusion's being equivalent to (with ??) the premiss in immediate inference is defined here: "that is, it may furnish sufficient [del]premises[/del] for recovering the premise." That I fully approve of; but it is in marked conflict with the Kantian view adopted in the preceding paragraph.
