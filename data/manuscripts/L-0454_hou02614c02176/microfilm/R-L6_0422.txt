as I understand it, the question in which all parties are chiefly interested. It will conduce to logical order, first to consider the theory without this element, and then to take up this as a separate question.

Both Sorby and Van Hise's microscopical examinations show innumerable [ins]minute[/ins] flakes of cleavable minerals in slate, especially mica and chlorite to be almost universally present. Sorby says that slates which do not contain them have but an imperfect cleavage. If he is right in supposing the mica was [del]there[/del] in the original clay, this would be an argument in support of the theory. But Prof. Van Hise says that not only the chloritic minerals (which Sorby admitted had not been in the clay) but also the mica has been developed [u]in situ[/u]; and if that is the case, it may be that an incipient cleavage has contributed to cause the deposition of the crystals in the interstices between the laminae. For although Prof. Hoskins may be, in the main, correct in saying that vacant spaces cannot exist in deep-seated rocks, yet that cannot be considered as absolutely so. For if we adopt the hypothesis of continuous matter (which, of course, is not strictly true,) then a force would have to be discontinuous to produce a discontinuity of motion; and the absolute closing of a cavity would be a discontinuous motion; while if we take into account the molecular constitution of matter, we must hold that there are vacant places, no matter how great the pressure. Besides, the argument of Prof. Hoskins does not exclude hollows filled with liquid in which crystals might form.

But that the cause supposed does exist there seems to be no doubt.
