We have next to inquire how far it is proved that a parallel
disposition of broad constituent particles would produce slaty cleavage.

That this would happen is supposed to be proved by artificially
produced cleavage, by field observations, and by microscopical
examinations (p. 639).

The experimentation was commenced by Sorby, whose memoir appeared
[date]April, 1853[/date] (Edin. New Phil. Jour. IV. 144). Having mixed some scales of oxide
of iron with soft pipe-clay, he changed its dimensions artificially, by pressure.
Having then dried and baked it, he found that the scales of iron oxide had all become
arranged normally to the pressure; and moreover, it then admitted of easy fracture
into thin flat pieces in the plane corresponding to the cleavage of the slate, whereas
it could not in that perpendicular to it. But clay, which has but few unequiaxed
particles [del]would not be made[/del] by similar treatment to cleave perfectly.

Prof. Van Hise mentions (p. 639) five localities where the secondary
structure of the rock can be macroscopically observed to be parallel to the
greatest dimensions of the mineral particles. The pebbles are flattened from
a small amount to a transformation into thin leaves; and in every [ins]sedimentary[/ins] case the
cleavage is along the planes of flatness. Moreover, the tendency of rocks to part
along their bedding surfaces is a further evidence of this point (p. 642). For
as each erosion particle has unequal diameters, it comes to rest in most cases
with its longer diameters in the surface of bedding.
