It seems to me, therefore, to be a weakness of [del]Tyng[/del] [ins]Tyndall's[/ins]
very able argumentation that he never unmistakably demon-
strates that slaty cleavage would result from his hypothesis.

No doubt, if the rock is heterogeneous and the adhesion
of the different materials is imperfect, a flattening of such
rock, if carried far enough might probably result in slaty
cleavage.

The next question under this branch of the inquiry is whether
[del]tabular[/del] [ins]crystals[/ins], if they were formed under compression in one
direction, would lie with their planes normal to the greatest
pressure. If there are any facts to prove this, they are unknown to me.
Tabular crystals, as they are formed in the laboratory lie in various
ways, according to their nature. I should think it very likely that their
situations would depend upon the pressure, and also upon their density
relatively to the mass out of which they were formed. But I know of no
facts upon the subject, of a general nature.
