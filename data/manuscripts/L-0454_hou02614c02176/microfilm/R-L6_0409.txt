Considered, as a deduction, then, the theory adopted by [del]to[/del] Van
Hise is not a strong one. Not only, is it not clear that the positions
of the [ins]flatnesses of the[/ins] crystals and of their planes of cleavage would result from excess
of pressure; but even if it did, it is far from certain that slaty cleavage
would result.

Yet [del]a theory[/del] [ins]a hypothesis[/ins] may be brought to a very high degree,
of certitude without having any such [u]a priori[/u] support whatever, much
as such support will strengthen a hypothesis. The most certain hypo-
theses there are, are as a matter of fact, devoid of [u]a priori[/u] support,
such as the hypothesis that Napoleon lived. [del]But[/del] In all cases, however,
it must be certain, or at least almost so, that the facts to be ex-
plained would result from the state of things supposed; and [del]this[/del] [ins]it[/ins]
is a weakness of the compression theory of slaty cleavage that [del]this[/del] [ins]it[/ins]
is very uncertain that the state of things its supposes would produce
[del]slaty cleavage[/del]. But let us see what empirical evidence is offered
that the flatnesses of the crystals and their planes of crystalline clea-
vage are at once normal to the greatest pressure and parallel
to slaty cleavage.

This seems to me, who am no geologist, decidedly the strongest
