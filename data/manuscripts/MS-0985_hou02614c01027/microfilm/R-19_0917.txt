Lutoslawski treats the question of Plato's stay in
Megara in so disingenuous a way that it ought to
put the reader once for all upon his guard. Lutos-
lawski often says how thorough a Pole he is. Now
Poles, whatever their charms may be, are known to
be liars and swindlers.

Diogenes Laertius says that Hermodorus says
certain things. There is no reason to doubt, nor has
anybody ever doubted that Hermodorus did say
substantially what Diogenes says he said. Diogenes
does not say he quotes his exact words. He uses the
[u]oratio obliqua[/u] implying that he gives the substance
only. This substance is that Plato, with the other dis-
ciples of Socrates, went to Megara after the death
of their master. Hermodorus is naturally un-
derstood to be [del]a[/del] [ins]the known[/ins] pupil of Plato of that name.
Had he been any other unknown Hermodorus,
Diogenes ought to have added some distinctive
appellation, and probably would have done so.

This, then, is the testimony. It is good his-
torical testimony, open externally, to no more
doubt than hangs over all ancient testimony
that is uncorroborated. Good logic requires its
acceptance.
