Objection 1. Lutoslawski says, p. 43, that the statement
is only "given as an opinion of Hermodorus."
This is a lie. It is given as the assertion of Hermo-
dorus, who, if he is the only Hermodorus we know,
would have been informed by Plato himself.

Objection 2. Lutoslawski says that Hermodorus is "discredited
by his ignorance of well-known facts. For it was not the
'tyrants' whom Plato had to dread," etc. This is all moon-
shine. In the first place we have very little reason to
suppose that Hermodorus used the words τῶν τυράννων.
It is very likely the word of Diogenes. But even if he did,
it would prove nothing; for Plato himself often uses the
word in the sense of one who uses power wickedly; so that there
can be no doubt that in talking to Hermodorus he would
call the persecutors of Socrates "tyrants."

Objection 3. Lutoslawski says, p. 49, that Hermodorus
"accuses Plato of cowardice". This is a lie. Hermodorus
uses the word δείσαντας, the mildest word, not imply-
ing any irrational fear, and governing the accusative where
φοβήσαντας would have been natural. Not only does
Hermodorus not "accuse" Plato of cowardice, but what
he asserts in no way justifies the inference of cowardice.
It is certain that Plato recognized that certain things were
δείνα (see Laches, Protagoras, Republic); but nobody but a Pole
could base an accusation of cowardice on that.

Objection 4. Lutoslawski says that if Plato had ever gone
to Megara, — an evening's walk from Athens, — Cicero
would have inserted a mention of it when he said
"audisse te credo Platonem Socrate mortuo primum in Aegyp-
tum discendi causa, post in Italiam et in Siciliam contendisse
ut Pythagorae inventa perdisceret." What is he undertaking to
enumerate? Journeys made by Plato, at great effort (contendisse)
for the sake of learning, and which are celebrated (audisse te
credo). Cicero would think the suggestion he should insert the
trip to Megara wanting in good sense.
