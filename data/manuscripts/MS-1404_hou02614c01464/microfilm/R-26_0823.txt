The writer of this notice is no student of history other than that of science
including philosophy. He owes the reader an apology for undertaking to review
this book. He engaged to do so before seeing it, supposing that it would be in large
part a history of science. It turns out to be nothing of the sort, but a history of the
attitude of theologians toward the discoveries of science. It is evidently a work of great
original research and appears to [del]be very[/del] [ins]give a[/ins] thorough and accurate account of the
"tergiversations of theology."

theoretical positions merely. It is one of the fruits of a hand-to-hand [del]struggle[/del] struggle
over what should be taught at Cornell University. The author narrates that at the establish-
ment of that institution he inserted stringent provisions into the charter to avoid en-
tanglements with any "single religious sect," that "opposition began at once," and
that "as the struggle deepened, hostile resolutions were introduced into various ecclesias-
tical bodies," that he first "fully tried" "sweet reasonableness," and that, finding
that of no avail, he entered upon the series of writings of which the present work
is a new recension and completion.
