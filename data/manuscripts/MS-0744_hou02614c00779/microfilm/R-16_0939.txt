[u]a priori[/u] &c

All arguments in the third figure are really inductive, for [ins]it is inferred that[/ins] [del]the premises[/del] different predicates of one subject are [del]argued to be[/del] connected. For instance,
All wild beasts fight
Yet some wild beasts are not wicked
$\therefore$ ([u]a fortiori[/u]) All the wicked fight.

On the other hand, all apodictic [del]conclusions[/del] [ins]arguments[/ins] are logically [u]a priori[/u] for the conclusion would not follow unless derived from a logical sequence.

It will be objected to this doctrine that syllogisms may be converted from one figure to another. To this I reply, that this can only be done by conversion by negation on the one hand or [del]conversion[/del] simply or by limitation on the other hand. Now [del]the first kind[/del] conversion by negation is not a real but only a logical conversion. For instance in the proposition
He who is not a man of genius is not a poet
which is evidently the result of this [ins]species of[/ins] conversion. Though He-who-is-not-a-man-of-genius is the logical subject, all-poets remains the real subject. But if it be converted into
No man of ordinary talent is a poet
we have now a different statement and reasoning from this will be different reasoning from the other. This proposition cannot be inferred immediately from the other. With regard to the other kinds of conversion, the reply is that they are not valid as immediate conversions, Because
"No animal is a griffin"
it does not follow that
"No griffin is an animal"
on the contrary "Every griffin is an animal."
