[u]Of the Distinction between à priori and à posteriori.[/u]

[del]But[/del] A distinction into two classes is generally a vague one. Reasoning à priori is inferring an effect from its cause. Reasoning à posteriori is inferring a cause from its effect. There is clearly a third way of reasoning, which consists in inferring a mutual connection between the several effects of one cause; this is inducti[del]on[/del][ins]ve[/ins] (or analogy).

[del]Reasoning[/del] These kinds of reasoning are all susceptible of subdivision owing to the different kinds of causes but the only distinction which is of importance here is between the cause in [del]an[/del] object and the cause in a representation[ins]*[/ins] ([ins]*[/ins] [del]This[/del] [ins]apparently[/ins] is a division into two, but the [del]cause in an[/del] [ins]arbitrary[/ins] image is omitted as [del]leading to[/del] [ins]being[/ins] no ground of inference). Every representation which can have a form at all must have a form of protasis and apodosis; now the negative of an apodosis will be a protasis of the negative of its own protasis. But this is a determination of the representation and is not represented as a determination of the object. Thus, suppose I say, "If the mould is concave the cast is convex" it will be the same to say "If the cast is not convex the mould will not be concave." Now the protasis in this latter statement is probably the [del](final)[/del] cause of its apodosis in the object, but it is not a [del]protasis[/del] cause in the object as it is [del]finally[/del] represented [ins]the protasis here[/ins]. It determines the apodosis of the proposition, [del]because this[/del] in virtue of this being a representation of the object; but the proposition does not represent its protasis and apodosis as being the [del]real[/del] [ins]respectively[/ins] cause and effect, but the reverse. By [del]cause[/del] and effect [del]here[/del] I mean the protasis and apodosis in the object
