We might define a relation as a fact about an ordered set of things.
[u]Ordered set[/u], I say, because it does not suffice to say that the fact of
giving is realized in A, B, and C; but it is requisite to distinguish
which is giver, which donee, and which gift. This we do by saying the
fact of [ins]the[/ins] giving by — of — to — is realized in A, B, C, in their order.
The definition is correct so far as it goes; but about any ordered set
innumerable facts are true. [del]The[/del] [ins]A[/ins] relation between them is any special
mode of fact considered as analogous to facts about other sets of
things. That is, the fact about the collection of things spoken of belongs
to a class of facts, — or, if you please, the collection of things belongs to
a class of collections, — which class is united by some common
idea attached to all its members. But this attachment of the general
idea to the collections is itself a relation, so that the definition de-
veloped according to its own spirit becomes a [u]circulus in definiendo[/u].
That is, the attachment of [ins]the general idea[/ins] [del]collection[/del] to [del]g[/del] the collection, belongs to a
class of attachments, united by a common idea attached to them all,
and so on, [u]ad infinitum[/u]. So that, narrowly looked at, this mode of
analysis of the conception only spins it out into an endless thread
of conceptions. The difficulty is not in the facts, but in the mode
of analysis. A and B are in relation, that is, the pair [del]is[/del] in relation
to that relation, that is, the pair consisting of that pair and the first
relation is in relation to the second relation, etc. These are not so
many different facts, but repetitions of the same fact due to [del]a[/del]
insisting on a mode of statement that gives rise to this result.
