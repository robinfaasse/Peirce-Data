The sun was setting when they reached the house. O Kyr-
giakoulakas had sent to say that he expected a hundred pounds for
the captive. This time, Karolo was careful to have himself duly
announced; but pressing into the chamber behind the doorkeeper, he
saw his beauty start at his name in much emotion. She flew to him
straightway, tears streaming down her cheeks, and seized him by the
arms. "Why what is the matter?" he asked through the old inter-
pretress. "O Karolo," she caught her breath. "O Karolo, I thought
you were gone; and there fell over my heart such a dark shadow of
some dreadful calamity. I was persuaded that you had met with
some accident, and that we should never meet again. Oh! I am so
glad you have come back." Karolo kissed her. "Tell me," she
said, "I did all I possibly could to make you go to-day, did I not?

"You certainly did".

"But it was not to be. It was fated you should not go,
and I am so happy."

At supper, the elder Maurokordato gave a very interesting
account of his celebrated father, Alexander, then still alive in
Egina, and of his ancestors, whose history the reader doubtless
knows by heart. To Karolo this garrulity was unspeakably grateful,
because it saved him from the necessity of either speaking or
thinking, at a moment when he could only feel.
