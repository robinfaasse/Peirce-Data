On Logical Graphs
By C. S. Peirce

The so-called "Euler's Diagrams" can be improved by drawing some of the enclosures with curves convex inwards, the positive, or affirmative, term being supposed to have its logical extension always on the side [del]where[/del] toward which the [del]curves of the[/del] [ins]parts of the[/ins] periphery [del]are[/del] [ins]are[/ins] concave. Thus, Fig. 1 expresses "Any [del]men there may be is mortal[/del]" [ins]man is mortal[/ins]," Fig. 2. "No man is a quadruped," Fig. 3, "Everything is either just or wicked"

[fig]Fig. 1. Oval within oval, labeled mortal and man[/fig]
[fig]Fig. 2. Oval between two inward-curving arcs, labeled quadruped and man[/fig]
[fig]Fig. 3. Inward-curving shape within oval, labeled just and wicked[/fig]

These figures ought not to be considered as asserting the existence of anything; but only as asserting the non-existence of the class for which no room is left, in Fig. 1. non-mortal man, in Fig. 2. quadruped man, in Fig. 3, non-wicked injustice.

In order to assert existence a large dot may be used. Thus Fig. 4. asserts that there is a soul; Fig. 5, that there is something besides money
