[u]Part I. Arrangement of Fractions by merely Counting them.[/u]

To this theory of Quantity, it has been objected that while multitude may involve nothing but succession, yet fractions involved the idea of the equality of parts, and that this is not reducible to succession is shown by the circumstance that the relative order of fractions can only be ascertained after they have been reduced to a common denominator.

This objection might be met in more ways than one; but as our principal object here is entertainment I will meet it by defining fractions without reference to any higher operation than addition and will then arrange them by numeration only.

[u]Definition.[/u] A rational fraction is an expression involving two cardinal numbers, one of which is called the numerator and the other the denominator of the fraction; and [del]every[/del]

1[u]st[/u], [del]every[/del] [ins]each[/ins] such fraction, except only that one, [del]0[/del] [ins]$\frac{0}{0}$[/ins], whose numerator and denominator are both zero, is either as great as or as small as each other fraction; but $\frac{0}{0}$ is neither definitely as great as nor as small as any fraction;

2[u]nd[/u], every fraction whose denominator is 1, the cardinal number next [ins](i.e. next greater than)[/ins] greater that is G to zero, is both as great as and as small as, its numerator;

3[u]rd[/u], if a fraction, $f_1$, is [del]as great as (or as small as)[/del] [ins]not as small as (or not as great as)[/ins], a fraction, $f_2$, whose numerator is the sum of the numerator of $f_1$ and [ins]added to[/ins] a cardinal number, A, and whose denominator is the [del]sum[/del] [ins]added to[/ins] of the denominator of $f_1$ and a cardinal number, B, then [ins]$f_2$ is as great as (or as small as)[/ins] the fraction, $f_3$, whose numerator is the sum of the numerator of $f_2$ added to A, and whose denominator is the sum of the denominator of $f_2$ added to B; [del]is also[/del] but otherwise not.

4[u]th[/u], if one fraction, $f_1$, is as great as a fraction $f_2$, then $f_2$ is as great as $f_1$ (and conversely); and then if [del]$f_1$ is as great as a fraction $f_3$, $f_1$ is as great as $f_3$;[/del] and every fraction is as great as itself.
