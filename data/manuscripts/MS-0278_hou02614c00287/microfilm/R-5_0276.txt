$2^{\text{nd}}$, If the [del]above rule is not the case[/del] [ins]greatest term of the two fractions is the same[/ins], but one of the two fractions has a term absolutely smaller than the one and the other term of the other fraction, the former shall be taken before the latter. Thus $\frac{1}{2}$ shall precede $\frac{3}{1}$, because the fractions have the same greatest term, and $2 < 3$.

$3^{\text{rd}}$, If the two fractions have the same terms, the one whose value is algebraically less (that is subtracted from the other leaves a positive remainder) shall precede. Thus $-\frac{1}{2}$ [ins]shall[/ins] precede $-\frac{2}{1}$; because $(-\frac{2}{1}) - (-\frac{1}{2}) > 0$.

The order will, then, run as follows, where a colon shows the $1^{\text{st}}$ rule decides, a [del]semicolon[/del] [ins]comma[/ins] the second, a [del]comma[/del] [ins]semicolon[/ins] the third.

$\frac{0}{1}, \frac{1}{0}; -\frac{1}{1}, +\frac{1}{1} : -\frac{2}{1}, -\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}, \frac{2}{1}; -\frac{3}{1}, -\frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{3}, \frac{3}{1};$ $-\frac{3}{2}, -\frac{2}{3}, \frac{2}{3}, \frac{3}{2} : -\frac{4}{1}, -\frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{4}, \frac{4}{1}; -\frac{4}{3}, -\frac{3}{4}, \frac{3}{4}, \frac{4}{3} : -\frac{5}{1}, -\frac{1}{5},$ $\frac{1}{5}, \frac{5}{1}; -\frac{5}{2}, -\frac{2}{5}, \frac{2}{5}, \frac{5}{2}; -\frac{5}{3}, -\frac{3}{5}, \frac{3}{5}, \frac{5}{3}; -\frac{5}{4}, -\frac{4}{5}, \frac{4}{5}, \frac{5}{4};$ etc.

It is evident the fractions can, in this way, be all numbered by whole numbers; so that the [ins]entire system of[/ins] whole numbers are as numerous as the fractions, although the whole numbers may be regarded as identical (as they are in value) with those fractions which have [del]unity[/del] [ins]unity[/ins] for their denominator. The proposition is true of finite collections; but of them it is not "self-evident," but capable of being proved from the definition of a finite collection.
