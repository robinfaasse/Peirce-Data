Cauchy's Theorem that every group contains a
substitution of the order of each prime factor of the group.
He says Cauchy's demonstration is à peu près the

they are not such in the sense of being drawn from a

Lemma I. If a group [del]of order l[/del] contains two other groups, the number
of its substitutions such that none of them gives the same
product when multiplied by any substitution of the first sub-
group that it does when multiplied into any substitution
of the second subgroup is a multiple of the product of
the orders of those subgroups, zero being regarded as
such a multiple. [ins]of order l[/ins]

That is I being a group (whose substitutions are
[del]U, V, W etc,[/del] let it contain a group (G) of order m
whose substitutions are $g_1, g_2 \dots g_m$ and also a group
(H) of order n whose substitutions are $h_1, h_2 \dots h_n$ Then
the number of [del]substitu[/del] [del]equal the[/del] mn inequalities
$$g_x U_z \neq U_z h_y$$
that are satisfied is divisible by mn [ins]say $U_w$[/ins]

For in the first place is any [del]subst[/del] one of the U's, satisfies
the condition, so also will every substitution formed
by multiplying this by a g and into an h; since if
$$g_x g_u U_w h_v = g_u U_w h_v h_y$$
then
$$g_u^{-1} g_x g_u U_w = U_w h_v h_y h_v^{-1}$$
which since the g's are a group and the h's another
is the same as
$$g_{x'} U_w = U_w h_{y'}$$
contrary to the hypothesis, Moreover the mn substitu-
tions formed by multiplying $g_u U_w h_v$ by a g and into
an h will be all [del]except[/del] unequal. Of course $U_w$ will
itself be one of these, since one of the g's will be 1,
and so will one of the h's.
