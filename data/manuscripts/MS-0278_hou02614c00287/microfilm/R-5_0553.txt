Space has parts, which are [u]Places[/u].
Further, every place may be cut into two smaller
places of the same kind; every space, for example,
into two spaces. No matter how many subdivisions
are made, places of the same kind to be cut are
only multiplied; there is no end to the possible
sections. All places are indefinitely divisible into
like places.

Much more; such is the multitude of the parts of
space, that there are [del]sp[/del] places which no such
subdivision could segregate or isolate. These places
are of a different kind. An ordinary body occupies
a place of like kind with space; we call it a [u]space[/u].
Imagine a pail of water. Let the place the water
occupies be separated into two parts by the [del]freezing[/del] [ins]formation[/ins]
of a lump of ice in the water. Then, besides the
places where there is only water and the places
where there is only ice, there will be between these
a place of another kind, a [u]surface[/u], no more and
no less occupied by ice than by water. No subdivision
of the [del]sp[/del] whole space will ever separate this surface,
or any part of it, from the water-space and from
the ice-space.
