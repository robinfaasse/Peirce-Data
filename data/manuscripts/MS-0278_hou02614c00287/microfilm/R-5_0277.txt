Indeed, most of the old writers, who dealt with "self-evident
[del]truth[/del]" held that nothing capable of demonstration
ought to be called self-evident. Accepting that definition,
we have the following

[u]Theorem[/u]. In [del]most[/del] pure mathematics, whatever is
self-evident is false.

[u]Demonstration[/u]. For the whole business of the mathema-
tician, [ins]as far as it need be considered here,[/ins] is to trace out the consequences of distinct hypotheses.
Now by the consequence of a hypothesis is meant that whose
falsity can be shown to positively contradict the hypothesis. Whatever
can be so shown is the denial of a demonstrable truth; for
such showing constitutes a demonstration [u]per impossibile[/u]. [del]Whatever[/del]
[ins]Hence, whatever[/ins] [del]then[/del] is a consequence [ins]of a hypothesis[/ins] can be proved to be so; but what is
not a consequence of his hypothesis, the mathematician is not
bound to pin himself down to for his purpose, and in his sense,
it is not true; and the recognition of such contingencies, enables
him (we remark in passing) to make generalizations which are
the very life of mathematics.

Propositions which are necessarily true are very frequently first
observed with [ins]our[/ins] surprise in particular cases. They do not appear by any means
evident at the outset. Propositions which appear selfevident usually have a
simple relation to the hypothesis; and if there is any difficulty in proving them
it is most commonly because the hypothesis has not been so framed as quite to
exclude their falsity. Experience justifies suspicion in these cases; but
loose reasoners are apt to deal with the selfevident by the wholesale. Do not
let us be understood to deny that natural beliefs are apt to be approximately right.
