Now as to "trees". In his article in [u]Phil. Mag.[/u], [date]1857[/date],
p. 172, Cayley remarks that the expression
$$A \partial_x + B \partial_y + C \partial_z + \dots$$ ,
where $A, B, C, \dots$ contain $x, y, z$, is of the nature both of
an operator and of an operand. Hence he calls it an
"operandator." Let $P, Q, R$ be any operandators, and
let $U$ be another, or, "to fix the ideas", let $U$ be a mere ope-
rand. Let us define $PU$ as the result of the operation
$P$ in $U$. Then
$$QPU = (Q \times P)U + (QP)U$$
$Q \times P$ denotes the algebraic product of $Q$ and $P$, so that
$Q \times P = P \times Q$. $(QP)U$ denotes that [del]the[/del] the operation $Q$
is performed on $P$, and then, considering the result as an
operator, the operation $(QP)$ is performed on $U$. "Without
a more convenient form of notation, Cayley remarks, it would
be difficult, to find the corresponding expressions for $RQPU$, &c.
"This, however, can be at once effected by means of the
analytical forms called "trees"." ... "the inspection of
