[pn]Solution to Question 1 (244)[/pn]

Let the remainder after dividing $n$ by $D$ be $n'$.
Then if the proposition holds true for $\frac{n'}{D}$ we will prove that it is [del]so[/del]
that is if $\frac{n'}{D} = \frac{1}{p} - \frac{1}{pq} + \&c$
we shall have $\frac{n}{D} = \frac{1}{p} - \frac{1}{pq} + \frac{1}{pqr} - \&c$.
now $\frac{1}{p} - \frac{1}{pq} + \&c = \frac{1}{p} - \frac{1}{p} (\frac{1}{q} - \frac{1}{qr} + \&c)$
$= \frac{1}{p} - \frac{1}{p} \cdot \frac{n'}{D}$
$= \frac{D - n'}{pD}$

But, by definition, $D = np + n'$
$\therefore n = \frac{D - n'}{p}$
Hence $\frac{D - n'}{pD} = \frac{n}{D}$ as we wished to prove.
But the remainders $n, n', \&c$ continually diminish till one of them [del]is[/del]
common measure of $n$ and $D$ and here the proposition is self-evident, theref[del]ore[/del]
in every case.
