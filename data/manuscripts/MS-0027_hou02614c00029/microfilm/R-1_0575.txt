or [u]integrate[/u] whole. The scholastic definition of an [u]integral[/u] whole as a whole which has [u]partem extra partem[/u] is either too narrow or too vague. What I mean is that any of the parts might be removed and still there [del]may[/del] would be a whole of the same general description.

That is, our conception of an integral whole is such that we should recognize every part of it not as an ultimate part but as [del]a[/del] [ins]a[/ins] whole of the same kind. Thus the conception of an [u]integral[/u] whole does not stand alone, but involves the conceptions of other wholes. Consequently, an object cannot be defined as an integral whole of N parts without our defining $2^N$ different integral wholes [ins](including nothing)[/ins]. This is very important. In this respect it is more complicated than an ordinal whole, which only contemplates N wholes. The partial wholes need not be singly defined but the definition of the entire whole must be such that the [del]being[/del] separate being of each partial whole shall follow from it by logical necessity.

A collection is not only an integral whole but it has ultimate, or individual parts, or members.

Moreover the collection is constituted by the mere [del]being[/del] existence of the individual parts, or members. That is to say it is the same collection no matter what else ceases to be true of the members except that each exists. But if any member ceases to exist, the collection becomes a different collection.
