[u]Another thing.[/u] There are certain personal views of mine
which I cannot compromise about at all, no matter how others
may view them, but must and will express in season and out.
They are matters of scientific ethics. Such a question, in particular,
is that of terminology. The terminology of logic is on the whole,
most excellent, exact, and generally accepted. It is absolutely
essential to the progress of science that different investigators
should work together harmoniously. One thing which is very important
to that end is that they should use technical terms in the same
senses. That they [ins]logicians[/ins] do, in the main; for their terminology is mostly
that of the very precise scholastics. But those terms do not
suffice. Kant's practice was to take a scholastic term and use
it in an [del]analogous[/del] [ins]analogous[/ins] sense, wide enough [del]apart[/del] away from
the original [del]one[/del] to avoid confusion, and to prefix [del]one[/del] when
convenient, his "transcendental." That proved quite convenient,
but it can hardly be carried much further. The experience
of those sciences which have the most difficulty with their terminology,
taxonomic zoölogy & botany, ought to be utilized by other sciences.
They have found that the only way of securing conformity is to
allow the man who introduces a conception as a scientific one
to give it a name, provided he fulfils certain essential condi-
tions, and provides it is not preoccupied. Then all other workers
are bound to express that conception by that name only, and
[del]to[/del] use that name for that conception only. I think that that [del]idea[/del]
rule ought to be adopted in philosophy. I think it a breach of
