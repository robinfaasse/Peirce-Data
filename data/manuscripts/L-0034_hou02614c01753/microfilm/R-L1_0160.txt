But if it be meant that new propositions are not to be inserted, allow me to point out a great difference between logic and psychology. Namely, the majority of the original statements I should be apt to make are not "views." They are propositions absolutely indisputable. The very man who denies them in general terms, when [del]he is[/del] confronted with the case, cannot deny that. For instance, am I to say, with "accepted opinion" that a universal proposition cannot follow from a particular one? I ask the average professor whether this proposition is universal or particular:
Some woman is adored by all catholics.
He will reply, particular, and truly, though for a wretched reason. I ask him whether this proposition is universal or particular:
Every catholic adores a woman.
He will reply universal. I ask him whether the second does not follow from the first. He will wriggle out of it by saying "No: only extralogically." He can't deny that it does follow. But he will go on teaching, without qualification, that a universal proposition cannot follow from a particular one. So about inference from two negatives, and innumerable other doctrines.

If you had wanted a person to do the logic who would represent "generally accepted views" in any wide sense, I am surely about the last man you would have called upon. But you, being a scientific psychologist yourself, naturally have a desire to imprint the scientific character upon your dictionary, knowing that if it is not today "generally received" it will be before your work loses its vogue, and knowing too that an important dictionary can do more to further scientific ideas than any treatise or plea can do.
