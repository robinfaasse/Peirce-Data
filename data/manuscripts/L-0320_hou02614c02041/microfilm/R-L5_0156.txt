January 19. 1901

Dear Mr. Peirce.

For six years I have given absolutely my whole time to the solution of the greatest problem in science - that of Space and its 'motions'. Standing at the same time in two streams of influence I have been driven with historic fatality to a conception moulded from the best both in philosophy and in science. Yet while I feel the main conception must be correct I find it a discouraging task to work out all the details which the revolutionary notion demands; and I would never consent to publish till I had actually worked out all of the essential details.

It seems a frightful thing to give ones whole life to a notion that [u]may[/u] be wrong. A true scientist always fears; yet fearing 'On Notions of Number and Space'. Royce, who is no friend of mine, uses it in his classes for reference. Münsterberg writes me it "is a very great work". And James says it "is the best piece of [del]supernatural[/del] [ins]superb[/ins] work ever done at Harvard" - meaning psychological work. Yet between you and me this book is extremely unsatisfactory and only gets praise from the above men and neglect from others from the same reason: namely, none of them understand in the least its fundamental reach, or really know if it is good or bad.

The main thought I ought to outline to you before asking you to give further time to it. Historically it might well have been suggested by the declaration of Maxwell that concerning the ether theory of light that "any other corresponding order of changes may replace the spatial changes or 'motions' of this fluid; and may do this without violating, in any way, the essential principles required of such a fluid by modern physics" Couple with this a full appreciation of Schelling and a [del]somewhat[/del] radically modified mind stuff conception [del]from[/del] [ins]that[/ins] of either Herbert or Clifford and you catch sight of my starting point.
