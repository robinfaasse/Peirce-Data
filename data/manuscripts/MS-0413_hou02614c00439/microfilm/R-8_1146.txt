* [u]Conversio[/u] is the word chosen by Appuleius to translate the ἀντιστροφή of Aristotle. Some ancient logicians distinguished between ἀναστροφή, conversion, and ἀντιστροφή, contraposition; and many logicians refuse to recognize contraposition as a kind of conversion, because the terms of the conclusion are the negatives of the terms of the [del]premise[/del] [ins]proposition[/ins]. But this is a wooden way of treating logic. [del]What[/del] Definitions must bend to the exigencies of natural classification.

Simple conversion, the [del]apung[/del] [u]conversio simplex[/u] of Boethius, was, I doubt not, borrowed from the Greek; though I believe [del]it is not to been[/del] [ins]the[/ins] expression has not been noticed in Greek earlier than the too much despised Joannes Philoponus ([u]Scholium on Arist.[/u] 25 a 5) in the seventh century.

The term [u]conversion by accident[/u] is intolerable. I prefer to call it, [ins]or rather what I deal with in place of it,[/ins] [del]paractic[/del] [ins]lectic[/ins] contraposition. What is called conversion by accident is conversion which changes the [del]lexis[/del] [ins]lectic[/ins] [del]paradosis[/del]. What I call [del]paractic[/del] [ins]lectic[/ins] contraposition changes the [del]lexis[/del] [ins]lectic[/ins] [del]paradosis[/del] of the predicate as well as that of the subject, just as ordinary contraposition changes the [del]phrasis[/del] [ins]phasis[/ins] [del]captasis[/del] of the subject as well as that of the predicate.
