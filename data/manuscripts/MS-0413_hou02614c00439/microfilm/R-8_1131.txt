It is a blunder on Aristotle's part, [del]as remarked in a[/del]
[del]previous chapter[/del] [ins]merely[/ins], to call the propositions A and E
contraries, because they may both be false, but not
both true. They ought to be called [u]incongruous[/u] or [u]disparates[/u], and
both these terms are somewhat in use. [u]Subcontraries[/u] (a
word of Boethius, imitating the [greek]ὑπεναντία[/greek] of Ammonius)
are propositions of opposite ecphasis but, being particular,
both can be [del]false[/del] true, though both cannot be false. It
would be well to [del]im[/del] follow the usage of those writers who
call any two propositions which can logically both be true but
not both false [u]subcontraries[/u]. [u]Contradictories[/u] (Aristotle's [greek]ἀντικείμενα[/greek].
The word contradictoria comes from Boethius) are propositions which
cannot both be true nor both false, but precisely deny one
another. [u]Subaltern[/u] (word found in the [del]Introduction to Translation of[/del]
Porphyry's Isagoge by Marius Victorinus in the 4th century. Porphyry's
word is [greek]ὑπάλληλον[/greek] [del]means[/del] but in the present sense first found
in Boethius) is a particular proposition which follows by an
immediate inference from its corresponding universal to which
it is [del]first[/del] [ins]said[/ins] to be subaltern.

contradictories. All other pairs of propositions may be true
