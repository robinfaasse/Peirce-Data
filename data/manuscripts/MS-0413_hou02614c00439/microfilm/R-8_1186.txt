§ 144. The terms of the premises that are not eliminated
should have the same distributions in the conclusion as in
the premises. The violation of this rule is [u]the fallacy of changed[/u]
[u]distribution[/u].

§ 145. The [del]degree of particularity[/del] [ins]degree of particularity[/ins] of a proposition is the number
of individuals which the proposition asserts to exist by virtue
of its form. Thus, the degree of particularity of the proposition
[u]Every triangle has three sides[/u],
is [del]zero[/del] [ins]zero[/ins]; for a universal proposition is not to be understood as
per se asserting that anything really exists. The degree of
particularity of the proposition
[u]Some triangle has three sides[/u],
is [del]one[/del] [ins]one[/ins]. For though four things are asserted to exist, the triangle
and the three sides, yet the three sides are only asserted to exist by
an accident, or material circumstance, not by the form of the
proposition. The degree of particularity of the proposition
[u]Some man is not some man[/u],
is [del]two[/del] [ins]two[/ins].

The degree of particularity of the conclusion of a syllogism
ought to be the sum of those of the premises; and any departure
from this rule is a [u]fallacy of erroneous particularization[/u].

The student should practice examinations of syllogisms in
about a hundred examples, until he can instantly state what
the character of an argument is, that is, if sound what the
name of the mood is, and if unsound what the nature of the
fallacy is.
