There is no difficulty in conceiving this; although,
owing to numerous associations of ideas, it is
necessary to devote some weeks to a careful study
of the matter before it becomes perfectly clear. Accord-
ing, geometrical propositions and arithmetical propositions
stand upon altogether different ground. The footing of
logical principles is intermediate between these. The
logician does not assert anything, as the geometrician
does; but there are certain [del]matters of fact[/del] [ins]assumed truths[/ins] which he
hopes for, relies upon, banks upon, in a way [ins]quite[/ins] foreign
to the arithmetician. Logic teaches us to expect some
residue of dreaminess in the world, and even self-contradic-
tions; but we do not expect to be brought face to face with
any such phenomenon, and at any rate [del]we[/del] are forced
to run the risk of it. The assumptions of logic differ
from those of geometry, not merely in not being assertori-
cally held, but also in being much less definite.
