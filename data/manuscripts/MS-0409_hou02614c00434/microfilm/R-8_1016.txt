<u>1.</u> Take any two things, A and C; then a thing, B, can be so chosen
that if A and C are men, B is a man praised by A to C.
<u>2.</u> Take anything, A; then a thing, B, can be so chosen, that whatever
third thing, C, be taken, if A and C are men, and B is a man
praised by A to C.
<u>3.</u> Take anything C; then a thing, B, can be so chosen, that
whatever third thing, A, be taken, if A and C are men, B is a
man praised by A to C.
<u>4.</u> A thing, B, can be so chosen that whatever things A and C
may be, if A and C are men, B is a man praised by A to
C.

We should usually express these as follows:
1. Every man praises some man or other to each man.
2. Every man praises some man to all men.
3. To every man some man is praised by all men.
4. There is a man whom all men praise to all men.

The words <u>universal</u> and <u>particular</u> are applied to propo-
sitions according as they refer to their subjects universally,
or [del]we[/del] [ins]particularly[/ins], essentially as above, in the ordinary
treatises on logic. The terms are found in Appuleius, the
author of the Golden Ass. The Greek terms are προτάσεις
αἱ καθόλου and προτάσεις αἱ ἐν μέρει.
