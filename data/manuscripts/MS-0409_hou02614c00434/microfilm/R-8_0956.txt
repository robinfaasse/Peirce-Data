§60. Besides the principal motive to the study of logic, namely, to learn to reason [del]well[/del] [ins]soundly[/ins] and cleverly, there is another motive, which is, to become acquainted with the ways of thinking that are used by the majority of men, as well as with the meanings of current phrases relating to reasonings.

The traditional logic divides operations of the Understanding (or thinking powers) into Simple Apprehension, Judgment, and Reasoning, which are the operations of thinking names or [u]terms[/u], [u]propositions[/u], and [u]argumentations[/u], there being the three kinds of symbols with which logic deals. A mental name, or what one thinks when one thinks of the meaning of a name, is called a [u]concept[/u]. A mental proposition is called a [u]judgment[/u]. An argumentation within one's own mind, by which the thinker is more or less inclined to believe something, or by which he sees that something must be true, is called an [u]inference[/u].

[u]Note[/u]. The English word [u]Understanding[/u] translates Latin [u]intellectus[/u], and in the best usage [del]is[/del] applies to all thinking power. Kant endeavored to limit the German [u]Verstand[/u], translating [u]intellectus[/u], to simple apprehension. This, like all Kant's views of logic, was [del]rather[/del] pretty shallow. It [del]arose[/del] [ins]came[/ins] from his strong leaning toward threefold divisions.
