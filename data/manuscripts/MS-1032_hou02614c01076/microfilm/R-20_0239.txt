[u]Acetylene[/u]

[u]§ 2. Combustion of acetylene.[/u]

Davy in 1836 found that acetylene was readily inflammable.

In 1860 April 30 Berthelot (Comptes rendus LI. 805) mentions that the flame is smoky.

In 1874 E. von Meyer found that if there is not enough oxygen to completely burn the acetylene all the oxygen goes to form carbonic oxide. And the same thing happens with ethelene. [It follows that the smoke is due to the decomposition of the acetylene by heat; and this supports the carbon theory of the luminosity of flames.]

1895 Dec 30 Henri Le Chatelier presented to the French Academy of Sciences (Comptes rendus CXXI. 1144) a memoir on the combustion of acetylene. He says that mixtures of acetylene and hair having less that 7.74 % by volume of C₂H₂ burn to carbonic anhydride, CO₂, and water H₂O, giving a yellowish dim flame.

For percentages ranging from 7.74 to 17.37 the flame is pale blue with a feeble yellowish aureola. The products are CO₂, CO, H₂O, and H. The proportion depends on the temperature centigrade t° according to the equation
log [fraction](Vol of H₂O) (Vol CO) / (Vol of H) (Vol of CO₂)[/fraction] = 5 - [fraction]5000° / 273° + t°[/fraction]

If I understand him rightly, he means that the reaction
C₂H₂ + O₂ = 2CO + H₂
is accompanied by the reaction
2C₂H₂ + 5O₂ = 4CO₂ + 2H₂O

With percentages above 17.37 by volume of acetylene, the products are CO, H₂, C, C₂H₂. When there is more than 20% by volume of acetylene, the precipitation of carbon becomes very shortly marked, and then the flame first becomes luminous. It is also red and smoky and more and more so as the combustible gas augments. After the passage of the flame there is a dark cloud of precipitated soot.
