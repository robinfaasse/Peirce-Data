In the first place, it is quite certain that all number and all
quantity of a single parameter, of which in general number is
the expression, is ordinal. What things are related in a
form of relation like that of [del]diff[/del] discrete points on a
line which does not branch may usefully have number
applied to them. Plainly, then, number can itself express
nothing but serial order. What Cantor calls ordinal
numbers are only a special variety of ordinals, which
may justly be [del]termed[/del] [ins]called[/ins] [u]Cantorian ordinals[/u]; and the
kind of series he calls "wohl-geordnet" ought to be termed
a Cantorian series. Its "well"-ness consists simply in
its being adapted to the ideas he had in view. There is nothing
in the nature of number to forbid its application to states
of things, or systems, in which two different numbers appear
as equal; as when we count a finite collection of objects
arranged cyclically, like the days of the week. But
in [del]themselves[/del] [ins]themselves[/ins] 1 and 8, 2 and 9, are different. Now
my question is whether it be so or not with $\theta$i and t.
In order to reach a really sound answer to such a question
one must be careful not to stick too closely to the point.
