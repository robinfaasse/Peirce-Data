You will see, from what I have said, that a probability, in the sense of the Doctrine of Chances, whatever phrase may be used to describe it (and the phrases in use are highly elliptical and extremely deceptive) does not relate to any single [del]event of[/del] [ins]or supposed[/ins] fact, but relates to a [u]species[/u] of possible events in connexion with a [u]genus[/u] of possibilities that includes that species. Let this sink into your soul. Although a probability is not strictly the quotient of the number of units in the species divided by the number of units in the genus, nor is in the strict mathematical sense the [u]limit[/u] toward which [del]that[/del] [ins]such[/ins] a quotient [del]converges[/del] when its dividend and divisor increase indefinitely, yet it is of the general nature of such a limit, and of such a quotient. And it is a real ("objective") state of things.

Two probabilities cannot be added to get the probability of one or the other event, unless the two species of events are strictly mutually exclusive. For instance given the probability that a letter will occur in one place, and the probability that the same or another letter will occur in another place, you must not simply add the two probabilities to get the probability that one or the other kind of event will occur.

If [u]p[/u] is the probability that a kind of event P will occur under stated circumstances, and [u]q[/u] is the probability that under the same genus of circumstances, if P occurs then another species of event Q will also occur, then p x q is the probability that [u]both[/u] will occur under the same genus of circumstances. And if it be [del]known[/del] [ins]at each[/ins] that Q has the same probability, [u]q[/u], of occurring under those circumstances, whether P occurs or not, then, and then only, the probability of their both occurring under the same genus of circumstances is p x q.
