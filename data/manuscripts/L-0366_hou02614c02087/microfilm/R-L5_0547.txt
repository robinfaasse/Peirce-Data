how little the attention of logicians
has been called to this question, and
how weak most of the attempts to
answer it have been. Suppose that
at any game of chance at each turn
of a card, a wheel, or anything of the
sort, Red (R) and Black (B) are
alone possible and are equally likely
to appear. Then the probability of
any sequence of ten such events,
such as
R R R B B R B R B R
is [ins]($\frac{1}{1024}$)[/ins] and this is equally probable
with any other sequence of ten, since
red and black are equally probable.
To be sure it is more probable that
the red and black will be in
approximately equal numbers than
that they will be very unequal; but
that is only because there are more
single sequences that are nearly equal.
There is only one arrangement in which
all are black and one in which all are
red. There are 20 in which all but one
are of one color and 1 of the other; 90 in

which 2 are of one color and 80 of the other; 240 in which 3 are of one
color and 7 of the other; 420 in which 4 are of one color and 6 of
the other, and 252 in which just 5 are of one color and 5 of the
other. Any sequence of 20 throws brings an arrangement which
will not occur again in more than a million trials in the long run;
and yet there is nothing surprising or calling for any particular
explanation. I repeat, then, [del]the[/del] [ins]the[/ins] question: what [u]is[/u] surprising and what
