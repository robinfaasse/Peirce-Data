Milford Pa [date]1909 June 25[/date]

My dear cousin Jo:

When we work the calculus of probabilities men endeavour to make the initial probabilities of a simpler nature than those you adopt. For example, I should take a thousand ten-syllable lines, scattered through the plays of Shakespeare, and should count the number of F's, of c's, and of B's in those thousand lines. As for the other letters of the name Francis Bacon, which occur in nearly every line, I should content myself with counting the lines, among the thousand, in which each did not occur. For, as I understand it, the autograph is supposed to be found by taking the B in one line, the a in the next, the c in the next and so on.

I do not think, myself, that any such method is a suitable one for answering such a question. So applied, the whole theory of probabilities is, in my opinion, fallacious. It is all right as long as one sticks to dice and such things, where it is known how often each side will turn up. But when it is used to ascertain the probability of a hypothesis, the reasoning is deceptive. For this reasoning is supposed to be mathematical. Now mathematical reasoning can conclude nothing that is not implicitly said in the data, the mathematical operations only serving to direct attention to it. But it is certainly not said in Shakespearean writings that Bacon is their author;—not I mean, in the sense in which it is said in saying that a train goes forty miles an hour
