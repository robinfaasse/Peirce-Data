I have pasted on this sheet a bit of squared
paper on which the little crosses are simply
mistakes to be altogether disregarded. The two
brown lines I have drawn are the axes of $x$ and $y$.
The fine black dots lie on the curve $y = fx = x^2 - 2$
The blue dots are at heights above the axis of $x$ equal
to the increase of $y$ [del]per unit[/del] (for the black dots) per
unit of $x$ at the same value of $x$, i.e. on the
same vertical. That is to say, the blue dots
have $y = D_x fx$, $fx$ being always $x^2 - 2$.

[fig]Graph with plotted points on grid paper[/fig]

When a symbol for an operation, such
as $D_x$ has an exponent, that exponent
expresses how many times over the
operation is performed. For example,
$D_x^2 fx$ means $D_x (D_x fx)$ and $D_x^3 fx$ means
$D(D^2 fx)$, etc.

We have the habit of writing $D_x^n f0$
to mean that $D_x^n fx$ is to be algebraically
expressed in terms of $x$ and then $x$ is to be put equal to
zero. For example when $fx = x^2 - 2$, $D_x fx = 2x$, and $D_x^2 fx = 2$.
Consequently [del]if having this meaning[/del] $D_x f0 = 0$, but $D_x^2 f0 = 2$.
[ins]But it seems to me more consistent to write $D_x^0 f0$ instead of $D_x^0 f0$, and I shall do so.[/ins]

Now Maclaurin's theorem is, that for ordinary functions, which
are expressible in a series according to powers of $x$,
$$fa = f0 + a D_x f0 + a^2 \frac{D_x^2 f0}{2!} + a^3 \frac{D_x^3 f0}{3!} + a^4 \frac{D_x^4 f0}{4!} + \text{etc. ad infinitum.}$$

Now I must mention that if $F$ and $\Phi$ [ins]and $\Psi$[/ins] are two symbols of complex
operations [del]ends[/del] composed only of [del]additions[/del] additions, multiplications, and
raising to positive integral powers of [del]nothing[/del] but such compounds of
$D$ and constant numbers, then $(F \Phi)D = F(\Phi D)$ and $(F + \Phi)\Psi = $
$F\Psi + \Phi\Psi$ and in short the rules of algebra generally hold good.
