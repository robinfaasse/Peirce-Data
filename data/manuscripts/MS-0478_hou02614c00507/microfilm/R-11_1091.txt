Ethics must appeal to Esthetics in forming its concep-
tion of the [u]summum bonum[/u]; and Logic, as the science
of controlled thought, which is but a species of controlled
conduct, [del]the latter[/del] [ins]it[/ins] must rest upon the science of such
conduct. [del]No[/del] [ins]A[/ins] logic which does not recognize its relations
to Ethics must be fatally unsound in its Methodeutic,
if not in its Critic.

Logic, which [del]began[/del] [ins]historically and in each individual still begins,[/ins] with the wish to distinguish
good and bad reasonings, developes into a general
theory of signs. Its three departments are the physi-
ological, or [u]Speculative Grammar[/u]; its classificatory
part, judging particularly what reasoning is good and
what bad, or [u]Logical Critic[/u]; and finally, [u]Methoden-[/u]
[u]tic[/u], or the principles of the production of valuable
courses of research, and exposition.

In this syllabus, only Speculative Grammar will
be touched upon.
