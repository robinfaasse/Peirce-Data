It must be remembered that my doctrine that quantity
belongs only to the subject, requires that when the predicate
is made subject and thus quantified it must at the same
time be supplied with a predicate. In conversion this
is done by making the subject predicate and so unquanti-
fying it.

Rules for the quantification of the predicate in conversion.

1 Affirmative predicates can only be quantified indefinitely

The reason is simple. In the praejacent proposition we [del]represent[/del]
[ins]a[/ins] predicate only within the subject and not at all without
the subject; hence it is entirely unlimited, and no represen-
tion represents the same fact which represents it as limited.

It has been said that the [del]converter[/del] subjacent has a peculiar
quantity "at least some." But this is not true. There are
only two quantities considered - Universal and Particular. If
we assert the particular, we do not assert the Universal. The
remark seems to contemplate "None" as a third quantity but
this does not belong to the quantity of the subject. Thus if I
convert [del]some[/del] "All griffins are animals" into "Some animals
are griffins" these some may as well be none as not. When
I say "All griffins are animals" I not only do not say that grif-
ins are actual, I do not say that they are conceivable, or have
any reality whatever, [del]so which[/del] animals can be quantified in order
to introduce a distinction between some and none [del]or[/del] i.e.
