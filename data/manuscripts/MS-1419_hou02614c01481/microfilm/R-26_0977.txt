We do not think it [del]is even[/del] needful to expatiate upon how well
Mr. Ford has treated the literary side of Franklin; because that
will be taken for granted by the whole public. The scientific side
is less well done. To begin with, the mosaic art does not lend
itself very well to this subject: and then Mr. Ford does not
sufficiently distinguish between the inventor and the scientific
discoverer. Thus, he speaks of the Franklin stove and the lightning
rod as important discoveries. He quotes, apparently with ap-
proval, at any rate without a jeer, Jefferson's stricture upon
the chemists of his day as not sufficiently confining their
attention to [del]home[/del] matters of human utility. That is, he
would have had Lavoisier, Scheele, and Priestley, tread the
pathway of Boerhaave, and Lemery, and the Cadets, who were
a sort of apothecaries. [del]He does not[/del] conveys not the dimmest
notion of what the nature and extent of Franklin's scientific
merits in the matter of electricity may have been. He tells
us something about [del]his[/del] [ins]Franklin's[/ins] studies of the Gulf Stream and of the
effect of oil upon ripples and waves, from which we
gather that they were of the slenderest and most weakly
amateurish description. Is this just? Does this fairly
represent Franklin's natural philosophy? [ins]How did his scientific powers compare with those of [del]Rumford[/del] the other Massachusetts boy, Rumford?[/ins] To get these
questions answered, the reader must go to other books.
