[u]The Many-Sided Franklin[/u]. By Paul Leicester Ford.
New York: The Century Co. [date]1899[/date]. 8[sup]vo[/sup] pp. 516.

Mr. Ford applies to our great diplomatist, politician, wit, moralist, and natural philosopher that same method of characterism he lately applied [ins]so successfully[/ins] to Washington, and which in a general way had already been applied, for example, by Waliszewski to Catherine II and still better by Lévy to Napoleon. That is to say, he considers Franklin under a dozen different aspects in as many separate chapters. As the progress of psychology gradually imparts to biography a greater scientific seriousness, this method is sure to be more and more employed and improved. Its merits are not exclusively scientific, however. It not only enables the reader to gain a more intimate acquaintance with a great man than any mere narrative of the events of his life could confer, but it makes far more interesting reading. Mr. Ford refrains from anything like an attempt at a psychological explanation of Franklin's achievements. He indulges in no other generalizations than everybody uses in speaking of any person's character. So the work is rather a conveniently arranged assortment of facts to serve as a basis for a thorough study of Franklin than an [del]attempt at a[/del] essay towards a unitary conception of his mental constitution.
