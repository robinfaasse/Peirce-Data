A heavy horizontal line, called an [u]obelus[/u], written over a proposition, has the effect of negativing it, that is, of precisely denying it, and of so asserting its negative, which is that proposition which is true if the original proposition is false, and not [del]otherwise[/del] true, unless [del]that statem[/del] condition necessitates its truth.

There are certain predicates of the meaning of which [del]logic[/del] [ins]the logician[/ins] is obliged to take cognizance, while of ordinary predicates [del]it[/del] [ins]he[/ins] knows only what is given in the premises. Since these may come up in any discussion, the characters which are to [del]repre[/del]stand for them ought to be determined in the general conventions which determines what the other signs of [del]logic[/del] [ins]the algebra of logic[/ins] are to be. The Arabic figures [ins]of numeration[/ins] may conveniently be used for one moiety of these, while their negatives, constituting the other moiety, are denoted each of them by such a figure with an obelus above it and attached to it. Since the appropriateness of the Arabic figures to this purpose is, in one sense, accidental, — namely, since [del]these[/del] [ins]the system of[/ins] predicates does not essentially involve the the idea of numeration, but it is, on the contrary, the idea of numeration which [del]essentially[/del] requires that [ins]certain[/ins] of the numbers shall coincide with these predicates, — it is proper that special shapes shall be given to the figures when they are used [del]in this sense[/del]; and these shapes may appropriately be of a markedly bare and simple character, as if stripped of something which belongs to them as [del]figures[/del] of numeration.
