[u]On Multitude.[/u]

[u]Art. 1.[/u] Mathematics is a study of exact hypotheses, in so far as consequences can be deduced from them. To limit mathematics to the deduction of those consequences would be to separate from it some of the greatest of the achievements of modern mathematicians, [del]and an[/del] achievements which nobody but mathematicians could have performed, — such as the formation of the idea of the system of imaginaries, and of the idea of Riemann surfaces. It must be allowed, therefore, that the formation of [del]the[/del] [ins]its[/ins] hypotheses is a part of the business of mathematics.

[u]Art. 2.[/u] The purpose of the present paper is to inquire what grades of multitude of collections are [del]purely[/del] mathematically possible, that is are possible as purely ideal, but exact hypotheses.

I can hardly contend that this inquiry comes within the scope of mathematics, although it must be conducted chiefly by means of mathematical reasoning. But it is not the usage of mathematicians to [del]inquire[/del] [ins]investigate[/ins] the possibility of fundamental hypotheses; they content themselves with adopting such hypotheses as are manifestly possible. If they inquire into the possibility of [ins]a[/ins] secondary hypothesis, [del]they do[/del] their problem is to determine whether or not it is compatible with a fundamental hypothesis. An inquiry like the present, not merely requires, like all mathematics a strict [u]logica utens[/u], but it must be based on the principles, or must develop new principles of [u]logica docens[/u].
