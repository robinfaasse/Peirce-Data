[u]Art. 3[/u]. It is requisite, in the first place, to define a collection. Now, it is impossible to give a genetic definition without taking for granted, at the outset, all the main results of the investigation. We must, therefore, content ourselves with the following:

A [u]collection[/u] is whatever stands to a general predicate of single subjects in a certain relation [u]sui generis[/u], such that for every such predicate there is a [ins]single[/ins] collection and for every collection there is such a predicate.

Of predicates some [del]are[/del] have single subjects and are expressible by intransitive verbs, [del]some[/del] [ins]while others[/ins] have plural sets of subjects and are expressed by verbs requiring in our languages a direct object and perhaps a certain [del]fixed number[/del] set of indirect objects. These objects are essentially subjects, and there is no one of the great families of human speech [del]as[/del] which does not embrace languages which habitually put the subject of a sentence into an oblique case, thus contrasting it no more with the other objects than these are contrasted with one another. Among the living European languages, the Gaelic is an example in point.

To say that a collection is in a [del]relation[/del] one-to-one relation [u]sui generis[/u] to a predicate of single subjects amounts to saying that it is such a predicate spoken of with a peculiar phraseology. For a relation [u]sui generis[/u] is indefinable, and an indefinable relation has no meaning.
