If a letter (neither $\Pi$ nor $\Sigma$) has subscripts attached to it, the letter with its subscripts asserts that [del]the[/del] [ins]a[/ins] particular predicate signified by the letter on the line is true of [del]the[/del] individuals denoted by those subscripts in their order. Thus, if $l$ signifies "— loves —," then $l_{ab}$ means "$a$ loves $b$." It must be clearly understood that the letter on the line refers to a class of ordered sets of individuals, where the word "class" is used to include the case in which there is no such ordered set[del]s[/del], as well as the case in which every possible such ordered set is comprised in the class. For example, the letter on the line cannot mean [del]"several[/del] "— loves every[ins]one each of which[/ins]body but —," because this does not refer to a class of pairs [ins]the first member[/ins] loves everybody but the second member.

The full expression of a general proposition in the General Algebra of logic consists of two parts one written to the right of the other. The left hand part, [del]called the [u]Peircian[/u],[/del] [ins]which I will call the Anonyman until a better [del]name[/del] is bestowed upon it,[/ins] prescribes how that set of individuals may be found which are denoted by subscripts that are not individual [ins]or[/ins] proper names. The right hand part, [del]called the Boolian,[/del] — [del]and[/del] it will be the whole in case all the subscripts are proper names, — is called the Boolian. It asserts whatever is asserted of [del]that[/del] [ins]each[/ins] single set of [del]pro[/del] [ins]individuals[/ins] which may be denoted by the subscripts.
