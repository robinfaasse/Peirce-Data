[u]Art. 5.[/u] I shall here make use of the General Algebra of Logic.
So much of the principles of this algebra as is needed in every demonstra-
tion shall here be set forth briefly, followed by a few other matters
which it will be convenient for the student to have collected together.

[del]The Notation of the General Algebra of Logic.[/del]

[u]Notation of the General Algebra of Logic.[/u] Every letter [del]written alone[/del]
without subscript letters nor being itself a subscript letter signifies a
proposition.

If a letter [del], other than [u]$\Pi$[/u] or [u]$\Sigma$[/u],[/del] has subscript letters attached to it,
those subscripts denote individual objects existing in the universe
of discourse. If the universe of discourse is of an ideal nature, the
subscripts denote objects having such ideal existence. If the universe
of discourse embraces everything which is logically possible, the
subscripts are objects which could exist without involving contra-
diction. Greek letters are used to denote predicates, including self-contra-
dictory predicates and absolutely meaningless predicates. The universe
of predicates to which these letters refer, will in the absence of any
special proviso, be included as a part of the general universe of
discourse to which the lower case Italic subscripts refer. Subscripts
other than Greek or Lower case Italics, refer to special universes of
discourse. The same discourse may relate to several universes.
