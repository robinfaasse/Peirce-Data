[pn]7[/pn] The nitrates are, of course, soluble; for most nitrates are so.
$\text{KNO}_3$ is called "saltpeter," or "niter." There is nothing to choose
between these words. Perhaps niter is considered more elegant,
saltpeter more commercial. Yet historically saltpeter goes back
to the Latin translation of Geber ([u]sal petrae[/u]), which is the
oldest antiquity of Western chemistry; [del]Nitrum, or[/del] while
nitrum [ins]is[/ins], properly speaking, an entirely different salt, $\text{K}_2\text{CO}_3$.
Saltpeter crystallizes in two forms, both anhydrous. The usual
form [del]consists of a[/del] rhombic prism simulating a regular [del]tri[/del]
hexagonal prism with pyramidal terminations, though owing
to the suppression of certain faces, this simulation is not
ordinarily obvious. The other form, which arises from slow
evaporation, is [del]a[/del] rhombohedron isomorphous with the Sodium
nitrate. The sp. Gr is 2.1, the melting point $352^\circ$. 100 parts of
water at $t^\circ$ Centigrade dissolve $13.3 + 0.535t + .01802t^2$ parts of [del]the[/del]
Saltpeter. The solution is accompanied with great destruction of heat, so
that the solution in [del]6 parts of water[/del] pretty cold water lowers the tempe-
rature about $10^\circ\text{C}$.
