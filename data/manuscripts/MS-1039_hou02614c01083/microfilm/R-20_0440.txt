[u]K Rb Cs[/u]

There are three elements whose atomic weights, atomic volumes, and melting points are as follows:

| | | At. Wt. [ins](O=15.96)[/ins] | $\Delta$ | At. Vol. | Melts |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Potassium | K | 39.03 | [ins]46.2[/ins] | 44.9 | 62.5 |
| Rubidium | Rb | 85.2 | [ins]47.5[/ins] | 56.1 | 38.5 |
| Caesium | Cs | 132.7 | | 70.6 | 26.5 $\pm$ |

They are all widely distributed but Rb and Cs only in minute quantities; so that our acquaintance with them is comparatively slight.

No other elements have such large atomic volumes in the solid state. They all soften considerably below their melting points.

They are lustrous silvery metals. K has a greenish blue tinge (rich purple by transmitted light) while Rb is yellowish. The vapor of K is green, that of Rb blue.

They all oxidize rapidly in ordinary air; but K, at least, does not if the air is perfectly dry.

They all energetically decompose cold water [del]forming[/del] [ins]evolving[/ins] H₂ and forming solutions of their hydrates KOH, RbOH, CsOH. These hydrates cannot be [del]reduced to[/del] dehydrated by heat.

They are the three most electropositive bodies known, Cs the most so, [del]and[/del] Rb next, and K the third. They form salts with all acids, which are, with few exceptions, soluble in water. Each forms but one [del]series[/del] [ins]type[/ins] of simple salts with monobasic acids, as KCl, and KNO₃. They form very few basic salts, and no oxyhalides.

Their halides are very stable [ins]when[/ins] heated. They crystallize in anhydrous cubes. Their chlorides are insoluble in [ins]an[/ins] anhydrous mixture of ether and alcohol.
