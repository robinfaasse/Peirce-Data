Chemistry

The word substance is used in a special sense in chemistry, which has become its most usual meaning in ordinary speech. The chemists use the word body in the same peculiar sense. Properly, a substance is anything having real being [u]per se[/u] except so far as it may be dependent upon the Creator; and a [u]body[/u] is a substance of the physical world. But in chemistry any two things having inertia and weight are said to be composed of the same "substance" or "body" if their smallest sensible parts have the same physical properties or if there be satisfactory evidence that their molecules are either alike or only differ as [del]the[/del] molecules are apt to be made temporarily to differ by the action of pressure and heat, by crystallization, etc., or by ordinary solution [del]pro[/del] from which the dissolved matter is recoverable by evaporation, cold, the admixture of another menstruum, etc. Thus, [u]ice[/u], [u]water[/u], and [u]steam[/u], or aqueous vapor, are chemically the same substance. So are [u]diamond[/u] and [u]animal black[/u], the former being merely the crystalline form of the latter. So [ins]are[/ins] [u]rock salt[/u] and [u]salt[/u] dissolved in water. But [u]quick-lime[/u] and [u]slaked lime[/u] are different substances. There are some cases in which substances transformable into one another are called the same substance simply from habit, although the progress of chemistry has shown that their molecules are really different. Thus, [u]ozone[/u] is generally said to be the same substance as ordinary [u]oxygen[/u], although we know that three molecules of ordinary oxygen make two of ozone.
