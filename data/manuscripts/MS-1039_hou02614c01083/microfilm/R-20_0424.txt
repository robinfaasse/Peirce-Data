An [u]elementary substance[/u] is a substance the molecules of which, [del]so far as we have been able to discover,[/del] contain but one kind of matter [ins][del]or, perhaps it would be better to say, are not composed of separable parts of different weights or other characters[/del][/ins]. The kind of matter composing an elementary substance is called a [u]chemical element[/u]. The elements of the chemist supersede the Four Elements of Empedocles, Fire, Water, Earth, and Air, each of which, according to Aristotle, contributes two of the four qualities, [u]hot[/u], [u]cold[/u], [u]moist[/u], and [u]dry[/u] to the bodies which are composed of them.

[fig]Two-column list of elements and their qualities[/fig]
Fire is hot and dry;
Air is hot and moist;
Earth is cold and dry;
Water is cold and moist.

The Elements and the [u]Elementary Substances[/u] go by the same names. Elementary substances composed of the same element but having their molecules differently constructed (and that not merely owing to the ordinary effects of pressure and heat, crystallization, or solution, but [ins]more[/ins] permanently) are called [u]allotropic forms[/u] of that element. Mere crystallization is regarded as constituting a distinct allotropic form? (But, by an inconsistency, no such importance is attached to the crystalline form of a compound.) Thus, the two crystalline forms of sulphur are two allotropic forms, [del]and[/del] plastic sulphur is a third, flowers of sulphur a fourth, etc.
