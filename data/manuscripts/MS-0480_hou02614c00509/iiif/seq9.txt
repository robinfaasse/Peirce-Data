Acad. Graphs. 9

Art. 10. It is now necessary to give a precise rule for the interpretation of the graphs above described. This can only consist in a rule for the expression of the meaning of a graph in ordinary language. But ordinary language is very ill-fitted to express at once precisely and comprehensibly the complicated relations which graphs are well-adapted to express. No student can have any mastery of the method of graphs until he learns to think in their language, any more than he could have any mastery of algebra [del]if[/del] as long as he had to translate every equation into words. The only kind of linguistic syntax which does not utterly break down in dealing with [ins]complex[/ins] triadic relations is that which the lawyers have developed and which simply consists in using letters of the alphabet as relative pronouns. Though this is not very familiar, it has the merit of precision and therefore the rule for interpretation shall consist in showing how to express the meaning of a graph in that syntax.

The bonds of a logical graph of the above description denote individual objects of the [del]com[/del] universe of discourse[del], which[/del]. They are the subjects of discourse, or, in the language of grammar, the subjects and objects. The spots, or letters, of the graph signify ideas. They are the verbs.

The first step in interpreting a logical graph is to assign letters to denote the individuals denoted by the bonds. We are to begin with the bonds [ins]any parts of which[/ins] [del]that[/del] are unencircled. To [del]the[/del] each of these one of the first letters of the alphabet is to be assigned. These objects are to be suitably chosen, to suit the intention of the deliverer of the graph, that is, the supporter of its truth. The word "[ins]some[/ins]" implies such choice.
