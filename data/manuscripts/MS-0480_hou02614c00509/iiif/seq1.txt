Acad. Graphs [u]On Logical Graphs.[/u] Ms. 480 1
[del]Part I[/del]
By C. S. Peirce.

§ 1.
Art. 1. By a verb, I mean either a complete proposition or a fragment of a proposition which would be rendered complete by filling up certain blanks belonging to it with proper names. Thus, “— sells — to — at the price of —” is a verb.
A verb having no blanks, that is, a complete proposition, I call a [u]medadic verb[/u], or [u]medad[/u]: a verb having a single blank I call a [u]monadic verb[/u], or [u]monad[/u]: a verb having two blanks, a [u]dyadic verb[/u], or [u]dyad[/u]; a verb having three blanks a [u]triadic verb[/u], or [u]triad[/u]; etc. The number of blanks of a verb I call its adicity.
Art. 2. The assertion that there are real things whose proper names filling the blanks of a verb give a true proposition, as “something sells something to something at some price,” I call a [u]thoroughly particular proposition[/u]. [ins]Such a proposition may be called medadic, monadic, dyadic, triadic, etc.[/ins]
Art. 3. I propose to represent a verb by an ideograph, or sign of the like of some [del]thing or fact[/del] state of things, — [ins]if the verb is not analyzed,[/ins] usually by an initial letter, — with the ends of lines attached to it at definite places to indicate its several blanks. Thus, [fig]figure[/fig] [ins]Fig. 1[/ins] shall mean “— sells — to — at the price of —” where the four blanks in their order are indicated respectively by the lines at the left, at the right, below, and above the letter. If these lines are drawn heavy the corresponding thoroughly particular proposition shall be meant.
