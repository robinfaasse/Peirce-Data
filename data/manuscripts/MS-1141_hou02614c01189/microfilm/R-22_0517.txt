BEINGS.

Of things in themselves [del]we know nothing[/del], but only of the [del]affect[/del]
they produce on our receptivities. Before speaking of any
kind of cognition we can only consider the words for things
in themselves.

[[del]Noumenon[/del]] (Gr. νέος. νόος) that of which we [ins]can[/ins] know nothing except by thought
[del]It[/del] Some philosophers thought we could become acquainted with nou-
mena by thinking on them.

[u]Substratum[/u] (Sub sterno) that which lies behind and underneath [del]what[/del] is pre-
sented to our senses.

[u]Substance[/u] (sub. sto) literally, that which supports accidents. That which can never
be predicate [del]but[/del] must always be subject. But that which is presen-
ted to our senses may be regarded as predicate of its substratum.
These three words denote the same thing but they have dif-
ferent definitions, nevertheless. That is they view things in
themselves in different lights.

[u]Thing in itself[/u] (Ding) literally, that which has weight. The substratum
of a single object.

[u]Matter[/u] (materia, [del]Welsh[/del] mater, mad) literally, that which produces. The sub-
stratum of the outward world. In the outward world
we see matter and form, it is commonly said. Matter con-
tains no mind and can therefore produce no effect and
cannot even affect us. therefore, it is noumenal.

[u]Stuff[/u] (Danish stof) that which fills out. In that thought, lies its dis-
tinction from matter. It is seldom used noumenally.

[u]Subjective[/u] (subjectus) that which, whether it actually exists or not, is
considered in itself and not in its relations.
