1. [u]Time is continuous[/u]. That is to say, taking any multitude, [del]in common[/del] M, whatsoever, numerable or innumerable, it is possible that time should be so divided into parts, that some of these, of which no two have any parts in common, should [u]exceed[/u] M in multitude, while each of them is such that the same thing is true of it.
It follows that time cannot consist of indivisible times. For if it did, the multitude of them being taken for M, the above would evidently not be true.
2. Every continuous [del]time[/del] part of time ([del]apart[/del] [ins]aside[/ins] from its "contents", for the meaning of which, see below) is exactly like every other, and its relations to other continuous parts of time (with which it has no part in common) are precisely analogous to those of every other (aside from the "contents" of the different times). This may be expressed by saying that time is topically non-singular.
