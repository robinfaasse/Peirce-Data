Chapter I. The Constitutive Conventions of the language of graphs.

1. A certain place is called the sheet of the graph, and what is
there written is called the graph.
We pretend that the graph is a general description of a certain
recognized state of things.
I say [del]We[/del] "pretend" it is so, because [ins]for the matter[/ins] [del]our purpose[/del] in experi-
menting with graphs is merely [ins]a formal[/ins] to study [ins]of[/ins] logic; [del]not to aver any[/del]
real convictions of positive science. We therefore do not care,
whether we really believe what we pretend to assert or not.
[del]It is a mere example of an assertion.[/del] [ins]nature or quality[/ins]
The graph is a sign. That is, we do not care for its [del]character[/del]
in itself, nor for its dynamic reactions which pair it with other things, but
we care for it as a medium whereby one thing, the idea in our mind, is
brought into correspondence with an object. Every triadic relation is
somewhat intellectual. Thus, if I accidentally upset my inkstand and make
a spot on the table cloth the purely physical [ins]act[/ins] [del]event[/del] is upsetting the [del]can[/del] inkstand.
The effect on the table cloth is a separate transaction between the ink and
the cloth [del]with[/del] in which I have no hand. [del]And[/del] I am only responsible in that
I might have known what would result; that is, it is an affair of know-
ledge. Even if the wind had upset the inkstand, it would have produced
the effect only so far as the effect was necessary. But this supposes a
law; and a law is an affair of intellect. Let us call a triadic fact
[del]genuine[/del] if it is not a mere aggregate sum of dyadic facts. Sig-
nification or representation is the principal kind of genuine triadic
fact.
