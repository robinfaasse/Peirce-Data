[u]On Chapter II[/u]

Usually in algebra: $u = 0$ [ins]or $u = C$ $\Phi x = C$[/ins] represents the truth of a [ins]certain[/ins] proposition the falsity of which is represented by $\Phi x$ having any other value.

So we may conceive that $\Phi(x,y)$ is the number of $x$'s that are $y$'s $\Phi(x,y) = 0$ represents No $x$ is $y$

Or we may write
$$\begin{array}{rcccl}
\Phi(x,y) & \text{number} & x\text{'s} & \text{that} & \text{are } y\text{'s} \\
\Psi(x,y) & \text{"} & \text{"} & \text{"} & \text{" not } y\text{'s} \\
\mathrm{X}(x,y) & \text{"} & \text{non } x\text{'s} & \text{"} & \text{" } y\text{'s} \\
\omega(x,y) & \text{"} & \text{non } x\text{'s} & \text{"} & \text{" not } y\text{'s}
\end{array}$$

Equating these to zero gives the four universal propositions, [del]writing them[/del] [ins]equating[/ins]
$$\Phi(x,y) \neq 0$$
(with Cayley) give particular propositions.

The above expressions are not really functions of $x$ and $y$; yet we have the equations
[del]$x = \Phi(x,y)$[/del]
$$x = \Phi + \Psi \qquad y = \Phi + \mathrm{X}$$
$$V - x = \mathrm{X} + \omega$$

So that we do have 3 equations among the 4 unknowns
[del]if[/del] then we assume one more say [del]$\Phi + \omega - \Psi - \mathrm{X} = V$[/del]
