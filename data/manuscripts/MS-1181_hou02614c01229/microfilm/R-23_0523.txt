Head must presumably, from the spelling have been in some
dialect [del]drawled[/del] drawled. The spelling heed is not uncommon
in the XVIth century, but that which until nearly 1600 rivalled
head in frequency was hed. All the orthoepists Smith, 1568 ([u]Fol. 41[/u]),
[ins]Bullokar, 1586,[/ins] Gill, 1619 (pp. 27, 35, 103, 104), Butler, 1633 (p. 5, 9, 10, 13, 23, etc), Price,
1665, etc pronounced the word short; and so did all the rhymesters,
from the earliest period of Modern English. Thus, Lydgate has,
One bade me buy a hood to cover my head;
But, for want of money, I might not be sped.

Sidney has,
Take thou of me smooth pillows, sweetest bed,
A chamber deaf to noise and blind to light,
A rosy garland, and a weary head.

Shakespeare has,
A glooming peace this morning with it brings.
The Sunne for sorrow will not show his head;
Go hence, to have more talke of these sad things.
Some shall be pardon'd and some punished.

There is no end of such rhymes, while those with drawled syllables are rare.
