* The testimony of orthoëpists concerning these words cannot be assumed to be very precise, since their sounds may have been peculiar.

Pleasant had its first syllable drawled by Gil (p. 142) and by Butler (pp. 51, 76, 160).

Peasant was regularly spelled pessant, as if the first syllable were short.

Pheasant had the first syllable shortened by Salisbury, about [date]1550[/date].

Heaven was regularly spelled with [u]ea[/u], but we can conclude nothing from that.

Treasure was drawled by Cheke, [date]1555[/date], and by Smith, [date]1568[/date]. Gil drawls it in one place (p. 126) and shortens it in another. Shakespeare rhymes it with a long syllable.

What hast thou din'd? The Tailor staies thy [u]leasure[/u]
To decke thy bodie with his ruffling [u]treasure[/u].

Measure was originally accented on the last syllable. In Shakespeare the first syllable is drawled.

Haste still paies haste, and [u]leasure[/u] answers [u]leasure[/u];
Like doth quit like, and [u]Measure[/u] still for [u]Measure[/u].

It seems probable, however, that it was sometimes short, and Price, [date]1668[/date], makes it so.

Realm was drawled by Gil (p. 122). With his usual pedantry, he sounds the [u]l[/u]. Hodges, [date]1643[/date], tells us that it was silent.
