[u]English Spelling.[/u]

I remember in Rome, in [date]1870[/date], an Egyptian obelisk, covered with hieroglyphics, surmounted by a gilt cross, and resting on a base [del]with[/del] [ins]bearing[/ins] an inscription of Trajan. This monument pleased me greatly as an epitome of Rome. Probably, ere this, the cross has been removed by those to whom it seemed barbarously unhistorical. They were unconscious that it was their own proceeding that was barbarous, oblivious of history. But I would not have it restored; for its removal bespeaks the spirit of a new historical phase of the eternal city. English spelling is like Rome: it is a historical composite of the most heterogeneous elements. It is often absurd; but that very absurdity is of value to us. To raze Rome and have it rebuilt by a Chicago architect would be less detrimental to the higher interests of the human race, than to reform English spelling so as to make it quite phonetic. The one would [del]doubly[/del] not only do terrible violence to our sentiments, but would, [del]doubt[/del] affect the aesthetic development of the world. But even that would not be so bad as to deprive English-writing thought — we must not say of its clothing, for that would be a most inadequate metaphor. To attempt to revolutionize English spelling while leaving Anglo-Saxon thought intact, would be like trying to [del]destroy[/del] burn up the cellulose of the rose while leaving its [ins][del]color and[/del][/ins] beauty and significance.
