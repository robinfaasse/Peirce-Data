[u]Stead[/u], in the word [u]instead[/u] was drawled by Gil (p. 103); but Butler
uniformly pronounced it 'steed' (pp. 5, 8, 18, 144). This was probably a
common pronunciation. It is singular that [u]stedfast[/u] was spelled
phonetically without the [u]a[/u]. [u]Steady[/u], which comes from a different
Anglo Saxon word from stead, was, in Middle English, [u]stidi[/u], apparently
our "stiddy", and as such words [del]are[/del] [ins]do[/ins] not often change from short to long,
we may assume that it was so at the time the XVI century printers
fixed the spelling.

[u]Yeast[/u] was another word in which [u]ea[/u] took the sound of [u]ee[/u], as
Smith, 1568 (fol. 17) testifies. [ins]But it was sometimes short, no doubt.[/ins] [u]Yeasty[/u], however, was sometimes spelled
[u]yesty[/u]. Thus the first folio Shakespeare has

Though the yesty Waves
Confound and swallow Navigation up,

and "a kind of yesty collection".

[u]Zeal[/u] was drawled by Gil (pp. 13, 105) but as to [u]Zealous[/u] and [u]Zealot[/u], no
evidence is at hand.
