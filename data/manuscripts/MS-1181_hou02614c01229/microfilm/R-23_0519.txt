Read, in the present tense, was doubled by Bullokar, 1580, [del]and[/del]
Gil, 1619 (pp. 48, 95), and by Hodges, 1643. But the preterit and past
participle were short with Smith, 1568, (fol. 11), with Gil, 1619,
(pp. 48, 52, 117), and with Butler, 1633 (pp. 16, 137), and with Hodges,
1643. The [del]ea[/del] [ins]e[/ins] was thus [del]not[/del] phonetic but etymological.

Spread, in the present tense, was doubled [ins]Cheke, 1547, and[/ins] by Butler, 1633 (pp. 90, 118).
It was early shortened in the XVIIth Century, however, and
where Milton perpetrates an imperfect rhyme in

Or Trent, who like some earth-born giant spreads
His thirty arms along th' indented meads.

Spread, in the preterit and past participle, however, was short, as we
are told by Gil, 1619 (p. 106), and Butler, 1633 (pp. 92, 95, 97, 109, 148).
It was sometimes so spelled. Spenser has,

Her lips did smell like unto gilliflowers,
Her ruddy cheeks like unto roses red,
Her snowy brows like budded bellamoures,
Her lovely eyes like pinks but newly spred.
