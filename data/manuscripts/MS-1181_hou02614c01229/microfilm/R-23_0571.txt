And the same rhyme occurs in four other passages [ins]of Shakespeare and in many of others[/ins], Breakfast, on the other hand, was, in the phonetic spelling of the XVth century, always written [del]for[/del] brekfast, and it sometimes appears as breckfast in the XVIth century. Its first syllable was, therefore, certainly short, and the ea was etymological.

Clean was rhymed with long syllables, as in the following:

Two households, both alike in dignitie,
In faire Verona, where we laie our scene,
From antient grudge breake to new mutinie,
Where ciuill bloud makes ciuill handes vncleane.

The spelling, though various, was regularly cleane; sometimes clean, clene, cleen, or cleene (the last two spellings [ins]which the rhymes also show[/ins] indicating that it sometimes took the sound of ee), but never, in the XVIth century, clen. Cleanly, on the other hand, was shortened by Butler (p. 64), and was not infrequently spelled clenly. So cleanse was shortened
