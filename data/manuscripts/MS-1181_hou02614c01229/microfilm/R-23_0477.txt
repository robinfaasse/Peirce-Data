The digraphs oa and ea may be said to have been inventions of the XVIth century printers. Not that oa and ea are not found in English much earlier; but they [del]were little use[/del] [ins]so seldom occurred[/ins] in the XIVth century, and were now introduced to denote the broader sounds of o and a. Ea was intended to represent a vowel-sound now lost, as long, from English standard speech, but which is preserved in the New England drawling pronunciation of the word bread; being precisely the short e of bred lengthened. The short e in bred, like all our short accented vowels, is abruptly cut off, giving a peculiar effect; while our long vowels begin to be enfeebled before the following consonant is reached.
