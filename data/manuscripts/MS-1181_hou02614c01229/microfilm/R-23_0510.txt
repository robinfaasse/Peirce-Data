George Herbert says of religion,

All may of thee partake;

Nothing can be so mean

Which, with this tincture, for thy sake,

Will not grow bright and [del]cleane[/del] [ins]clean.[/ins]
[ins]Shakespeare has a similar rhyme in the Prologue to Romeo and Juliet.[/ins]

It was usually spelled with the <u>ea</u>, or earlier <u>clene</u>, which denoted the same drawl. Occasionally <u>cleene</u> is met with, but never <u>clen</u>. [del]It[/del] [ins]The word[/ins] is not noticed by any orthoepist before Price, 1668, who drawled it. <u>Cleanse</u> and <u>Cleanly</u>, on the other hand, were quite frequently spelled <u>clense</u> and <u>clenly</u> in the XVIIth century. <u>Cleanse</u> [del]is[/del] [ins]was[/ins] made short by [ins]Butler, 1633 (pp. 53, 84), by[/ins] Cooper, 1685, [del]the first orthoepist to notice the word,[/del] and by Jones, 1701. <u>Cleanly</u> was made short by Butler (p. 64).
