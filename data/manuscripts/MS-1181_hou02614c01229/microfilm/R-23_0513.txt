Foot-note, continued

Desert went naked in the cold,
When crouching craft was fed;
Sweet wordes were cheaply bought and sold,
But none that stood in stead.

The spelling of steady is sufficient to show that, if it was never
drawled, the word was supposed to be derived from stead. Now
that it was not drawled is shown by the frequency of the spelling
steddy both in the XVIth century and much later. (and this was the Middle English spelling.) The rhymes
confirm this. Lord Brooke (or more probably Sir Philip Sidney) has

They all alike feele dissolution ready,
Their owne subsistence failing, and unsteady. (it would be too long to explain why)

Hodges, 1643, the first orthoepist to give the word, shortens it.

Threat was drawled by Gil, 1619, (p. 99); but threaten had its
first syllable short, according to the archaic Bullokar, 1580. It thus
appears that the a was inserted merely because it was supposed to derive
from the earlier verb to threat.
