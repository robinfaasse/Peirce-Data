In fact, if $x$ be the upper and $y$ the lower number, the ordinal number of the pair is $\frac{1}{2}(x+y)^2 + \frac{1}{2}x + \frac{3}{2}y + 1$. It is applicable, as Cantor has shown, to the [del]multitude[/del] of algebraic numbers, or numbers which are roots of algebraic equations with commensurable coefficients. It is even applicable to the multitude of numbers which are functions of algebraic numbers, provided those functions can be generally defined by differential equations of finite order and degree. All of these multitudes are to be regarded as equal. They may be said to be [u]discretely infinite[/u].

But the Fermatian inference is not applicable to the multitude of numbers between 0 and 1 to which ordinary decimals can indefinitely approximate, nor [del]to[/del] the number of points on a line, however short. These multitudes, which are incapable of having separate ordinal numbers [ins]assigned[/ins] to all their members, may be called [u]continuously infinite[/u]. All these are to be regarded as equal. For instance, the [del]new[/del] multitude of points in a line is plainly the same as that of points in a solid. For the points in a solid can be discriminated by three coordinates.
