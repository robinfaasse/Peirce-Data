[u]Art 18.[/u] To say that a relation is implied in another relation is
to say that it is simpler, has less logical depth and more
logical breadth. Hence the aggregated relation [del]that[/del]
which means a degenerate locus is implied in the aggre-
gants. To say that a point is on a [del]skew cub[/del] spacious
cubic is to imply that it is on the intersection of two
quadric surfaces having a generator in common.
But, of course, it is natural to use the word "imply" in
a different sense.

[u]Art 19.[/u] So far we have supposed a relation consisted merely
in excluding certain possibilities. But now Cayley intro-
duces the word [u]distributively[/u], [del]an any[/del] [ins]utterly[/ins] inadmissible
word, having a peculiar logical sense. To mean that
the relation is [del]to consist[/del] no merely in denying certain
possibilities but in asserting the rest.
The [ins]point[/ins] equation of a line does not expressly say anything
except that the point cannot be elsewhere. An equation
cannot by itself assert that the point can be anywhere. But
the "distributive" relation means that [del]the[/del] points exist everywhere
where not forbidden.
The quantifier of the logic of relatives is required to express this.

[u]Art 20.[/u] With this understanding, no relation can imply any other.
The truth is Cayley is trying to combine irreconcileable points
of view.

[u]Art 21.[/u] What [del]Cayley[/del] calls aggregation ought to be called composition.

[u]Art 22.[/u] Cayley says there is no meaning in combining a relation with itself.
Yet we know this enters largely into Schubert's abzählende Geometrie
and is absolutely essential to the logic of relatives. To assert the
same thing twice over is highly necessary, in different connections. The truth
is the case is precisely like the aggregation of the locus to itself. The dif-
ference which Cayley asserts does not exist.
