It is Schröder's predilection for equations which motives
his preference for the algebra of dual relatives, namely,
the fact that in that algebra, even [ins]a[/ins] simple undetermined
inequality can be expressed as an equation. I think, too, that
algebra has merits; it certainly has uses to which Schröder
seldom puts it. Yet, after all, it has too much formalism to
suit me, — too many bushels of chaff per grain of wheat.
I think [del]Schrö[/del] Prof. Schroeder likes algebraic formalism better,
or dislikes it less, than I. [fig]bracket[/fig] [pn]9.7[/pn]

He looks at the problems of logic through the spectacles of
equations; and formulates them from that point of view, so
he thinks, with great generality; as I think, in a narrow spirit.
The great thing is to solve [del]an equation[/del] [ins]a proposition[/ins], and get a value of [u]x[/u], that
is, an equation of which [u]x[/u] forms one member without occurring
in the other. How far such equation is [u]iconic[/u], that is, has a meaning,
or exhibits the constitution of [u]x[/u], he hardly seems to care. He
prefers general values to particular roots. Why? I should think
the particular root alone of service, for most purposes, unless
the general expression were such that particular roots could
be deduced from it, — particular instances, I mean, showing the
constitution of [u]x[/u].
