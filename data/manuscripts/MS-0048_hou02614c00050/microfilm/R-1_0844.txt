17[/pn] Whenever one person informs another of any fact, that information has, as you know, two parts. The one part [del]tells[/del] [ins]indicates[/ins] what well-known [del]objects[/del] [ins]subjects[/ins] the information is about, whether [del]it[/del] [ins]they[/ins] be called by their proper names, or indefinitely indicated as belonging to a known class, or whether the hearer be given the right to consider any or all of them as whatever members of a given class he pleases, or whether some periphrasis indicates them. The other part [del]signifies[/del] [ins]conveys[/ins] the [ins]idea[/ins] [del]substance[/del] of the information. If there is any idea in the first part, that is merely subsidiary to its purpose of indicating the subjects. But if the second part does not convey an idea [del]then[/del] the whole asserts nothing. Besides these two parts, there must be some sort of attestation, to prove that the informer assumes some responsibility for the truth of his tale.
