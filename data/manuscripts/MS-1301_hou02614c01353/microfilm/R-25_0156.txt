Calm may she glide. A further tale obtains,—
That once she was a denizen of earth,
Met mortals face to face, and ne'er disowned
Or men or women of the race of old,
But though immortal sat amid them all.
They used to name her Justice; she would call
The elders to broad street or city-square,
Declare men's rights, and see that right was done.
As yet they knew not aught of grievous strife,
Nor aught of blameful faction, or of din;
But thus they lived. Nor recked of ocean-toil,
For life¹ not yet did vessels bring afar;
But ploughs and oxen, and the people's queen
Justice, right-giver, richly all things gave.
This was whilst earth maintained the golden race.²
In silver days she little mixed with man,
Changed, for she missed the ways of those of yore.
Yet through the silver age she still remained.
She came at even from the echoing hills
Alone, nor spoke to mortal soothing word;
But when the highlands were replete with men,
These she would threaten, and their sins would chide,
And vow she ne'er would come in sight though called.
'Oh what a race the golden sires have left,
Inferior to themselves; but you will father worse.
Then wars and slaughter will prevail with men,
And woe shall fall upon them for their sins.'
She spake and sought the heights, and left the hosts
Whilst on her all were gazing earnestly.
But when these too had died, and others came,
The brazen race, more hurtful than their sires,
They for themselves first forged the highway knife—
An evil work, and first plough oxen ate;
