p. 118: [ins][[/ins]In 1822 M. Cagniard de la Tour observed the effect of a
high temperature upon liquids enclosed in glass tubes of a
capacity not much greater than that of the liquid itself.
He found that when the temperature was raised to a certain
point, the substance, which till then was partly liquid and
partly gaseous, suddenly became uniform in appearance [del]in[/del]
throughout, without any visible surface of separation, or
any evidence that the substance in the tube was partly in
one state and partly in another.

He concluded that at this temperature the whole became
gaseous. The true conclusion, as Dr. Andrews has shown,
is that the properties of the liquid and those of the va-
pour continually approach to similarity, and that, above a
certain temperature, the properties of the liquid are not
separated from those of the vapour by any apparent distinc-
tion between them.[ins]][/ins] "This is not a good description. Above
that temperature there is no liquid state. At that tempera-
ture there is a point of condensation where there is no dis-
tinction between liquid and vapor though if the volume is
greater it is vapour or if less one may call it liquid or
vapour. At a temperature a little above there is a point
where a change of pressure has a maximum effect on the vol-
ume."
