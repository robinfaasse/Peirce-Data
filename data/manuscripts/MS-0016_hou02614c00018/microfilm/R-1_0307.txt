The mathematician at once substitutes for this a mere conception, a mere dream, which he neither asserts nor proposes to assert nor interrogatively suggests as true or as false of the real world. He has nothing to do with the real world. His hypothesis is a mental creation. It is its own ideal world. If the [del]physicist's[/del] hypothesis be [del]proposed[/del] [ins]composed[/ins] by a non-mathematician, it will be pretty sure to require simplification by the mathematician. But at any rate, its meaning is entirely different from the mathematician's hypothesis, in that its assertoric character disappears in the latter; because the physicist is always do reasoning about the facts and the mathematician about a pure idea. This transformation, whatever change in [del]the[/del] substance it involves, is performed by the mathematician himself. If anybody else does it, he becomes for the nonce a mathematician.

[u]Art. 2[/u]. The hypothesis of the mathematician is always the conception of a system of relations. In order that they may be reasoned about mathematically, these relations must be conceived as embodied in some kind of [del]objects;[/del] [ins]objects;[/ins] but the character of the objects, apart from the relations, is utterly immaterial. They are always made as bare, skeleton-like, or diagrammatic as possible. With mathematicians [del]not born blind[/del] they are always visual objects of the simplest kind, such as dots, or lines, or letters, and the like. [del]A[/del] mathematician often passes from one mode of embodiment to another. Such a change is no change in the hypothesis but only in the diagrammatic embodiment of the hypothesis. The hypothesis itself consists in the system of relations alone.
