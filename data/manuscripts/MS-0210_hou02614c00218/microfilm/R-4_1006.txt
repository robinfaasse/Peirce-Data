Should the head of the house of Rothschild, during a sojourn at
Monaco, take it into his head to earn one franc each day by one-franc
bets at the roulette table, and having won his one franc to go back to
his hotel to gloat over his victory for the rest of the day, what would be
the result? The result would be that some day he would find the luck
did not turn in time for him to get back to dinner. But if there were
any contrivance by which he could make bets enough each day for his
purpose, then he could not prudently prolong his [del]visit[/del] [ins]stay[/ins] into the third
week. For supposing the odds on any bet are as 30 in favor of the player to
31 in favor of the bank (which, all things considered, is nearly the truth) the
odds in favor of M. de Rothschild's gaining his first day's franc would only
be as 30 to 1. (This is one of the corollaries of the famous problem of
the [del]Duration of[/del] Play which is discussed in all considerable treatises on
[del]the[/del] Doctrine of Chances.) To find the chance of his holding out for 3 weeks,
or 21 days, we must raise 30/31 to the 21[ins]st[/ins] power, which gives 0.502;
and this subtracted from 1 leaves 0.498, so that the bet is 502 to 498,
or very nearly even, that (could he crowd so many one-franc bets into a
single day), on some one of [del]these[/del] 21 days he would rise from the table
a ruined man. This is not very surprising when we reflect that since
the chances are against him, if the bets become numerous, he will be
pretty certain to go on losing more and more without ever making his
net gain of 1 franc.
