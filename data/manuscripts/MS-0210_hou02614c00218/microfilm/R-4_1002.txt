The nominalist school of philosophy which dictated the accepted logic of mathematics from the time of Gen[sup]l[/sup]. Carnot (therefore from the time of the French Revolution) until the views of Dr. Georg Cantor began to prevail, about [date]1880[/date], used to say that a contradiction was involved in literal infinity, but that whenever the word occurred in mathematics, it must be understood as implying that if a finite quantity were to be put in place of it, that [del]the[/del] which is asserted of infinity, if not true for the finite quantity, would approach nearer and nearer to being true as the finite quantity were made greater and greater (at least, this would be the case, after any fluctuations due to the quantity being too small had ceased), and would [ins]for a[/ins] [del]some[/del] sufficiently large value of the finite quantity come nearer to being true than any degree of approximation named in advance. The doctrine that this is all that [del]a[/del] [del]st[/del] mathematical assertion about infinity means is called the Doctrine of Limits. The mathematical method upon which this philosophical analysis is founded, the Method of Limits, is as old as Archimedes, and in a less perfected development is employed by Euclid.
