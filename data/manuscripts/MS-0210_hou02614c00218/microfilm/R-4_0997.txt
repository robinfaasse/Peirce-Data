If M. Albert de Rothschild were to determine that during a sojourn at Monaco he would play [del]until[/del] each day until he had netted for the day a gain of one franc, he would do well not to prolong his visit beyond a fortnight. For the odds in favor of his ever making a net winning of one franc would only be 30 to 1; and if he stayed for three weeks the chance would be even that [ins]on some of the 21[/ins] days he would rise from the table a ruined man before he had made his franc. This is because of the advantage of the bank. The rule is, that the odds in favor of an immensely rich man making a net gain equal to a single bet before he is ruined are to the odds against him as the odds in his favor on a single bet are to the excess of the odds against him [ins]for each bet[/ins] over the odds in his favor. For example, let there be 30 chances in the player's favor against 31 for the bank, which is supposed to be about the true ratio: then [del]the[/del] a fair bet in favor of Rothschild's winning one franc, at franc bets, before he was ruined would be as a bet of 30 to 31 minus 30, or of 30 to 1. The odds that Rothschild would win a franc for 21 days successively are obtained by substituting in place of 30 and 31 in the above calculation the 21st powers of these numbers. Now $(\frac{30}{31})^{21} = 0.5069$.
