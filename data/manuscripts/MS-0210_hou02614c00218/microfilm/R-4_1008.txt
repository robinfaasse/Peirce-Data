For instance, suppose in that case a player to sit down resolved to win
one franc at one-franc bets, and then to quit play forever. His staying-

to succeed. Or rather, since it is evidently logically possible that he
never should win even a single throw, it would be more microscopically
accurate to say that the probability of his winning the desired franc, if his
bets can be innumerable, is so great as to be numerically indistinguish-
able from certainty. Suppose, however, that as soon as this player has
won his one franc, as he probably will in a few throws and is quasi-
sure to do in time, he vacates his place at the table and [del]goes[/del] [ins]then[/ins] another
player sit down [del]of[/del] equally unlimited resources, who proceeds to play in
precisely the same way. Suppose there are indefinitely many players, and
a constant crowd of onlookers anxiously waiting for places at the table; and
suppose every man of them pursues this same policy of one-franc bets and
of quitting forever as soon as he has gained one franc and not before.
Then it is clear that every man of them will win his franc. Neverthe-
less, the game being even, the bank maintains an approximate balance,
nor is the banker one whit worse off than he would be if his whole
clientele were to reverse their tactics and were always to leave off when
they began to net a loss.

This sounds mighty paradoxical. We must expect paradox
when we make hypotheses about infinite quantities. Yet
an infinite quantity really lurks beneath every possible suppo-
sition, whatsoever.
