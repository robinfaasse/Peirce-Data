[pn]459[/pn] But it may be urged that the very basis upon which the doctrine of chances is reared is that whatever is possible is destined actually to occur once in so often, according to the composition of the case. Now, nobody can deny that it is possible that a man should, on the whole, lose more and more, perpetually. Hence, though the probability of this case is by calculation zero, yet out of that infinitely long run of cases which alone lends any meaning to numerical probability, that one case must occur once at least. Thus, horrible to contemplate, when finite periods of time, years, centuries, and cycles had all elapsed, there out of the infinite multitude of players for a franc, would still sit one [del]woful[/del] Ahasuerus, left behind to bear upon his shoulders losses equal to the infinite sum distributed as gains among the rest, and doomed to struggle on through all eternity in the desperate and hopeless quest after that one accursed franc. This case has to be taken into account in striking the general average; and it reduces the total advantage to zero, even without regard to the loss of time.

460 But is this sound doctrine? How many things are possible that are inconsistent with one another, so that it is absurd to say that whatever is possible is sure to happen!
