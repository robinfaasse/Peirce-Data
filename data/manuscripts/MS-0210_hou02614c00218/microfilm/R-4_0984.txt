[pn]450[/pn] Were the resources, as well of the bank as of every player, limitless, and the game perfectly fair, sundry curious situations might arise. Suppose, for example, a player to sit down, resolved to win one franc alone-franc bets, and then to quit play forever. His staying-power being unlimited, and the game perfectly even, he would be sure to succeed. Or rather, since it is evidently logically quite possible he should never win even a single throw, it would be more microscopically accurate to say that the probability of his winning the desired franc, if his bets can be innumerable, is so great as to be numerically indistinguishable from certainty. To continue our hypothesis: as soon as this player has won his franc, as he probably will in a few throws and is quasi-sure to do some time, let him vacate his place at the table and make room for another player. Suppose that there are indefinitely many players, and a constant crowd of onlookers anxiously waiting for places at the table; and suppose every man of them pursues this same policy of one-franc bets and of quitting forever [del]when[/del] [ins]as soon[/ins] as he has gained 1 franc and not before. Then it is clear that every man of them will win his franc. Nevertheless, the game being even, the bank maintains a balance; nor is the banker one whit worse off than if his whole clientele were to reverse their tactics, and were always to leave off when they began to net a loss.
