[del]45[/del] [ins]7[/ins] One side of the theory remains, however, but cursorily considered; and
to this, pertinent criticism is particularly invited. Namely, while we
have argued [u]ad nauseam[/u] that the proposed method of play could not
be of the slightest disadvantage to the banker, we have possibly been
a little hasty in assuming that there would be any real advantage
to the players. A good many doubts might be started about that; but
there are only two concerning which some suggestions seem needed.

The first relates to the loss of time. Namely, while of any large
number of players who should begin the play simultaneously one half
would gain their francs at the first bet, only one fourth of those who
remained would gain theirs at the third bet; of those who then remained
only one sixth would gain theirs at the fifth bet; and so on, the denomi-
nator increasing by two at every second bet, while at the intermediate
bets no player could become a winner. Thus, the progress would be slower
and slower, because those who remained at the table would continually
be showing a greater and greater average of losses. It would take over 6000
bets to get 99/100 of the players their francs; and out of millions of players,
some might waste the best parts of their lives, though they lived to the
age of Methusalem, in attaining their ends.
