Milford Pa [date]1901 May 20[/date] /

My dear Prof. Langley:

Your sending me a copy of your former letter indicates that you think I have not sufficiently studied it.

I think I had done so. It is not my cue to create imaginary difficulties in any job you [del]give me[/del] [ins]are kind enough to give me[/ins]. But when you asked me to treat of Laws of Nature with special reference to Hume's argument against miracles, being desirous [ins]and [u]not[/u] [del]of[/del][/ins] writing an article which should seem to have some definite purpose, I found myself in this condition, [ins]that,[/ins] as well as I remembered, Hume's argument had nothing to do with any metaphysical definition of a miracle; and if he had conceived that "the question turns on" that, as you say, he would not have shoved the gist of his argument into a foot-note. Subsequent thorough rereading, not only of Hume but of a dozen other books, Voltaire's correspondence, & some reports of conversations at the time, fully confirm my impression, 1[ins]st[/ins], there there is no logical connection between any particular conception of Laws of Nature and Hume's argument, 2[ins]nd[/ins], that Hume did not conceive there was such a connection, 3[ins]rd[/ins], nor did the philosophers of his time or subsequent times, 4[ins]th[/ins] nor did the general public at his day.

I am extremely desirous of writing you an article on Laws of Nature, with some reference to Miracles, & then I can branch off to say that this has nothing to do with Hume's argument, and I will give the true reason why Hume's argument excites no particular attention today, which is that German historical criticism has carried altogether similar argumentation about every part of the Bible, about the History of Philosophy, and about all history relating to high antiquity so very much further, that Hume's seems good sense in comparison. I do not think that anybody but theoreticians & critics of the German school ever did take much stock in the balancing of probabilities (which is Hume's method) as a way of judging of testimony. It is impossible to make out that Hume's argument added anything to the distrust of miracles prevalent at the time of its publication. Its weight then was so near zero that it cannot now have less. Probably more readers now follow German criticism than then followed Hume. I shall go ahead on this line, unless I hear to the contrary.

Very respectfully,
C. S. Peirce
