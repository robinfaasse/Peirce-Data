My dear Prof Langley:

Hume's "Treatise" reached me from you at a time when I was entirely overwhelmed with long communications from different countries about my Pragmaticism. But and as I have thus been driven, I have fulfilled my promise of carefully examining Hume's work again. I at once found the passage to which I thought you referred as the end of Vol I; but it is not so absolute a nihilism as I had remembered it & much less so than you seemed to remember it. I therefore went through the pages. If we reject it, we subvert entirely the human understanding. We have no choice left but betwixt a false reason and none at all... If we believe that fire warms or water refreshes, 'tis only because it costs us much pains to think otherwise."

There are many other passages, but none are more sceptical than these; and none are dogmatically nihilistic. I may say that in copying these I have endeavored not so much to represent what is in the book, as what you would probably have remembered.

The whole of Hume's sceptical arguments were answered when he was 9 years old by "s'Gravesande in his "Introductio ad Philosophiam." Hume's whole intense & absurd position is due to a pedantic and utterly false, conception of the function of reasoning. He "proves too much," as they say; for if he proves anything, it is that all reasoning is essentially and [u]ipso facto[/u] illogical. And why? Why, because it rests on completely satisfactory and indubitable premisses!
