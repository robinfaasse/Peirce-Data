My dear Prof Langley

I had better not [del]write[/del] what you desire until I can get hold of a copy of Hume's Essays & refresh my mind on the subject. But as I remember it, it was not necessary for him to define a miracle metaphysically, as he does. My recollection is that he argued that when a witness testified to anything very extraordinary, it was necessary to balance the improbability of his story against that of his un-truth and decide in consequence; and that the improbability of miracles was such that no testimony could be adequate to supporting it.

Now if I find, on rereading Hume, that that was his argument, I will, if you approve of it & would like it, consider the matter somewhat as follows (but adapting my talk to your readers as I need not here do.

1[sup]st[/sup] I will consider the real value of the argument and
2[sup]nd[/sup], the state of the public mind with regard to it at the two epochs.

Under the first head, I will consider
A. the argument in its essence.
B. how far it would be strengthened or weakened by [ins]particular[/ins] metaphysical conceptions of miracles and laws of Nature.
