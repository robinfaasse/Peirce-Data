[marginalia]Kelah[/marginalia] [del]the stanzas of Kelah end, without exception, on a "shore" that will naturally and properly be read with a down-ward inflection of only a semitone, with what Dr. Rush's "median stress", the maximum being near the commencement of the vowel sound which then dies away as it is somewhat prolonged, in a manner that singularly adapts the semitone to the [ins]mental[/ins] timbre of the poem.[/del] The out of door scenery is likewise the same in the two pieces; a desolate night-shore in bad weather, while in both the poet sits within before a flickering fire.

In short, Kelah was, beyond doubt, a study for the Raven. Now "studies" are very apt to be fresher and more vigorous than finished works; — a remark that one cannot repress at several passages of this one. "The night's Plutonian shore" of the Raven, for example, is it not a grandied elaboration of the "dull, Neptunian roar" of the simpler and less inhuman Kelah?
