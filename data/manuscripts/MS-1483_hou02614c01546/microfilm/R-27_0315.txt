It is certain that a new account of Spinoza's philosophy
is much needed. For a thinker who has exercised so much
influence during the nineteenth century and who continues
to attract such immense numbers of students ought to be
understood historically. Yet hitherto he has not been
understood historically and consequently has not been
understood in respect to the characteristics of his own
thought. Berendt and Friedländer's book, for example, is
entitled "Spinoza's Erkenntnisslehre in ihrer Beziehung zur
modernen Naturwissenschaft und Philosophie." Spinoza
himself was hardly aware there was such a branch of philos-
ophy as "Erkenntnisslehre." We are receiving too many
works in these days concerning this or that historically
[del]yet[/del] important system of philosophy "in ihrer Beziehung
zur modernen Wissenschaft und Philosophie," a point of
view which may enable one to understand modern
philosophy better, but which throws comparatively little
light upon the system of philosophy which is treated in
that Beziehung. What is needed in order to understand a
past system is, first, what were the received ideas out
