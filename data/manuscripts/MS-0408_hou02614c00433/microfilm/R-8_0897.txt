Ratiocination is defined by St. Thomas as the operation by
which reason proceeds from the known to the unknown. Inferences
are of two kinds the necessary and the probable. There are in
either case (such is the traditional opinion which will be modi-
fied in this work) there are certain propositions called [u]premises[/u] laid
down and granted; and these render another proposition, called
the [u]conclusion[/u] either necessary or probable, as the case may be.
[ins]The conclusion is sometimes said to be [del]collected[/del] [ins]a necessary inference[/ins] from the premises. It is also said to follow from them.[/ins]

follows, that is, is rendered necessary or probable, is called the
logical [del]rule,[/del] [ins]dictum,[/ins] law, or principle. A [del]conclusion[/del] from a single
premise is called an [u]immediate inference[/u], from two premises
a [u]syllogism[/u], from more than two a [u]sorites[/u]. The massing of a
