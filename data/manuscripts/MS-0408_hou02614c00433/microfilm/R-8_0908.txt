[u]Hypothetical[/u]. This expression was used [del]throughout the middle ages[/del] [ins]from the time of the early[/ins] followers of Aristotle down to modern times, to signify a proposition composed of two or more propositions. The word [del]hypothetical[/del] [ins]ὑποθετικός[/ins] does not occur in the writings of Aristotle; but John Philoponus, a writer of the early part of the seventh century, who is [del]supposed[/del] [ins]said[/ins] to have had the advantage of the [del]lib[/del] [ins]MSS[/ins] of the Alexandrian library, [ins]part of whose commentaries is printed in the Berlin edition of Aristotle,[/ins] says that the early peripatetics Theophrastus and Eudemus, analyzed the logic of the προτάσεις ὑποθετικαί, and gave that name to them; and as he describes them, they were the [del]same[/del] [ins]compound[/ins] propositions in [u]if[/u], with a [u]protasis[/u] and [u]apodosis[/u], as the Grammarians say, which we call in logic still, hypothetical, or better, conditional propositions. The propositions not categorical are best called [u]composite[/u], the word recommended by Appuleius, whom circumstances as well as his intrinsic merit have rendered a high authority.

[u]Antecedent[/u] and [u]Consequent[/u]. The words antecedens and consequens occur in Martianus Capella. The Stoic terms were ἡγούμενον and λῆγον, i.e. beginning and ceasing.

[u]Copulative[/u]. Propositio copulativa appears very early in the thirteenth century in translations from the Arabic. But copulatum, from which the word must [del]have[/del] been derived is mentioned by Aulus Gellius in the second century of our era, along with conjunctum as two ways of translating συμπεπλεγμένον which is the Stoic word for the copulative proposition.
