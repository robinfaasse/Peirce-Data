[u]Dictum[/u], [u]rule[/u], [u]law[/u], [u]principle[/u]. The word [u]dictum[/u] is applied
particularly to the [u]dictum de omni et de nullo[/u], which is
Aristotle's way of saying that if A implies B, and B implies C,
then A implies C. [del]For[/del] It is early referred to as the [u]dici de omni[/u]
and the [u]dici de nullo[/u]. The word [u]principle[/u], Latin [u]principium[/u],
Greek [u]ἀρχή[/u] is somewhat vague. The word [u]rule[/u], Latin [u]regula[/u],
is chiefly applied to secondary logical principles. The word [u]law[/u],
Latin [u]lex[/u], is applied chiefly to some principles which cannot
be immediately applied to determine the validity of an inference.

[u]Immediate[/u]. The immediate illation is so called, because there
is no "middle term". The phrase originated among the reformed
Aristotelians subsequent to scholasticism. (See Eustachius a Sancto Paulo
Dialectica. Pars III. Tract I. Disput I. Qu. 1.) By the scholastics, it was called
a [u]formal consequence[/u] (Scotus in I Pr. Anal. qu. x.) Such a thing was
not usually considered as an argument at all, although a syllogism
becomes just this as soon as its two premises are [ins]brought by colligation in a copulative proposition[/ins].

[u]Syllogism[/u]. The syllogism is the great discovery of Aristotle
and always has been and always shall be, while men retain any
decent gratitude, be called by the name he gave to it. Syllogism
is of course, by formation the same as collection.
