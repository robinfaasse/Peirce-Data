[u]Judgment[/u]. The English word [u]judgment[/u], so common in Chaucer,
where it is trisyllabic, is from the very old French [u]jugement[/u], from
[del]a[/del] [u]judicamentum[/u], a medieval word of legal signification. But
[u]judgment[/u] is used in logic to translate the Latin [u]judicium[/u], the
old French [u]juise[/u] never having been introduced into our language.
[u]Judicium[/u] was used in the sense of opinion in classical Latin,
just as judgment is with us. It early became a term of logic in
the sense of [del]estimating[/del] passing judgment upon the conclusiveness
of an argumentation. But in the sense of a mental proposition
it seems to have been introduced by Ockham. At least he says that
an act in which the understanding not merely apprehends an object,
but assents to it or dissents from it, "may be called a judicative act."
Now he would hardly have considered speaking of what was already
called [u]judicium[/u] as [u]actus judicativus[/u], as new terminology. Therefore,
we may assume (unless an earlier example can be produced) that before
Ockham a mental proposition had not been called [u]judicium[/u]; although,
in the sense of [del]privately[/del] settling a question, especially an opinion to the
advantage or disadvantage of anything, the word was common enough. Gregory
of Rimini, one of the earliest followers of Ockham in logic, gives Prantl's
earliest instance of [u]judicium[/u] itself so used.

[u]Ratiocination[/u]. [del]The[/del] [ins][u]Ratiocinatio[/u][/ins] was used in Classical Latin
in the same sense; though it was sometimes applied, as inference
now is, to [del]con[/del] imply a doubt of the conclusion. J.S. Mill, on the
other hand, would restrict it to demonstrative reasoning.
