Categorical propositions are said to be divided according to their
<u>Quantity</u>, into the universal, the particular, the indefinite, and the singular.
A universal proposition was said to be a proposition whose subject is a
common term determined by a universal sign. A common term was
defined as one which is adapted to being predicated of several things
(aptus natus praedicari de pluribus) The universal signs are, [del]all[/del] [ins]every[/ins], [del]non[/del] [ins]no[/ins],
[del]any[/del], etc. A particular proposition was said to be a proposition
whose subject is a common term determined by a particular sign.
The particular signs are, some, etc. An indefinite proposition [del]is[/del] [ins]was said to be[/ins]
one in which [del]the subject is[/del] a common term without any sign, "of
homo currit." That unfortunate "indefinite" man has been running
on now for so many centuries, it is fair he should have a rest, and
that we should revert to Aristotle's example, "Man is just." A singular
proposition was [del]said[/del] to be one in which the subject is a singular term.
A singular term was defined as "qui aptus natus est praedicari de uno [ins]solo[/ins],"
[del]that is, it is a proper noun.[/del] Kant and other [ins]modern[/ins] logicians very rightly
chop the indefinite propositions which merely arise from the imperfect
expression of what is meant. Singular propositions are for the
purposes of formal logic equivalent to universal ones.
