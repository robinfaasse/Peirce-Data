Boethius became very learned. He early received high
honors, such as the consulate, and finally was appointed to a
[del]high[/del] most responsible post in the palace of Theodoric. He
had married the daughter of Symmachus, a man of vast wealth
and great personal worth. His wife was a very superior woman, and
his four sons were all he could desire. Two of them were
consuls. But when Justin issued his edicts against the
Arians, Boetius was arrested upon some trumped up
charge, [del]and[/del] and was first imprisoned. It was in this im-
prisonment that he wrote his book [u]The Consolation of Philosophy[/u].
The next year he was put to death.

St. Isidorus, the greatest scholar of his age, became bishop
of Seville A.D. 600 and died A.D. 636. Among his numerous
works is an encyclopaedia in 20 books, of which the second
treats of logic, partly. Notwithstanding its brevity, it is by no
means useless in reference to the history of logical terminology.

In the ninth century, the Irish monks kept alive a

treatise [u]de divisione naturae[/u] which has some interest for
the history of reasoning.
