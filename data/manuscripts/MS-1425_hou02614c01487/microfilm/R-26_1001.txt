The ambition of Lyon Playfair was at first directed toward scientific eminence.
[del]A[/del] [ins]Pupil[/ins] of two of the greatest chemists that a man born in 1818 could well have
for masters, Graham and Liebig, he made, [del]of[/del] as chemists always do, several discoveries.
The chief of these was that of the nitroprussides. It was already known that the coffee-
colored solution formed by adding nitric acid to ferrocyanide of potassium contained
something peculiar and interesting, and it was no great feat to neutralize the acid
and isolate the salts, especially as they yield magnificent crystals. Playfair never
threw any light on [del]the[/del] constitution of their acid. Apart from this, his two chief
researches were carried on in collaboration, the one with Bunsen, the other
with Joule. Some of Playfair's own theories, — such as that the specific
gravities of the elements are proportional to different powers of their atomic
weights, — are downright absurd. On the whole, the evidence is that his purely
scientific genius was not extraordinary. That this was the opinion of Faraday
