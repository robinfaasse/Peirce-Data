The Census Theorem of Euler* takes the form
$$N_5 = 12 + N_7 + 2N_8 + 3N_9 + 4N_{10} + \text{etc.}$$
where $N_i$ is the number of regions of $i$ boundaries. It
maybe remarked that some sets of values of the $N$s which
this formula would permit are nevertheless impossible.
For example, there cannot be a map [ins]of this sort[/ins] with just one
region of more than five boundaries. Moreover, many
arrangements of the polygons are impossible.

It is to be expected that the problem would be simplified
if we could reduce the number of classes, or colors, from
four to two. In order to effect that result, I consider
the corners instead of the regions. The problem then takes
the following form:

[u]It is required to prove that in every map of the kind[/u]
[u]considered, it is possible to assign to each corner one of the[/u]
[u]two values $+1$ and $-1$ in such a way that the sum of the values[/u]
[u]of all the corners of every region shall be divisible by [del]three[/del] 3.[/u]

In order to show that this is [ins]essentially[/ins] the same as the original problem,
let me first propose a definition. Since in our map three
boundaries meet at each corner, not only does
every boundary separate two confine regions,
as $b$ and $c$ in Fig. 4, but it may also be said to [u]tie[/u]
two peneconfine regions, as $a$ and $d$ in [del]the[/del] [ins]the[/ins] same figure.
