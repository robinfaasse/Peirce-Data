Roughly, we divide terms into the universal or general,
the singular, and the indefinite, or vague, or "particular."
A proper name is set down as singular, although in
absolute strictness, of course it is not so. The plural
of a common noun is usually universal or general.
For every object of thought is One. The plural is one
object, one collective object distributively taken, "Men
are mortal," "pigs have great pointed snouts." The sin-
gular of a common noun is usually indefinite
"A man married a wife," i.e. a man not speci-
fied. The singular term [del]determinately[/del] [ins]apparently[/ins] denotes the very
object. The general term leaves the object partly
indeterminate, and leaves the person addressed (and
there always is a person addressed, for solitary thought is
dialogue) to make the further determination at
his pleasure. "Men are mortal" = "Any man you please
is mortal." The indefinite term is partly indeter-
minate, but the speaker reserves the determination.
"A man marries" = "A man [del]who might be instanced[/del] [ins]and a woman[/ins] marries."
The general term, [del]in so far as it is,[/del] and in those respects
in which it is general, is not subject to the principle
of excluded middle. [del]That[/del] "Men are wise" and "men are
foolish" ("men" being taken generally,) may be alike false.
The indefinite term, in so far as, and where, it is indefi-
nite, is not subject to the principle of contradiction.
