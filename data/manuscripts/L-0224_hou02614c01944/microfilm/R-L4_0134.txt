In order to show what this means, I will remark
that he writes [del]$O$[/del] $E(\alpha)$, $E(\alpha\gamma)$ etc to assert that
$\alpha$, or $(\alpha\gamma)$, or whatever collection follows the $E$, is
not an $O$-collection. Therefore his first clause
may otherwise be stated, thus:
No matter what collections $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ may be,
if $E(\alpha\gamma)$ [ins]is true,[/ins] [del]then[/del] $E(\alpha)$ is true. Or, if we write $\kappa$
for $\alpha\gamma$, it will read thus:
No matter what collection $\kappa$ may be, so
long as $\alpha$ is a part of it, if $\kappa$ is an $E$-collection
so also is $\alpha$.

That is, to say that $\alpha$ is an $E$ collection implies
(besides what may be implied in Clause II) that the
members of this collection have a relationship
which, if it [del]is[/del] subsists between all, subsists also
between [del]any[/del] some of them.

This is precisely Aristotle's definition of a universal
predication, which has been adopted by all logicians. I
[del]gave it on p. 179 of the April Monist.[/del] Therefore to say
that any collection is an $E$-collection, or is of the $E$-form,
implies (besides what Clause 2 may imply) only that [del]all[/del]
some predicate is true of all the members of that col-
lection.
