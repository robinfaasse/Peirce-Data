Two letters of our alphabet have accrued to it
since the introduction of printing. They are [u]v[/u], which
was formerly merely the initial form of [u]u[/u], and [u]j[/u] which
has been differentiated from [u]i[/u], [del]while the tail had[/del]
no tail in English until well into the XVIIth century.
This explains why it is that certain orthographical
rules applicable to [u]i[/u] and [u]u[/u] are extended to [u]j[/u] and [u]v[/u].

Q and Z were not used in writing Anglo-Saxon,
and k very rarely. In Middle English, k [ins]lately[/ins] became common,
and [del]not[/del] long after q began to appear. The letter g was
differentiated into two forms, one of which took the shape
[u]ȝ[/u], which must have been well-known to those who
used it as a zed; for it was an extremely common
letter in Anglo-Norman. It was sounded, however,
nearly like a consonantal y.
