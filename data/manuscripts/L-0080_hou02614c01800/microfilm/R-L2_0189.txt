[u]Teleological[/u] proof, that proof of theism which is
based upon the principle of final causation. Caldecott
mentions three varieties of this proof: the first is from
the teleological nature of biological organisms; the second
is drawn from the adaptations of independent organisms
and circumstances to one another; the third is drawn from
the teleological unity of the whole universe, as showing
one order throughout. The last argument may be put as
an analogy or as a direct inference.

[u]parallelism[/u], [u]n.[/u] [u]6.[/u] The opinion that the re-
[del]lation of matter with matter and that of Mind[/del]
[del]with Mind suffice to explain all the action of[/del]
[del]matter and of ideas, and that there is no ulti-[/del]
[del]mate and immediate causation between the[/del]
[del]brain and the mind in sensation and volition.[/del]
Specifically called [u]psychophysical parallelism[/u].
Acceptance of this principle of parallelism, as a work-
ing hypothesis in psychology, is compatible with all the
solutions and difficulties into which the principle of dual
action plunges us.
F. B. [u]Titchener[/u], Outline of Psychol., p. 361.
The idea meant to be conveyed by the spatial brand of

— [u]Epistemologi-[/u]
[u]cal parallelism[/u], the opinion of some philosophers of
the general following of Kant that space and time, which
are native forms of the understanding, are at the same
time dominant in the absolutely objective world; a posi-
tion held by Schleiermacher, Herbart, and Trendelenburg. The
term cannot properly be applied to a similar doctrine
held by pre-Kantian writers; otherwise it must be attri-
buted to all those before Kant who believed in innate
ideas.
