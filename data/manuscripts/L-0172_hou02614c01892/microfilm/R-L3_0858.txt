If, however, there was originally an apposition, it might happen
that the final letter of the preposition would point to the
case it preferably governs. In point of fact, it happens that
Greek prepositions ending in ι require the dative, while the only
ones that forbid the dative (except ὑπέρ) end in α or ς; and
all prepositions ending in α or ς, except παρά and πρός forbid
the dative. All these prefer the accusative except ἕνεκα, διά,
κατά, μετά, and ἐξ, which more or less incline to the genetive.
[del]All[/del] others except ἀμφί, ἐπί, περί, ὑπό, ὑπέρ require the gene-
tive. Those which forbid the genitive are σύν, ἐν, εἰς, ὡς, ἀνά.

In Latin in, being used for εἰς as well as for ἐν, takes either case.
Cum (with [u]coram[/u] and [u]palam[/u]) takes an instrumental ablative, as σύν
does a similar dative. All ending in a and e, except a, e, de, [ins]se,[/ins] sine, take
the accusative, while those ending with other vowels ([del]i[/del] including prae)
take the ablative. Pro like πρό, and prae requiring, while περί prefers, the
ablative or genitive. All ending in s (or any other consonant, with the
exceptions of abs, tenus, [del]but[/del] ex, coram, cum, procul, sed) take the
accusative. Ab like ἀπό, ex like ἐξ, per like παρά (usually), super like ὑπέρ,
sub like ὑπό. Ob may be the accusative of a noun meaning "back", of which
[del]ἐπί[/del] is the locative (preferring dative accordingly) and ἀπό instrumental
(preferring genitive accordingly).
