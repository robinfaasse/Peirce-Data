Every term may be conceived as formed by the conjunction of
certain minimum terms called singulars. Thus we may write
$$a = A +_1 A' +_1 A'' +_1 A''' +_1 \text{etc}$$
These singulars are supposed none of them to be the absolute mini-
mum 0. We may drop the accents which only serve in this
formula to distinguish the [del]separate[/del] A's as different and write
$$a = A + A + A + \text{etc} = \Sigma A$$
Here the sign $+$ implies [del]itself[/del] the different denotation of
the A's. If only a part of the singulars are written
$$A +_1 A' +_1 A''$$
we denote a part of $a$ but if we write this in the form
$$A + A + A$$
then we indicate distinctly the indifference what part is in-
dicated. In this way we get the conception of number. [del]As[/del]
$A +_1 A' = A$ is always false, so $A + A = A$ is false.

We now come to operations of the second order. Relative terms
also have their singulars. Let $(A:B)$ be such a term that it [del]has[/del]
denotes nothing by itself but written before the singular $B$
gives $(A:B)B = A$ and written before any other singular [ins]or before 0[/ins] gives
$(A:B)C = 0$. On the same principle we [del]may[/del] write
$$(A:B)(B:C)C = (A:[del]B[/del])B = A$$
Since therefore $(A:B)(B:C)$ written before $C$ gives $A$ and [del]written[/del] [ins]before[/ins]
any other singular or zero gives zero, we have,
$$\underline{(A:B)(B:C) = (A:C)}$$
