First, what is the logic of relatives? It is a subject treated in all the more complete medieval handbooks of logic, and hinted at by Aristotle himself. But the first hint of how it might appear in a modern scientific discussion was first put forth by [del]Ellis,[/del] the editor of Bacon's Philosophical Works. In [date]1858[/date], Augustus DeMorgan published a [del]work[/del] [ins]memoir[/ins] of 50 quarto pages on the subject, in which he laid down many of the fundamental principles, and above all, showed that instead of being a special branch of logic, it was really far more general than the old science. In [date]1870[/date] appeared the first of a series of contributions by [ins]the American[/ins] Mr. C. S. Peirce, [del]one[/del] of which [ins]acknowledges[/ins] forms the basis of the present volume by Dr. Schröder, who has made important additions of his own, and put the whole into a more scientific statement. Mr. Peirce's scholars have added a great deal, especially Prof. O. C. Mitchell of Marietta. At this point, the subject began to attract attention of students scattered everywhere. Most of them followed more or less closely the methods of Peirce. But Mr. A. B. Kempe, sometime President of the London Mathematical Society, in a great memoir in the Philosophical Transactions, has struck out a new method of his own.
