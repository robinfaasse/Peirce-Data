[del]Each[/del] [ins]and familiar[/ins] scheme of quantity [ins](to notice only the [ins]more[/ins] important schemes)[/ins] is that which is connected like the points of a line. This gives real continuous quantity. It embraces the system of integer numbers as a part of it. Next, there is the scheme [del]which[/del] which is connected like the points of a plane. This is the system of "complex" quantity [del]chiefly deals[/del] [ins]with which the[/ins] theory of functions chiefly deals. There is nothing more incomprehensible about it than the points on a plane. Certain natural phenomena, especially in two-dimensional hydrodynamics, have a natural correspondence with such a scheme, just as the flow of time has with real quantity. But the utility of the scheme is no more limited to cases in which such [ins]a[/ins] correspondence exists, than the usefulness of numbers is limited to counting collections that are really arranged in a [del]line[/del] [ins]linear[/ins] series. This is usually, but not very distinctly, called the system of complex quantity. It might better be termed superficial quantity. The system of quantity of quaternions, which is connected like the points of a four-dimensional space, is more complex.
