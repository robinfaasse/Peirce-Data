[pn]§3.[/pn] [u]A General Type of Argument[/u].

If an argument has more premises than one, there
may, as a first step, be combined into one complex pro-
position. A proposition which formally asserts the truth
of every one of a number of propositions is termed, alike
by grammarians and logicians [u]copulative[/u][ins]*[/ins]. The opera-
tion of thus collecting premises into one may be called
[u]colligation[/u], in imitation of Whewell's "colligation of facts,"
which is [del]substantially[/del] [ins]substantially[/ins] the same [del]operation[/del] [ins]thing[/ins]. It is in
form an argumentation; and, in some cases, is
no small nor easy advance toward the final con-
clusion.

of view, for a moment, and to consider, in place of
the argument addressed to a second person, the
mental process of [u]inference[/u], which is a spontaneous,
