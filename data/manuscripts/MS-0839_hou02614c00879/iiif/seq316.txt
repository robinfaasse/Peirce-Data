2nd Consider the two matrices
$$\begin{matrix} A & B & \Gamma \\ \Gamma & A & B \\ B & \Gamma & A \end{matrix} \quad \begin{matrix} Z & E & \Delta \\ E & \Delta & Z \\ \Delta & Z & E \end{matrix}$$
Take $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta, \epsilon, \zeta$ as such matrices that the above two are
$$\begin{matrix} \alpha A + \beta B + \gamma \Gamma \\ \delta \Delta + \epsilon E + \zeta Z \end{matrix}$$
Then we have the equation
$$\alpha + \beta + \gamma = \delta + \epsilon + \zeta$$
It is plain that if we write
$$h = \frac{1}{3} (\alpha + \beta + \gamma)$$
$h^2 = h$, and $u$ being any [del]other[/del] [ins]math[/ins] linear expression in $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta, \epsilon, \zeta$,
$$hu = uh = N_u h$$
where $N_u$ is the sum of the coefficients of $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta, \epsilon, \zeta$. But it is obviously possible to find 4 mutually independent quantities for each of which $N = 0$.
Let us inquire how many such quantities can be idempotent. We have, in the first place, $\gamma^2 = \beta, \delta^2 = \alpha, \gamma \delta = \zeta, \delta \gamma = \epsilon,$ so that
[del]$(\delta - \gamma)^2 = (\alpha - \zeta - \epsilon + \beta) = \delta - \gamma$[/del]
Let us, then, take [del]$i = \delta - \gamma$[/del], [del]Now $\mathcal{A}$ is unity,[/del]
so that [del]$(\alpha - i)^2 = \alpha - i$[/del] and $i(\alpha - i) = (\alpha - i) i = 0$
