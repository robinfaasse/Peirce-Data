Criterion is taken in two senses; for the rule is called the criterion in one sense and the
object whose determination is evidence is called a criterion in another sense.
The Oxf. Dict. says it is a test, principle, rule, canon, or standard. Now test, principle,
rule and canon seem to be the all one. But the [u]standard[/u] seems to be the object determined.
Fuller says "the moving of a statue" was made the criterion of women's chastity." (But whether
its moving or not, emits moving one way or another, is the criterion is not said.) In any case, it
[del]seems[/del] he seems to mean that the principle is the criterion, and yet he does not say so. [del]He is this[/del]
he says [del]the[/del] state of the statue is to motion that is the criterion. This is the object determined.
But Blackstone says "some probation or trial, which the law of the country has ordained for a
criterion of truth and falsehood" Here the [u]trial[/u] is the object determined. But whether the mode of trial
is so, or not, is another question. That mode remains the same whichever way the trial issues. A mode
of trial, being ordained, would seem to be the rule.
Mrs. Hughes says "regular uniformity and the straight line were the criterions of taste and
beauty" She probably means that the rule that where there is regular uniformity & straight lines
there there is taste or beauty; That would make the rule the criterion; but she expresses herself
so inaccurately that it is hard to say.
[ins]Another quotation (from Tales of Sedley) says "Lord Stokesland the criterion of gallantry and politeness"
