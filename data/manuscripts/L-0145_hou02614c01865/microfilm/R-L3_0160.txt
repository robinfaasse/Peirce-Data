Princeton, [date]Dec. 26th / 1900[/date].

My dear Professor Peirce,

Professor Baldwin has shown me your letter of protest against the definition of Continuity for his Dictionary signed H.B.F. It is not likely that I am making an unexpected recreation in confessing to the authorship of this article.

My reply to your criticism is simply this. You entirely misconceive what I have attempted to do. Baldwin wished from me as clear and vivid a statement as I could give, intelligible to non-mathematical readers, of the mathematician's present working definition of continuity. Now I do not need to tell you that that definition concerns itself with [ins]ordinal[/ins] assemblages of points or numbers — and [del]not with[/del] [ins]not with[/ins] the more profound question, which you handle so ably, of the "connection between the parts of time" (or of a line) "without regard to their order of sequence or to their being limited to one dimension." I have said nothing about the line's being [u]made up[/u] of points and I cannot imagine that any man in his senses can have such a conception of its constitution. My task is about the assemblage of [del]the[/del] points on the line and I have merely given Dedekind's formulation of what the everyday mathematician means when he calls that assemblage continuous. And by points I mean only what you call "designable points".

As to the proposition on which you ground your criticism: That one can define an assemblage which is richer in individuals than the assemblage which I have called continuous, I am ready to grant it of course. In fact it seems to me easy to convince oneself of its truth by very elementary [del]studies[/del] [ins]considerations[/ins].
