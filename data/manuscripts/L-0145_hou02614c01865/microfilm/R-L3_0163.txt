[u]Princeton[/u] [date]Jan. 14. 1902[/date].

My dear Professor Peirce,

I feel like a poltroon when your second letter arrived because my delay in answering the first one had put off the trouble to write it. That delay was due to a brief absence from Princeton and severe carelessness on my return which I hope you will pardon in the sense of my having been very much occupied. I think both applications of the fraction very interesting and I have never seen either of them before.

And at the same time your critique of Cantor and Russell is in the Bulletin or the Transactions of the American Math. Soc. It would have a host of interested readers, Cantor himself among them. I am ashamed to say that I have so far forgotten Cantor's [del]proofs[/del] that I cannot now compare it with yours - which I was greatly impressed by. It gave me a realization [del]of the mathematical[/del] of the logic of relations of the power of which I never had before.

You are quite right in saying that the differences between us are chiefly in the use of words - discreet for instance; and probably you are right as to Weierstrass's proof; and the readers of Baldwin's Dictionary. At the same time, I am bound to say that in my opinion you are a mathematician, and one who has done more thinking about the foundations than most mathematicians do, and is able to understand the proofs of the existence of [del]abnormal[/del] limits etc. G. Cantor is "eminently" a man of his kind.
