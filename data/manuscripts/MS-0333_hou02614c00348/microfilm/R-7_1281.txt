The [del]word[/del] [ins]term[/ins] means literally a boundary or end. It is
the Latin [u]terminus[/u], which translates the Greek ὅρος (Hóros),
a landmark. Aristotle, the father of logic (384-322 B.C.), applied
the word to those parts of propositions which he denoted by letters.
The early logicians (perhaps Aristotle himself) used diagrams in
which the propositions were represented by lines of which
the extremities [del]were[/del] represented the subject and predicate. The
word ὅρος may have been applied to a name, either as being
marked by a letter or as being the bound of a line.

The [del]word[/del] [ins]proposition[/ins] (Latin [u]propositio[/u]) is a word formed
by Cicero (106-43 B.C.) in imitation of the Greek πρόσληψις. Namely
[u]pro[/u] = πρός means 'over and above', and [u]positio[/u], a placing, corresponds
to λῆψις a taking. The word πρόσληψις had a special meaning
in Greek logic which we will not stop to explain; but before
the second century of our era [u]propositio[/u] had acquired its
present meaning of something written, spoken, or thought which
is by its nature either true or false. The word [u]judgment[/u], Latin
[u]judicium[/u], has borne the same [ins]logical[/ins] meaning from [del]an[/del] [ins]the[/ins] time of
Cicero: but the legal meaning is the earliest, [u]judicare[/u], to judge, is
[ins]jus dicere, to speak the right.[/ins]
