PRACTICAL ARITHMETIC.

CHAPTER I. INTRODUCTION.

Practical arithmetic is the knowledge of how to use numbers. In this practice, two qualities are to be aimed at,–accuracy first, and also dispatch. The necessity of accuracy in the use of numbers is plain; the man who only claims ten dollars when a hundred are due him will soon come to grief, while he who claims a hundred when only ten are due is in greater danger, still. The advantage of being able to make calculations rapidly, can only be appreciated by experience. Time must be taken by the fore-lock; opportunities occur which must be seized promptly, or not at all; and upon these the success of life often depends. But wise decisions requires calculation [del]and rapid calculation calls for rapid calculation.[/del] [ins]which must be rapid.[/ins]

[del]Many[/del] [ins]A few[/ins] rules for attaining these ends, will be found in this book: [del]but[/del] [ins]but[/ins] a few general maxims may be given at the outset.

I. Do not make too much exertion; or rather, exert yourself easily and composedly.

II. Think of what you are about, and keep all wandering thoughts out of your mind.

III. Never trust to a result being right on the first calculation; but perform the computation again, in a [del]secondary[/del] [ins]different[/ins] way, if possible; or retrace the work from the last step to the first, or otherwise assure yourself that no mistake has been committed. The best arithmeticians sometimes (though rarely) commit errors.

IV. Try to view every problem from a practical standpoint; imagine yourself in a situation in which you can immediately see, if not exactly, at least approximately, what the result will be.

V. Write your work [del]in such a form[/del] [ins]so[/ins] that anybody can see, at once, what you have done, [del]that the[/del] [ins]and that you have performed[/ins] the right operation [del]to be performed,[/del] and [del]that[/del] you have performed it rightly. CHAPTER 2.

Counting, Measuring, and Decimal Names of Numbers.

[del]The[/del] [ins]or count-words[/ins] [u]numerals[/u] or cardinal numbers are a series of words used to count with. They are, [del]one,[/del] [ins]one,[/ins] two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve, etc. [del]A[/del] [ins]They are words with which a count-word is associated[/ins]

To count a collection or lot of things, [del]is to connect these numerals successively with the things, one numeral with each thing,[/del] [ins]is to take them in their order, beginning with [u]one[/u], and connect them with the things[/ins] – as, for example, by pointing at the things one after another and calling the numerals in regular order as we point. [del]If[/del] [ins]When[/ins] there remain no more of the lot connected with numerals, the last of these used is the number of things in the lot. This number will be the same in whatever order the things are counted. Thus, the number of the evangelists is four, Matthew, one; Mark, two; Luke, three; and John, four: or, John, one; Matthew, two; Mark, three; and Luke, four.

Thus, one, two, three, four, etc. in addition to serving as count words, somewhat like [u]many[/u], [u]meany[/u], [u]mony[/u], [u]mi[/u], are also used to designate the lot. A lot of four things is one which needs [u]one[/u], [u]two[/u], [u]three[/u], and [u]four[/u], but not [u]five[/u] to count it; and so forth. These numerals are called cardinal numbers, that is the principal numbers. There is besides another set, called ordinal numbers which are applied to the different individual things at which the different count-words are called.
