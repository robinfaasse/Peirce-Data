PRACTICAL ARITHMETIC.

CHAPTER I. INTRODUCTION.

Arithmetic is all there is to be known about numbers. Practical arithmetic is all there is
to be learned about using numbers. It is the most indispensable branch of knowledge, after
reading and writing. [del]The rest of the chapter is not to be studied at once, but referred to as occasion
rises, until familiar.[/del]

In practising it, two qualities are to be aimed at, accuracy first, and then dispatch. The
need of accuracy is the use of numbers is while the need of dispatch is that we may not be stun-
ned by the details. No one is more likely to make mistakes than a hundred when calculation is due;
is in greater danger still. It is only by experience that we can fully appreciate the advantage
of being able to make calculations rapidly. Every man finds, however, that time must often be
the success of opportunity. Decision must be based upon calculation, and rapid decision
requires rapid calculation. So you can see that rapidity of calculation must be a valuable accomplish-
ment. I shall here set down a few general maxims for the attainment of these ends, - accuracy and
dispatch. They should be placed where they can be easily referred to, and we should mean to follow
them, as in everything else, is most needful for us to remind ourselves to what we mean to do,
by often calling to mind the ends at which we aim, by reviewing the means at our disposition for
the attainment of those ends, and by sternly calling ourselves to account for every fault we
commit. To use those means of self-control is what we mean by "trying", and it is the only sense
that speaking to us more than to children could only know what that word means. If our ans-
their childhood would be gone at once. You cannot know; but you can see how serious grown people
are, and you can guess that they have had a glance at something that has made them serious.
Mind then, these admonitions now, and get your lessons now, and then, when you come to grow up, that Gor-
gon's head will not stare you so terribly in the face.

I. Do not try too hard, but exert yourself coolly. Keep your mind upon your sum, and wan-
dering thoughts away.

II. Strictly, follow every rule, and mind every trifling recommendation in the book; small
things come to something considerable, in arithmetic.

III. Set down your work in so neat a shape that every arithmetician can see [ins]at a glance[/ins] what you have
undertaken to do, that you have done the right thing, and that you have done it correctly.

IV. When a problem is set you, begin by imagining roughly the state of things it supposes,
so that you can see about what the answer will be, and thus avoid gross blunders, such as mis-
taking dollars for cents and bushels for months.

V. Prove every result.

VI. Practise incessantly, both systematically and unsystematically, in school and out of
school. Be always setting yourself sums.

CHAPTER II. NUMERATION.

[fig]Table of numbers from one to eleven represented by dots and words[/fig]

The series of words used in counting are called count-words, numerals, or cardinal numbers.
At the end of the chapter I shall give some explanation of the reasons of these expressions, and
their different meanings.

What is counting? The pupil can already count; to answer the question, then, he need only
observe what it is that he does when he counts. However, as he has not yet learned to observe,
I must help him a little with it. To count a collection of things is to take the things singly,
and to give a count-word to each, using the count-words in their regular sequence, beginning
with one. For example, we may point to the things one after another, calling a number at each
we point; and we must call off the numbers in their regular order.
