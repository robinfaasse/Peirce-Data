CHAPTER I. INTRODUCTION.

ARITHMETIC is the whole of what is to be known about numbers. Practical arithmetic is the whole of what is to be learned about using numbers. It is the most indispensable branch of knowledge, after reading and writing.

[del]This chapter is not to be studied at first, but to be referred to by the teacher as occasion delivers when the pupil is hard of it, until it becomes familiar.[/del]

In practising arithmetic, two qualities are to be aimed at, -- accuracy, first, and then dispatch. Accuracy is the hundred of numbers, is plain: the man who [del]calculates[/del] [ins]reckons[/ins] only claims ten dollars when a hundred are due to him, is soon come to grief; while he who claims a hundred when only ten are due is in greater danger, still, of being [del]prosecuted[/del] [ins]sued[/ins] for theft. But few after taking up practice the advantage of calculating rapidly; but every man finds that time must be saved, and upon the success of opportunities often depends, which must be seized promptly, if not at all, and rapid decision for rapid calculation. So you can see that rapid calculation must be often and accomplished, and I shall set down a few general maxims for the attainment of these ends. -- First, for accuracy, in arithmetic, as in everything else, we must be careful, and readily turn back to them; we mean to do by often calling to mind the ends at which we aim, by reviewing the means at our disposal for the attainment of those ends, and by sternly calling ourselves to account for every fault we commit. I. Do not try too hard, but exert yourself coolly. Keep your mind upon your sum and wanderin thoughts away.

II. Strictly follow every rule, and mind every trifling recommendation in the book. Small things come to something considerable in arithmetic.

III. Set down your work in so neat a state that every arithmetician can see at a glance what you have undertaken to do, that you have done the right thing, and that you have done it correctly.

IV. When a problem is set you, begin by imagining roughly the state of things it supposes, so that you can see roughly what the answer will be, and thus avoid gross blunders, such as mistaking dollars for cents on bushels for years.

V. Prove every result.

VI Practise incessantly, both systematically and unsystematically, in school and out of school. Be always setting yourself sums.

The series of words used in counting are called count-words, numerals, or cardinal numbers. See the end of the chapter for further explanations.

What is counting? The pupil can already count: to answer my question, then, he has only to observe what it is that he does when he counts. But he has not yet learned to observe: so I must help him out. To count a collection of things is to take the things singly, and give a count-word to each, using the count-words in their regular sequence, beginning with one. For example, we may point to the things one after another, calling a number at each as we point: and we must call off the numbers in their regular order, one, two, three, etc. During this process, each thing, as soon as it receives a count-word, is said to be counted, and when all the things have received count-words, the whole lot is said to be counted. The last count-word is the number of things in the collection.

Two different lots of things can be counted at the same time, by taking a thing of one lot with the right hand and a thing of the other lot with the [del]left[/del] left hand each time a number is called. This is called double counting.

In counting things from memory, we can point at our fingers. This is called finger-counting. It is useful in aiding us to keep the run of the numbers.

When some time elapses between the occurrences to be counted, a good way is to keep tally. We set down an upright stroke for each thing, placing the first four strokes side by side. The fifth we write across the other four from the top of the first to the bottom of the last of them. We then go on to make four strokes side by side, and draw the next across these four, but this time from the bottom of the first to the top of the last of them. And so we go on, alternately making the cross-strokes one way and the other way, so that we can easily count up the sets of ten. We have now to study the system of count-words.

We have now to study the system of count-words. The first ten are simple words: one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten. The next is eleven. A thousand years ago, and more, this was called enlevene. The en means one, just as an in "an egg" means one. The leven seems to mean ten; so that eleven means one-ten. The next count-word is twelve, or twa-leve, two-ten. We now go on to thirteen, three-ten; fourteen, four-ten; fifteen, five-ten; sixteen, six-ten; seventeen, seven-ten; eighteen, eight-ten; nineteen, nine-ten.
