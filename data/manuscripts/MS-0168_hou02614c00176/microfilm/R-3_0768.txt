To understand what a fraction is, look at your metre scale. It consists of ten numbered
joints, all of which are equal; that is to say, one will fit over another. Each of these
is called one of the small parts, of course, is called a tenth part, or tenth, of it. In like
manner each of these joints has ten larger divisions upon it. We can compare these, by putting
the ends of two of the small parts together, and laying their ends down on the paper, and then
carrying it forward by another. In this manner of other we will find I think, that the ten
parts of a joint are all equal; and they are tenths of it. Now, of these large divisions
there are ten in each of the small parts, so that there are ten times ten, that is a hundred in ten
tens, and so on. As long as each of these small parts on the metre is ten joints, the metre is
ten tens of these divisions. If of this we make clear to you, it is little trouble to count
the divisions in a metre. So then, there being a hundred small divisions in a metre, each of
these is called its hundredth part, or hundredth. Now, if these divisions had not been equal,
though there were a hundred of them, they would not be called "hundredths". Again, you will see
that there are still smaller divisions, and these small divisions,
one hundred of them, are all equal. They, of course are tenths of the large divisions, and
hundredths of the joints. Many of them are there in the whole metre? If you do not care
to count them, you may find out by reckoning. [del]In a metre[/del] [ins]A hundred of them[/ins], and the metre
