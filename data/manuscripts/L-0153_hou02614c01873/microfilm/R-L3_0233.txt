EDITORIAL ROOMS OF THE STANDARD DICTIONARY.

NEW YORK, [date]Aug. 6th, 1891.[/date]

C. S. Peirce, Ph.D.,
Milford, Pa.

Dear Sir:

Yours of the 1st, inst., came to hand. We are a little at a loss to understand your meaning when you say, "You (we) rely a little too much on the plan of sponging upon people for gratuitous assistance." We think that we can safely say that no dictionary ever prepared had a larger corps of paid workers upon it than has the "Standard." We have many workers upon our Dictionary who were engaged upon the "International" and upon the "Century." In every instance, we are paying as high salaries as either of these dictionaries paid---in many instances higher to the same persons.

It is certainly not an unusual thing for editors, authors and publishers to send samples of their work to scholars and other public men for criticism or suggestion, especially if the work is of so complex and costly a nature as that of a dictionary. Of course, we would not for a moment compare the "Standard Dictionary" to the new English Dictionary of Dr. Murray, But as you well know Dr. Murray again and again has asked assistance from scholars all over the world, and almost always this assistance was to be rendered gratuitously.
