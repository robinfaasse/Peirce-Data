[u]The Constellations[/u]

Before the times of telescopes astronomers used to
designate stars by stating upon what parts of the
conventional figures of men and animals drawn on
globes they [del]were to[/del] [ins]might[/ins] be found. Thus, Sirius was the star
in the mouth of the dog, Canopus the star in the rudder
of the ship Argo, Arcturus the star between the legs of the
herdsman, Capella the star on the left shoulder of the
charioteer, etc. In fact, the constellations, or groupings
of stars named after those figures, remain in use
to this day. They now number 85; but about half
of these are modern and uninteresting. The rest are
of great antiquity, and stimulate our curiosity by
many a mysterious circumstance.

The stars have been catalogued many times.
The oldest such list that has come down to us is
that contained in Ptolemy's Almagest, a celebrated astro-
nomical treatise by an Egyptian-Greek living about [date]A.D. 140[/date].
