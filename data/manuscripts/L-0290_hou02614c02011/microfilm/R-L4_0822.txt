[u]84 Broad St.[/u], [date]1897 March 26[/date].

My dear Miller:

For years I have not only not attempted to write for pure amusement before, [del]nor have I[/del] [ins]but have not[/ins] read amusing things nor talked with amusing people nor frequented any kind of amusements, — for which I have lost all taste. So if this my first attempt is a little owly, please remember this: Everybody who has tried me has [del]been[/del] remarked my surprising readiness at learning things that did not come natural to me. If I receive substantial encouragement, you may be assured that I shall improve fast and faster (and I can be useful to you in more ways than one.) But if I don't, I shall have no option but to turn to something else. So I advise you to try this thing, if it will at all do.

Now my plan is this. This first week appears this starter. Next Sunday I have a better paper about infinite collections, — both amusing and very valuable to thinkers; and we print the best of the letters that come in. The third Sunday, I return to the problem, — criticize the letters, show what a flood of light is thrown upon the question by what had been said the Sunday before about Infinity, but not applied to this problem, and bring out [del]new[/del] various unnoticed features of the problem. —

By that time, the readers are thoroughly interested. Attention is called to this corner. I [del]take[/del] care not to bore them with one thing; and I take up some amusing performances with cards, which at first blush appear as very pretty [del]and[/del] neat tricks or in some cases patience-games; but as [del]we[/del] [ins]they[/ins] study them, turn out to have depths of interest in them of another kind. My general idea is a frothy surface and deep soundings.

Yours faithfully
C. S. Peirce
84 Broad St.
