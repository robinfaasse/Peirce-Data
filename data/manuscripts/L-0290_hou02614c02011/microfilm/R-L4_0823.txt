84 Broad St, [date]1897 March 27[/date]

My dear Miller:

For years I have not only not attempted to write for pure amusement before, but I have not even read anything amusing nor talked with amusing people nor frequented public amusements, — indeed I have lost my [del]taste[/del] [ins]inclination[/ins] for the whole thing. So if this my first attempt in that line be a little owly, I pray you to believe me when I say this: [u]Every person who has tried me has always been surprised at my readiness at learning things I was most awkward at in the beginning.[/u] If I receive substantial encouragement, you may be assured that I shall improve fast and faster (and besides, I might be useful to you in more ways than this one). But if I don't, I shall have no option but to turn to something else. So, I would advise you to give me a trial, if this beginning is at all passable.

My plan about it is this: Next Sunday, this starter. For the following Sunday a better paper, but a briefer one, about [u]Infinite Collections[/u], — entertaining mainly, but also extremely valuable to thinkers. That same day such really good letters as come in, — I mean those with any intelligent point at all, — to be judiciously cut and printed. The third Sunday I return to the problem, and show how what had been said the week before about Infinite Collections throws a perfect flood of unexpected light on the problem, lighting up many corners not [del]even[/del] suspected before; and I criticize the letters with such wit as I can.

I must next care not to bore readers with one thing. The fourth Sunday I take up some very pretty tricks and patience games with cards of my invention, and I show they have depths of serious interest in them. The general idea is a pretty froth upon the surface, a rich and sweet [ins]golden[/ins] custard of philosophy beneath.

very faithfully
C. S. Peirce
