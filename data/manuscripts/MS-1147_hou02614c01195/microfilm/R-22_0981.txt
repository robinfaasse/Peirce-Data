Still, all logicians of any learning, [del]like Whately,[/del] who wrote
on English, like Whately, spelled the word in the plural, as
they would in the singular, or in Latin, with [u]ss[/u]. But when
Hamilton wrote logical articles for the [u]Edinburgh Review[/u],
the [del]law[/del] lawyers who conducted that Journal spelled the
word in legal fashion; so that in Hamilton's writings we
find the word spelled both ways according as the spelling
was his own or not. The result was that modern makers
of dictionaries, etc., as well as proof-readers, followed
the [u]Edinburgh Review[/u], and ignorantly wrote [u]premise[/u],
where, no person competent to hold an opinion would
at present doubt that [u]premiss[/u] was the preferable
spelling. [del][[/del] A premise is a thing premised; a premiss
is a [ins]fact [del]or consideration of[/del] which logically[/ins] contributes to the determina-
tion of a conclusion of reasoning.
