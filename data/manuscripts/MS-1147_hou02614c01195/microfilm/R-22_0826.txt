Matter. [Fr. matière, L. materia]

A [del]term[/del] conception introduced, as far as we know, by Aristotle (Gr. [u]ὕλη[/u]).
It may almost be said to be the key to his philosophy. It is explained in the
first book of the [u]Physical Auscultations[/u], but there are significant passages in
the first book of the [u]De Generatione et Corruptione[/u]. Wherever there is change,
there must be something as subject of that change; and this is the Aristotelian
matter, defined 194 b 24 (and 1013 a 24) as that [u]ἐξ οὗ γίγνεταί τι[/u]
[u]ἐνυπάρχοντος[/u], that is, that germ that must be in anything in order
that out of it anything may be produced. Hence, it is often the synonym
of [u]δύναμις[/u], the rudimentary state of being.

Matter is usually, by Scotus, for example, distinguished into
[u]matter ex qua[/u], as above defined; [u]matter in qua[/u], which is a subject;
[u]matter circa quam[/u], which is an object. There are many other distinctions.

matter is its three terms; while its form is its Mood, Figure, etc. But
