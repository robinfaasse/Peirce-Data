Three sources of confusion of thought. In the first place, man
is endowed with a logical instinct, which, although not nearly
so unerring as many instincts of lower animals, has a fixity
which produces the effect of extreme self-confidence. This instinct
is an essential constituent of the faculty of reasoning; so that
we cannot reason about truth without making use of this
instinct. Now one confusion into which logicians have often
fallen ([del]C. Sigwart[/del] [ins]for example[/ins]) is between using this instinct in
reasoning about logic and taking it as a premiss in our
reasoning about logic. The confusion is the more difficult to
avoid because reasoning essentially involves a more or less
conscious reference to a possible class, or type, of reasonings
similar to the one in act, with the thought that all such reasoning
would lead [del]from[/del] [ins]to[/ins] the truth from a true premiss, or at any rate that
it would do so if steadily persisted in, or at least would be on
the whole conducive to the attainment of true knowledge, if true
knowledge be anyway possible. Thus in every act of reasoning
