[marginalia]Laws of Thought[/marginalia]

The principle of excluded middle includes in the relation of [del]not[/del] [ins]not-all[/ins] pairs of the form A:B.

We are thus led to see that there are three other similar pairs of formulae defining the relations of identity, coexistence, and incompossibility:

Whatever is A is identical with A: Includes all pairs A:A.

Whatever is identical with A is A: Excludes all pairs A:B.

Whatever is A is coexistent with A: Includes all pairs A:A.

Everything is either A or coexistent with A: Includes all pairs A:B.

Whatever is both A and incompossible with A is nothing: Excludes all pairs A:A.

Whatever is incompossible with A is A: Excludes all pairs A:B.

* Notes upon the history of the so-called Laws of Thought will be found in an appendix to Hamilton's Lectures on Logic and in Überweg's Logik. Neither account is satisfactory. If a writer casually interjects that nothing can both be and not be, though it be before the days when the principle of contradiction was prominent, [del]to[/del] [ins]say[/ins] that does not constitute a contribution to Logic; but it is simply a platitude. It is not enough, therefore, to be informed that certain writers have enunciated the different formulae.
