The older exact logicians, however, regard terms in the traditional
way, as class-names, without any predicative character, at all;
and even Schröder in his first two volumes argues strenuously
for this view.

A proposition need not, as far as exact logic requires,
have any other subject than a universe. Thus, in the condi-
tional proposition, "If A is true, B is true," it is not necessary
to ask whether the propositions A and B have any subjects
other than that universe of possibilities [ins]for[/ins] [del]to[/del] which the whole
proposition is intended to assert that the truth of A is
accompanied by the truth of B. A [ins]non-relative[/ins] categorical proposition
has a single subject, as "Something is a blue swan," or
"Some swan is blue," "Everything is either not-a phenix or else
rises from its ashes," etc. A relative proposition has
two or more subjects, as "Ashley Cooper was a grandfather
of the author of 'Characteristics,'" "A gives B to C," etc.
