[[[u]ἡ ἡ μετρική[/u], prosody, from [u]μέτρον[/u], a measure.]

The theory of measurement. Measurement is the expression of fact by means of number. This, in a narrower sense, confined to cases in which counting would not suffice. Numbers, whether integer or fractional, express, in themselves, nothing but serial order; and the whole [del]value[/del] arithmetic of fractions can be deduced from their order of succession, without any assumption about a unit being divided into equal parts. Now the mere serial order of points on a line can be expressed in all sorts of irregular ways. But to find a new system of numbers to express all real and imaginary quantities so as to preserve the serial orders, and to proceed [ins]by[/ins] [del]on[/del] one general [del]law[/del] rule, we [del]can[/del] must confine ourselves to a [del]linear[/del] [ins]lineo-linear[/ins] transformation. It follows very obviously that any two such systems of numbering will apply the same numbers to two [del]point[/del] quantities.
