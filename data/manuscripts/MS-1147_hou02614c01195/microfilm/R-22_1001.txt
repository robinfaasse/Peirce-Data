I am often satisfied myself, not absolutely but approximately,
that the information has been collected and arranged; so
that any reasonable man going through it will have his
doubts removed as mine have been. When that is done, I
call [del]the arrangement along with[/del] [ins]this array of[/ins] facts [del]and[/del] [ins]with[/ins] the indication of the process
of thought, [u]proof[/u]. But nobody without special training is
competent to say with any approach to certainty, whether
a given argument amounts to proof, or not.

The theory of proof consists of two great and
widely separated branches (in which we do not
include the general theory of knowledge upon which
they must be founded), the one relating to the critic
of proofs, the other to the Heuretic of proofs.

Proofs are of very different nature according to the
nature of the science or practical knowledge which they
are designed to further. They may be divided into, 1, mathe-
matical; 2, philosophical; 3, nomological (psychical and [ins]physical[/ins])
