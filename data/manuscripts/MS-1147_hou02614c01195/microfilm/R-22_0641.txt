[u]Leading Principle[/u]. Ger. [u]Leitende Princip[/u].

It is of the essence of reasoning that the reasoner should be conscious of proceeding according to a general method which he holds, in different kinds of reasoning, would either always lead to the truth, provided the premises were true, or, consistently adhered to, [del]always at length[/del] [ins]would[/ins] correct any [ins]degree of[/ins] error[del]s[/del] to which it might at first lead, or would [ins]in all similar cases[/ins] be conducive to the ascertainment [ins]when[/ins] of truth, [del]if[/del] [ins]if[/ins] the truth could be ascertained in any way. Suppose, then, that the premises of a reasoning are P and its conclusion C. The reasoner [del]is conscious of[/del] [ins]determines[/ins] this conclusion from by modifying P according to a general method, which he may not be able to define exactly, but which he believes to be correct in one of the three senses just stated. This method might be analyzed and its general correctness stated in a proposition, L. This proposition, which is the formula of the habit which governs the reasoner on this occasion, and which plays a different part from the premises in determining the conclusion, is called the [u]leading principle[/u].
