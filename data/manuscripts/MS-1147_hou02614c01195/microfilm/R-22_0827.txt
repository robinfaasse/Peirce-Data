The matter of a proposition is understood in two senses: materialiter,
it consists in the terms of the proposition, the copula being the essential
form; formaliter, the matter is the "habitudo extremorum ad invicem,"
that is, the general state of things in regard to the relation of that which
the subject denotes and that which the predicate signifies. This is the
matter which tradition, going back to the Greek commentators, [del]makes in[/del] [ins]John of Salisbury, Metalogicus IV. 14[/ins]
the Latin terminology to be either natural, contingent, and remote. These words
do not precisely correspond to the Greek; so that the doctrine was probably
derived through Arabian intermediaries. The Summulae define natural matter
as that in which the predicate is of the essence of the subject or a property of it,
contingent matter as that in which the predicate may be present to and may be
absent from the subject without destroying it; remote matter as that in which
the predicate cannot belong to the subject. These usual definitions are
