[pn]Modal[/pn] [[I. [u]modalis[/u], from [u]modus[/u], tr. of Gr. [u]τρόπος[/u].]]

Any qualification of a predication is called a mode.
But Hamilton slips when he says ([u]Lectures on Logic[/u], xiv) that all
logicians call any proposition affected by a mode, a modal.

From the time of Abelard (before which the expression is hardly
found, if at all) [ins]almost always[/ins] a modal proposition [del]for[/del] means a proposition qualified
by one of the [del]six[/del] [ins]four[/ins] modes [u]possibly[/u], [u]impossibly[/u], [u]contingently[/u], [u]necessarily[/u].
[del]Some add [u]truly[/u] and [u]falsely[/u], but this was not consistent with the accepted doctrine.[/del]
[del][u]Truly[/u] and [u]falsely[/u]; the last two are often dropped.[/del] The subject is
treated by Aristotle [u]De Interpretatione[/u] ch. xii. xiii. It is certainly
an extremely proper one for logical analysis; but the treatment
by Aristotle and his followers is so confused and unsatisfactory
that it is no wonder that it is almost entirely dropped from modern
treatises. The [del]old[/del] doctrine was that when in the proposition
"homo necessario sedet", the meaning is that the composition of
"session with man" is a necessary one. The four modes may be
taken either first intentionally or second intentionally, [del]or[/del] [ins]and[/ins] first
intentionally, either categorematically or syncategorematically.
