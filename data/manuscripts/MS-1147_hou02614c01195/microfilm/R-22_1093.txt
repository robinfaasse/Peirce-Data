Enough, and perhaps too much has been said as to the
mode of being of points, etc, in our hypothetical Time
and [del]the[/del] Space. It still remains to define [del]true[/del] continuity.

That which binds together possibilities is a general law. Although
Time and Space are presented to us in intuitional form, they are
nevertheless, as Leibniz said, of the nature of general laws. In-
deed Kant's word "form" imports as much. They are generals
differing from ordinary generals in no other than those two
respects in which every conception of the logic of relations
differs from the corresponding conception of ordinary logic.
These two respects are, first, that the logic of relations considers
sets of individuals in place of individuals, and secondly, that its
conceptions are much fuller and more definite than those of ordinary
logic. Somebody will remark that such terms as paternity,
loving, donation, which are general relations, have no
resemblance to Time and Space. That is true; although not
