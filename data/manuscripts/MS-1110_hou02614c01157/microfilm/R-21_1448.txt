Most of the philosophical writers of this century have agreed that Feeling, Knowledge, and Will make up the mind. Few of those who make use of this distribution of the faculties pretend that it is scientifically accurate; but it has proved itself convenient. Perhaps it is to emphasize [del]the[/del] [ins]its[/ins] unscientific character, that it is often called an enumeration of the "parts of the soul," a phrase that must always have sounded nonsensical. The ordinary view is that Feeling, Knowledge, and Will are the names of three classes of states of mind, in such a sense that a person is [del]only[/del] [ins]at any one[/ins] [del]one[/del] time in one of these states, and not [ins]in[/ins] more than one.

The division is usually attributed to the great father of German philosophy, Immanuel Kant, who died in the last year of the seventeen [del]hundreds[/del]. There is this much truth in that attribution that Kant, in borrowing the division from his master, Tetens, changed the boundaries of the department of Feeling.
