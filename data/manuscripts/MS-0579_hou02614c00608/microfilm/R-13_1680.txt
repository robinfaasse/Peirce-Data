[u]proposition[/u]
$(a \prec b) \prec a \prec c$

By Rule III from this follows $b \prec a \prec c$. But further from the latter the former follows. For by prop. B, this is true provided
$(b \prec a \prec c) \prec (a \prec b) \prec a \prec c$
or
$a \prec (a \prec b) \prec (a \prec b \prec c) \prec c$.

But, by prop. A, this holds; for if $a$ and $a \prec b$ are true, $b$ is true; and if $a$ and $b$ and $a \prec b \prec c$ are true, $c$ is true.

Next consider the proposition
$[(a \prec b) \prec c] \prec a \prec d$.

By Rule III this follows from $(b \prec c) \prec a \prec d$. But further the latter follows from the former. For this is true provided
$\{[(a \prec b) \prec c] \prec a \prec d\} \prec (b \prec c) \prec a \prec d$
[del]For[/del] This is true; for the principle of contraposition gives
$(a \prec b) \prec (b \prec c) \prec a \prec c$
or
$(b \prec c) \prec a \prec (a \prec b) \prec c$
Thus if $a$ and $(b \prec c)$ are true $(a \prec b) \prec c$ is true and if further $[a \prec b) \prec c] \prec a \prec d$ is true, $d$ is true.

Now, every case of repeated antecedent [ins]of the kind defined by the rule[/ins] is evidently reducible to one of these cases.
