It is much the same as Secondness. But it must not be supposed that
that which I find to admire in Scotus lies upon the surface. Anything more
terribly metaphysical cannot be imagined. He is always discussing some
question which means nothing in this world. And his arguments appear to
be exclusively little texts of Aristotle, or of some other authority, wrested
completely from the meaning they originally bore. It is only by the most deter-
mined study that one can discover any sense in it; and even then there is
but a grain of wheat to a bushel of chaff.

It is somewhat refreshing to turn to Ockham who holds the whole of
Scotism to be nothing. He explains everything by means of words. In large
measure it is quite just. But he carries it too far. He does not deny that
things really are alike, but he denies that there is any real respect in which
they are alike. He admits they really have qualities, but he denies that
those qualities are general. He does not perceive that to say that the statement
that two things have greenness in common is a mere phrase expressive of
the fact that they both excite a green sensation is to assert and not to deny
the reality of the general quality [u]green[/u].
