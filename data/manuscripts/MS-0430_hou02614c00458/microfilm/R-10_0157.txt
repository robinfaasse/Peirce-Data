$$\begin{array}{ccc}
x = y & x = \bar{y} & x = \bar{y} \\
x = V & x = V & x = F \\
\therefore y = V & \therefore \bar{y} = F & \therefore y = V
\end{array}$$

Really the same as the last two, though they appear different owing to the imperfection of the notation use, are the following:

$$\begin{array}{cc}
x = \bar{y} & x = \bar{y} \\
y = F & y = V \\
\therefore x = V & \therefore x = F.
\end{array}$$

The mathematician always extends and generalizes his notation. Why not write $x = \bar{V}$ and $x = \bar{F}$, [del]to which yield[/del] [ins]to which correspond[/ins] the [del]following[/del] [ins]inferential[/ins] forms?

$$\begin{array}{cc}
x = \bar{F} & x = \bar{V} \\
F = F & V = V \\
\therefore x = V & \therefore x = F.
\end{array}$$

The premises $F=F$ and $V=V$ are no new postulates. For a postulate expresses in part the hypothesis of the system. But these are mere empty formulae arising from the superfluities of the notation. For $x = \bar{F}$ is but a superfluous form for expressing $x = V$, and $x = \bar{V}$ of expressing $x = F$.
