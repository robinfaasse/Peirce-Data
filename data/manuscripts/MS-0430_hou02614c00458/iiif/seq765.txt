We come now to the vital definitions of the algebra, as follows:

We say that an expression is [u]admissible[/u], if, and only if, it applies [del]to so[/del] a definite sense to a certain object, definite and individual, V.

The [del]definition of[/del] [ins] [fig]figure[/fig] [/ins] is contained in two clauses, as follows:

If anything, q, is admissible, then p [fig]figure[/fig] q is admissible, whatever p may [del]be[/del] signify.

If p [fig]figure[/fig] q is admissible, then if p is admissible, so is q.

The principal theorem of the algebra is that a consequence is admissible, whatever the significations of its letters, if, and only if, in every one of its ultimate consequentials, some evenly antecedental element and some oddly antecedental element are the same letter.
