[u]An Attempted List of Human Motives.[/u]
By C. S. Peirce

In the [u]Popular Science Monthly[/u] for [date]January 1901[/date] (pp. 298 et seq.) I [del]gave[/del] [ins]enumerated[/ins] a number of ethical classes of motives. Of course, such a classification may be made more minute, by subdivisions, or broader by welding several classes. In my enumeration, I endeavored to approximate to something like uniformity in my degree of specification. On looking over what I said, it appears to be more nearly systematic than I was aware of; and it appears to me that it will be useful to restate the matter. For if others are interested in it, we may, between us, succeed in drawing up a complete list of classes of motives which, if it were systematic and rational, would, it seems to me, be of considerable utility in philosophy.

In this restatement, I shall use a notation showing what the different elements of the [del]classes of[/del] [ins]general[/ins] motives are.

[u]A[/u]. A man may act in a quasi-hypnotic response to an instant command.

[u]B[/u]. A man may act from obedience, but not to a concrete command. This I indicate by the letter [u]B[/u]. If he is to do this without any reference to a general rule, it can only be by a sort of personification of other people in general, which I will indicate by the letter [u]z[/u], [del]placed after the B.[/del]
