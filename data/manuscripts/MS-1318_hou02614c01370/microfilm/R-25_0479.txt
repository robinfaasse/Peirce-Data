"All christendom," says the contemporary chronicler,
"experienced an emotion, and seemed to be suddenly
roused from sleep."

Unfortunately, Rienzi did not know what to do next.
His success had been so sudden, that he was quite unprepared
for the situation it created. Besides, it was found that not-
withstanding the great revival of elevated sentiments,
misery had not altogether disappeared. Nay, all this had
cost money, much money, and taxes had to be increased.
Consequently, enthusiasm began to cool. Things were far
better than before, all allowed; still, clearly no millen-
ium was at hand. Besides, Rienzi's almost blasphemous
pomp was not to the accurate taste of the Romans. He had
embroiled himself, perhaps needlessly, with the barons,
and on November 20 had decisively defeated them,
killing seven Colonnas. Then came trouble with one
Count Gianni Pipino, who had been expelled from
Naples for evil doings. On December 15, Rienzi sounded
the call to arms to put down this obstreperous count.
