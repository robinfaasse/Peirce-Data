[u]Rienzi, the Last of the Tribunes.[/u]

The leader, — one had better say the author — of Rome's
extraordinary revolution of the fourteenth century was
named Cola Rienzi. Cola is short for Nicholas, Rienzi
for Lorenzo, or Lawrence; sirname he had not. His father
was a saloon-keeper, his mother a washerwoman. He was
born in Rome, in 1313. How he contrived to pick up
any education we know not. The Popes were then in
Avignon, and Rome was sunk into the deepest barbarism.
Justice and order were unknown. Every crime and inde-
cency was perpetrated with impunity (except for private
vengeance) by day in the open street. For the safety of a man's
goods, he had to depend on the arms of himself and his
friends; if he had too handsome a wife, she might be carried
out of his house in the city by a band of marauders. It was
chiefly the barons who worked all this wickedness, — the same [del]great[/del]
families some of [ins]which[/ins] [del]whom[/del] had been great since Latin times, and [del]most[/del]
[del]of which are great today,[/del] the Colonnas, the Orsinis, the Marinis,
the Ventottos, the Saviellos, etc. Each had his fort and his band
of ruffians, and knew no law.
