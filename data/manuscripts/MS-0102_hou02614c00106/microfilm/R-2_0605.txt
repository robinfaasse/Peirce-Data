[u]Let $P_1$ and $P_2$ be any two points.[/u]
[fig]Line segment with points P1 and P2[/fig]

Now consider this expression
$\lambda P_1 + (1-\lambda) P_2$
where $\lambda$ is a number. $P_1$ and $P_2$ are not numbers,
and therefore the binomial cannot be understood
exactly as in ordinary algebra; but we are to seek
some meaning for it which shall be somewhat
analogous to that of algebra. If $\lambda = 0$, it becomes
$0 P_1 + 1 P_2$.
and this we may take as equal to $P_2$, speaking $0 P_1 = 0$
and $1 P_2 = P_2$. Then if $\lambda = 1$, the expression will
become equal to $P_1$. When $\lambda$ has any other value,
we may [del]suppose[/del] assume that the expression
denotes some other point, and as $\lambda$ varies con-
tinuously we may assume that this point moves
continuously [del]all the values[/del] [ins]as $\lambda$ passes[/ins] through
the whole series of real values. [del]The point will[/del]
[del]describe a line; and the simplest supp[/del] [ins]Our assumption[/ins] is
is that this line is straight. [del]That we will assume;[/del]
but at present we make no [ins]further[/ins] assumption as to the
position of the point on the line when $\lambda$ has
values other than $0$ and $1$. We may write
$\lambda P_1 + (1-\lambda) P_2 = P_3$.
Transposing $P_3$ to the first side of the equation, and
multiplying [del]by any number,[/del] [ins]both sides[/ins] we [del]have an ex-[/del]
[del]press[/del] equations of the form
$a_1 P_1 + a_2 P_2 + a_3 P_3 = 0$
where
$a_1 + a_2 + a_3 = 0$
[del]and[/del] Such an equation will signify that $P_1, P_2, P_3$
are in a straight line; for it is equivalent to
$P_3 = \frac{a_1}{a_1+a_2} P_1 + \frac{a_2}{a_1+a_2} P_2$.
