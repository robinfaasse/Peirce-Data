[u]On the Ways of Thinking of Mathematicians.[/u]

If you [del]ask[/del] were to ask me how many different objects could be distinguished by the answers, yes or no, to twenty questions, I should reply that their possible multitude would be two to the twentieth power. Thereupon, you might very likely ask "How much is $2^{20}$?" And if I were to reply, $1\,048\,576$ you might say, 'Now I know; before you only replied by [del]asking[/del] telling me that it was the solution of a problem in arithmetic.' Yet how can you be said to know the [del]multitude[/del] [ins]number[/ins] any better when you are told that it is $1\,048\,576$ than when you are told that it is $2^{20}$? Why should $10^6 + 4\cdot 10^4 + 8\cdot 10^3 + 5\cdot 10^2 + 7\cdot 10 + 6$ express the essence of the number rather than $2^{20}$? One expression is in the decimal system of numeration, the other in the decundal system.
