"It must not be supposed," said C. G. J. Jacobi, one of the
great simplifiers, "that it is to a gift of nature that I owe
such mathematical power as I possess. No, it has come
by hard work, hard work. Not mere industry, but
brain-splitting thinking, — hard work, hard work, that
has often endangered my health." Such reflections
enable us to perceive that [del]if[/del] [ins]if[/ins] modern mathematics is great,
so [del]are[/del] [ins]were[/ins] the men who made it great.

In short, mathematics has sought the kingdom of God
and divine perfection, and utility has been added unto
it. With the philosophical sciences, it has been too much
the reverse. Their hot pursuit of practical ends, — logic
after some philosopher's stone of thought, metaphysics
after a patent self-acting religion, — has been time wasted.
